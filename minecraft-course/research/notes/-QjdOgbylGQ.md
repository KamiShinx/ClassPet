# Types of Character Arcs: Lecture #6.5 web-exclusive Q&A (Brandon Sanderson, 31.7 min)

**What it is:** A web-only companion video (not filmed in the classroom) where Sanderson answers 10 pre-selected viewer comment-section questions following the two character lectures in this batch — no new framework introduced, all extension/troubleshooting of the proactive/relatable/capable and motivation/personality/values material from the other two videos.
**Substance:** mixed: several genuinely useful troubleshooting answers (risky-decision likeability, main character being less interesting than side characters, sequel arc pacing) delivered densely since it's Q&A with no classroom tangents, but it's entirely reactive to specific novel-writing problems (viewpoint scenes, series continuity, character-name repetition) that assume a full novel manuscript already exists.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:04] Apprentice arc (recap from lecture #6/#5): defined here as the interesting part being where the apprentice diverges from the master — what they can do that the master can't, and vice versa; useful for differentiating two characters who share a skillset.
- [0:02:07] Classic "flaw" arc: establish early how a trait (e.g. pride) is *both* an asset and a cost in the character's life before the story tests whether they'll drop it at the critical moment; warns against a mechanical "fail three times then succeed" pattern — the *why* behind the final choice is what matters, not the repetition count.
- [0:03:42] "Ignorance to knowledge" arc: a character learns about themselves while solving an external mystery — cites a (spoiler-flagged) late Poirot novel about learning that some truths can be known but never proven.
- [0:05:54] Sequel/series arc-pacing: decide upfront whether a character is "iconic" (external arcs only, little book-to-book change, readable out of order) or a genuinely arcing character; for arcing characters, avoid having them re-learn the same lesson twice — give them a small, real victory in book 1 with a bigger unresolved problem still looming (Star Wars trilogy structure as the model).
- [0:09:40] Explicit caution: don't make a character backslide on the same flaw repeatedly across a series — readers tire of it (Spider-Man 2 held up as the one example that pulled it off, framed as an exception not a rule).
- [0:10:10] Handling a competent character's risky mistake without readers calling them "an idiot": foreshadow the specific weakness that causes the mistake beforehand ("hang a lantern on it"), keep the character competent in other visible areas so one failure doesn't overwhelm likeability, and have other characters react proportionally (blame, then someone reframing it as an understandable judgment call).
- [0:12:50] Selfish-motivated mistakes are much harder for an audience to forgive than selfless ones, even though both are "the same size" mistake — cites a specific critique of a Shadow and Bone TV adaptation choice.
- [0:14:23] General principle: you can make risky/unlikeable choices in your story on purpose, but do it deliberately and own the consequence in-story, not accidentally.
- [0:14:57] Viewpoint-selection rule of thumb for a given scene: default to the character taking the action / with the most emotional stake / going through the most pain; deviating from that is rare and should be intentional.
- [0:15:34] Setting-as-character: to write a setting (Arrakis-style) as if it were a character, give it quirks, advantages *and* disadvantages, and let it actively cause conflict/complications for the characters living in it, not just passive backdrop.
- [0:17:45] Sanderson's own dialogue-writing process: enters a scene with a short bullet-point "goal list" (what needs to happen, what each character wants from the exchange), then drafts loosely/improv, and defers small fixes to a revision pass rather than perfecting live.
- [0:19:21] Iconic (non-arcing) characters work especially well as villains (no arc needed, e.g. Joker in The Dark Knight — contrasted explicitly against Batman, who does change) or as mentors (Gandalf: wise, largely unchanging, there to give the protagonist what they need) or as a large supporting ensemble cast (Mission Impossible team) whose job is continuity/comic relief/emotional support rather than arcs.
- [0:23:44] Troubleshooting "my side characters are more interesting than my main character": usually caused by giving side characters distinctive quirks/flaws and forgetting to do the same for the lead — cites his own early draft problem (Wayne overshadowing Wax in the Wax and Wayne novels) and the fix (giving the lead a concrete flaw, e.g. "too analytical, forgets the people involved," and a real arc around it).
- [0:26:38] Named this the "Harry Potter problem" — a viewpoint character can end up flatter than the friends around him if he isn't given equally specific flaws/quirks.
- [0:26:38 area] Repeating a character's name too often: not worth stressing about — pronoun clarity matters more than name-repetition anxiety; only address it if getting explicit reader complaints.
- [0:28:19] On what makes Robin Hobb's characters resonate: she includes unusually granular daily-life detail most action-focused writers skip, which builds bonding depth before hardship hits, making bad character choices land as "our friend's mistake" rather than "movie character being dumb."

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Foreshadow the flaw" check for boss fights**: before a boss's big mistake/weak-point moment (the thing that lets the player win), have the kid write one earlier "tell" that hints at it, so it reads as a fair, telegraphed weakness rather than a random nerf — from [0:10:10]. Very concrete for mod bosses: e.g. if a boss's fire-vulnerability is the win condition, add one small early animation/behaviour cue.
- **"What's this mob's one flaw, one quirk" pass on the PLAYER'S OWN kit**: applied to the item/ability system the player builds (not just mobs) — since Sanderson's "main character less interesting than side characters" problem [0:23:44] maps onto a common game-design trap (the player's custom tools/abilities feel blander than the custom mobs), this becomes a short checklist: does the player's new item/ability have as clear a strength+limitation pair as the mobs the kid designed?

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Iconic vs. arcing split, applied to **mob roster planning**: most mobs are "iconic" (a zombie behaves the same forever); only a mod's signature boss(es) might get an "arc" in the loose sense of changing behaviour/phases as a fight or playthrough progresses. This is the same idea as in lecture #5/#6's notes but reinforced here with a clearer three-role list (villain / mentor / ensemble) that maps to boss / an NPC that gives the player their starting gear or lore / a roster of common mobs.
- The "hang a lantern on it" foreshadowing principle [0:10:10] survives well as boss-design or trap-design advice — a fair difficulty spike needs an earlier readable cue, which is standard game-design practice anyway (telegraphing), so this is a place where novel-writing craft and game design genuinely agree.

### Doesn't transfer
- Nearly everything else here assumes a multi-book prose series already exists: sequel pacing across books [0:05:54], series backsliding [0:09:40], viewpoint-selection between multiple first-person narrators [0:14:57], dialogue-scene bullet-outlining process [0:17:45], and the Robin Hobb "daily life realism" discussion [0:28:19] are all novel-manuscript craft with no mod-design analogue at this class's scale.
- Setting-as-character [0:15:34] is an interesting idea in the abstract (a biome/dimension having "quirks, advantages and disadvantages that cause conflict") but is really a worldbuilding topic, not a character one — better filed under whichever worldbuilding lecture batch covers dimensions/biomes, not duplicated here.
- Character-name-repetition anxiety [0:26:38 area] is a prose-mechanics concern with zero application to a project where kids barely write prose at all.

## Honest caveats
- This video is explicitly billed as answering leftover questions from the other two character lectures — it introduces no new load-bearing framework, so treat its ideas as clarifications/edge-case patches on lecture #5/#6, not standalone content.
- Several answers are framed by Sanderson himself as "rules of thumb" with an admitted ~10% exception rate (e.g. [0:14:57]) — don't present these to kids as firm rules.
- The Shadow and Bone / Spider-Man 2 / Robin Hobb examples are adult, R-rated-adjacent or long-form franchise references our 11-13-year-old audience likely hasn't seen or read; any classroom use of this material should swap in age-appropriate examples (Minecraft or well-known kids'-media equivalents) rather than reusing Sanderson's references directly.
- My own extrapolation: the "player's own kit needs the same quirk/flaw treatment as the mobs" activity is not something Sanderson discusses at all (he's talking about a novel's protagonist vs. side cast) — it's our inference from the underlying "don't forget to give your main character texture too" principle, applied to game-design.

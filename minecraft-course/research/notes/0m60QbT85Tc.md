# How to find amazing game ideas (Game Maker's Toolkit, 27.1 min)

**What it is:** A structured tour of where real indie game ideas came from (existing games, genres, mechanics,
experiences), plus 10 rapid-fire tips and a checklist for judging whether an idea is worth pursuing.
**Substance:** substantive. Densely packed with sourced examples (dev quotes, named games) throughout; the only
padding is a couple of quote-cards repeated across shots. No sponsor read.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:08] Four ways to find a game idea: modify an existing game, bend a genre, invent a new mechanic/system, or
  start from an experience/fantasy and pick mechanics to match.
- [0:02:08] Take an existing game and shift its perspective, theme, or medium (Satisfactory = Factorio in
  first-person; Subnautica = "Minecraft underwater"; Among Us = real-world Werewolf digitised).
- [0:04:54] Find a genre's flaw and fix it (Crypt of the NecroDancer fixed roguelike RNG-unfairness with rhythm).
- [0:06:00] Combine two genres to solve both of their problems at once (Spelunky = platformer + roguelike).
- [0:06:37] Take a mechanic AWAY from a genre instead of adding one (Captain Toad removes jumping).
- [0:07:12] Keep a genre's mechanics but swap the metaphor/skin (FPS crosshair -> camera = Pokemon Snap; -> pressure
  washer = PowerWash Simulator).
- [0:08:18] Mine real-world jobs/activities for mechanics (Papers, Please came from passport-control work).
- [0:09:52] Isolate ONE mechanic from another game and build a whole game around it (Braid from Prince of Persia's
  rewind; Shovel Knight from Zelda 2's downthrust attack).
- [0:10:54] "Bottom-up" dev: start with the verb/mechanic, add story and theme afterward (Splatoon started as
  ink-hiding blocks; character design came later).
- [0:11:26] "Top-down" dev: start from a fantasy/vibe, then pick mechanics to match (FTL = "feel like a starship
  commander").
- [0:13:00] Turn a vague idea into a playable loop via goal / obstacle / fail state / player actions (worked
  example: Crazy Taxi). (generic structure, but a clean one-pager.)
- [0:15:13] Rapid tips include: mentally atomise games into a "periodic table" of mechanics to remix (generic
  framing); always challenge genre conventions; play outside your usual genre; impose a self-limitation; use game
  jam themes as constraints; keep the first idea small.
- [0:18:31] Claim (unsourced in-video): brainstorming sessions with a whiteboard are bad for idea generation; ideas
  come when the mind wanders. Stated as fact but not cited — treat as opinion.
- [0:19:35] Before committing: can YOU specifically make it, given your time/skill/budget? Rule of thumb quoted:
  "if the prototype takes 1-2 days, the game takes 1-2 years."
- [0:21:12] A "hook" = one sentence that makes people want to try or discuss the game; test it with a fake headline.
- [0:22:17] Don't be too novel — an idea so unfamiliar that buyers don't trust it can flop even with good reviews
  (Arco cited as an example).
- [0:23:51] An "anchor" = the familiar part that makes a new idea feel safe; formula: simple + one unexpected twist.
- [0:26:33] Final gut check: you can't know if an idea is fun until you prototype it — "our brains are terrible
  video game simulators."

## What the frames add
Mostly a gameplay montage (Papers Please, Spelunky, Hotline Miami, Stardew Valley, etc.) matching narration
exactly, cut with talking-head shots. A few slide cards give reusable diagrams: a "Bottom Up" triangle
(mechanics/systems at the base, story/theme/experience at the top, [0:11:15]), a red goal/obstacle/actions/
fail-state 4-box diagram ([0:14:00]), and a literal grid of two-letter mechanic abbreviations styled as a periodic
table ([0:15:15]) illustrating the "atomise mechanics" tip. Worth redrawing as a worksheet.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Weekly idea-generator card**: each week, draw one prompt card (remix an existing MC mechanic in a new
  context; swap a verb's target; take a rule away and ask what fills the gap; mine a personal hobby for a mob
  theme) and end with a one-sentence mechanic pitch. 15-20 min. Directly answers Ben's ask for a repeatable
  ideation method.
- **Hook + Anchor one-pager**: write the idea as [Anchor: an existing MC system] + [Hook: the one twist], then a
  one-sentence "would a classmate want to try this" test. 15 min.
- **Goal/Obstacle/Actions/Fail-state worksheet**: one page per mechanic idea before it goes to Gemini as a spec.
  20 min.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
"Bottom-up" fits NeoForge modding well, since kids already have an existing "genre" (vanilla Minecraft) to bend
rather than invent from nothing. Swap-the-metaphor and take-something-away are both cheap, buildable scope
constraints for a 65-min week. The goal/obstacle/actions loop maps directly onto a single mob or item ability.

### Doesn't transfer
Steam-marketability framing (headline tests for strangers, standing out in a market) — this is a school project,
not a commercial launch. The 1-2 day/1-2 year scope heuristic doesn't map to a solo 20-week kid project. The
anti-brainstorming claim is in tension with running a scheduled weekly ideation session at all (see caveats).

## Honest caveats
The "10 rapid tips" section restates well-known creativity advice (challenge conventions, play widely, impose
limits) dressed in good examples — call it useful packaging, not new theory. The anti-brainstorm claim
("the science says...") is asserted with no citation in the video; my own extrapolation is that the fix is to
make the weekly session a constraint/prompt generator (which several of the video's own tips support: jam
themes, limitations) rather than an open "think of an idea" session, since open brainstorming is what the video
itself warns against.

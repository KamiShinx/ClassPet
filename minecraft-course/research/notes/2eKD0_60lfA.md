# Videogame Worldbuilding vs Normal Worldbuilding (Hello Future Me, 31.8 min)

**What it is:** a working game developer (credits his own commercial game, Oathsworn Into the Deepwood) contrasts book/film worldbuilding with game worldbuilding, arguing games worldbuild through space, mechanics and player psychology rather than prose.
**Substance:** substantive: cites two real industry/academic sources on-screen (Bartle's "Designing Virtual Worlds," Steve Dee's "Worldbuilding for Game Designers"), uses his own shipped game as a worked example, and stays focused on the player-vs-reader question this batch is meant to investigate — though it has heavy Nebula/his-own-books cross-promotion at both ends.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:00] Skyrim's city Snowhawk existed on the original 1994 Arena map but is absent from Skyrim; lore blames its destruction, but the real reason is almost certainly level design — removing it protects a design effect described below.
- [0:01:05] Distinct "organizational substructures" per region (language, religion, currency, architecture) make a game world feel bigger than it is — quoted on-screen from Bartle's Designing Virtual Worlds.
- [0:01:37] The "horizon" trick: Skyrim's major cities are deliberately blocked from seeing each other by placed mountains/hills (shown on a map with red sightline circles) — this manufactures a feeling of distance and travel without real distance. Elden Ring uses the same trick with path geography.
- [0:04:17] Key distinction from books: games are physically walkable ("every square foot is felt"), so authors can't just narrate "two years passed" — scale and time must be faked through spatial/design tricks, because a player's body moves through it.
- [0:05:04] Cyberpunk's Night City feels huge because its confusing, labyrinthine urban geography and background NPCs imply an off-screen world the player will never fully see — "the confusion is the point."
- [0:05:57] SimCity anecdote: developers wanted strict city-simulation realism, then realized real cities are mostly boring parking lots and airports — games often simulate an idealized, more-fun *version* of reality rather than literal realism.
- [0:07:34] Blocky, simple graphics (explicitly names Minecraft, also Undertale) can be *more* immersive than photorealism because they leave space for the player's imagination to fill in detail.
- [0:08:06] No Man's Sky's launch as a cautionary case: infinite procedural scale with no real cultural/organizational variation felt small and same-y — reinforces the "substructures create scale" point above, not raw size.
- [0:10:19] Quotes Steve Dee: worldbuilding is "teaching and selling," and "game design is more psychology than maths... more psychology than geography" — games worldbuild by directing player *attention* and choices, not just describing a setting.
- [0:11:20] Landmark placement (a castle/tree/tower visible on the horizon of an open starting area) is a deliberate design tool to pull players toward specific content; shown in his own game Oathsworn, where a coliseum sits dead-center of a city map to draw exploration.
- [0:12:55] Warhammer 40k's 2003 "Eye of Terror" campaign: 40,000+ real tabletop players' logged game outcomes were used by the studio to decide a canonical story event (the Fall of Cadia) — an extreme example of a game inviting players to literally become part of its history, something books/film structurally cannot do.
- [0:14:33] Because players choose how much to engage, games can carry far more total lore text than film/books ever could (Cyberpunk's script tops 1 million words) — but designers must be ruthless about what's mandatory, since roughly half of players skip all optional narrative content by his own experience.
- [0:15:38] Numbers vs. no numbers is itself a worldbuilding choice: showing "25 damage" signals a masterable, gamified world; withholding numbers (Hollow Knight, early Morrowind) signals a mysterious, dangerous one.
- [0:16:49] Skill trees as worldview signals: heavy combat trees signal a violent world; Disco Elysium's skill tree (facets of the protagonist's psyche, not weapon types) signals an introspective, unresolvable world.
- [0:19:58] Players hate being lied to far more than book/film audiences tolerate unreliable narrators, because players are making real strategic decisions on the information given — he personally scrapped an unreliable-narrator idea in his own game for this reason.
- [0:21:41] Fast-travel/navigation design is worldbuilding: No Man's Sky's instant menu-teleport made the universe feel small; Cyberpunk's fun driving made players ignore fast travel entirely; Morrowind's marker-less, verbal-directions-only navigation forced players to learn the world as a physical place (though he admits he personally gave up on Morrowind's main quest at age 12 because of it).
- [0:24:57] In-world maps are artifacts, not just nav tools — a map's *style* (hand-drawn/disproportionate for a nostalgic game, black-and-white survey-style for Red Dead 2's 1899 setting) communicates culture and period on its own; quotes Steve Dee: "a good map should reveal how the world works and how characters think."

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Blocked-horizon sketch**: before placing a second structure/build in the shared world, kid sketches on paper (10 min) where hills/trees should sit to hide it from their first build, then places it in Minecraft and checks whether it actually works.
- **One landmark rule**: each kid places exactly one visible feature on their area's skyline that pulls a visiting player toward their best content — concrete, buildable in ~20 min including in-game placement.
- **Numbers-or-mystery decision**: before writing a mob/item tooltip, kid explicitly chooses and justifies in one line whether it shows exact stats (gamey/masterable) or stays vague (dangerous/mysterious) — a real design decision Gemini can implement as a data choice.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Very well — sightline blocking, landmark placement and map-style choices are spatial/environmental moves the kids execute directly by building, with no drawing or writing skill required.

### Doesn't transfer
The Warhammer 40k "player actions become canon" story and Cyberpunk's million-word script scale are interesting but need either a live community of thousands or a writing volume far beyond 8 kids in a 20-week course — keep as inspiration only, never an activity target.

## Honest caveats
The presenter sells his own worldbuilding books and plugs Nebula throughout (soft sponsorship at both ends) — treat his opinions with a bit more skepticism than the two external, cited sources (Bartle, Dee), which are legitimate industry/academic references. His Morrowind praise is one-sided: he holds it up as superior design ("I will die on this hill") while admitting in the same breath that as a 12-year-old he gave up the main quest because of exactly that design — which is effectively a confession the technique fails for an 11-13 audience like this class. For our kids, favor clarity/markers over Morrowind-style mystery-navigation.

# Sanderson's Laws of Magic: Lecture #7 (Brandon Sanderson, 73.5 min)

**What it is:** BYU lecture (first of the "worldbuilding" unit), in-person classroom with heavy Q&A, laying out Sanderson's three laws of magic-system design — the most famous and most re-cited piece of his teaching. Covers Law 1 (problem-solving is proportional to reader understanding) in depth, then Law 2 (limitations > powers) in depth, and only sketches Law 3 (don't go wide, go deep) in the last ~5 minutes before time runs out.
**Substance:** substantive: this is the tightest, most concept-dense lecture in the batch — every idea ties back to one of three named, memorable rules, each illustrated with multiple worked examples (LOTR, Mistborn, Harry Potter, Dune, Superman, Wizard of Oz, Star Trek) and stress-tested against real counter-examples in Q&A. Some runtime is Sanderson's own publishing-career anecdotes (Mistborn's near-flop, cover redesigns) which are entertaining but not craft content.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:09:52] Hard vs soft magic defined precisely: hard = reader can learn the rules and anticipate outcomes, story revolves around characters mastering/exploiting those rules; soft = reader can't know all the rules, magic produces wonder/horror precisely because it's unpredictable. Presented as a spectrum, not a binary (Harry Potter = hard within one book, soft across the series).
- [0:11:29] Key distinction: **internal logic** (is the system consistent with itself?) is what makes something "hard," not **external logic** (does it sound sciencey/plausible by real-world standards). Superman's power source ("the sun, who cares why") is just as "hard" as X-Men's pseudo-genetic explanation, because both are consistent and knowable — the sciencey flavor is decoration, not the mechanism.
- [0:14:08] Worked comparison: the One Ring (hard — invisibility, corrupts, Sauron can sense the wearer) vs. Gandalf (soft — powerful but unquantified) within the same book, to show both registers can coexist and serve different narrative purposes (wonder vs. mastery).
- [0:18:30] **Sanderson's First Law** (the headline rule): *"Your ability to solve problems in a satisfying way with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands said magic."* If you want magic to resolve the climax, the reader must already understand the relevant rule; if magic is meant to stay mysterious, don't let it resolve the ending — let character choices resolve it instead.
- [0:21:14] A "friend shows up out of nowhere and saves the day" scene works fine as an *introduction* device early in a story (proves the character's competence for later use) but reads as a cheap deus ex machina if used as the *climax* resolution — same mechanic, opposite effect depending on placement.
- [0:22:22] Extended worked example: LOTR's "Look for me at dawn on the fifth day" (Gandalf/Helm's Deep) as a textbook Law 1 execution — a soft-magic character given a hard, promised, foreshadowed condition, so the payoff feels earned even though we never learn how Gandalf's power actually works. Contrasted with the same film's army-of-ghosts / Aragorn rescue, which lacks that setup and reads as a weaker, less-earned resolution.
- [0:28:19] Practical seeding advice: put in *more* clues about your rules than you think necessary and scale back only if beta-test readers "get it" too easily — his stated experience is that authors under-seed far more often than they over-seed.
- [0:28:50] To avoid info-dumping magic system rules: reveal them through action/demonstration scenes with other things happening simultaneously (character stakes, relationships), not lecture-style exposition; cites his own choice to delay showing Mistborn's full magic system until a hands-on training scene.
- [0:34:51] Self-critical case study: Sanderson admits he broke his own First Law at the end of Mistborn 1 (character discovers an unforeshadowed power to solve the climax) — done under editorial pressure for a stronger ending, worked emotionally in the moment, but he flags it afterward as a structural weakness and the actual origin of this "law."
- [0:38:37] Twilight counter-example: a resolution can involve the protagonist unconscious during the "fight" because the book's real emotional throughline (the romance) is resolved elsewhere — Law 1 applies to whatever the *actual* promise/payoff axis of the story is, not necessarily the surface-level action plot.
- [0:39:43] "Hanging a lantern" on a mystery you plan to keep unexplained (having a character explicitly say "we don't know why, don't worry about it") signals to the reader that the gap is intentional, not an oversight.
- [0:40:20] "Deus Ex Wrench" (coined by a writer friend, Bryce Moore): readers tolerate some authorial interference that makes things *harder* for characters, but only sparingly — complications should trace back to a character's choices/existing flaws wherever possible, not to arbitrary new obstacles the author invents on the spot.
- [0:49:39] **Sanderson's Second Law**: *"Flaws/limitations are more interesting than powers."* Origin story: he built Mistborn's Allomancy to feel original, only to have a fan point out it's essentially Magneto's powers — what actually made it distinct and fun was the constraint set (push only directly away, pull only directly toward, mass/weight tradeoffs), which forces tactical, strategic use, not the power itself.
- [0:56:36] Extends explicitly to character too (echoing lecture #5): Superman's best stories are about kryptonite, someone physically stronger than him, or a problem powers can't fix (Lois Lane) — strong stories hit characters (and magic systems) at their weak points, not their strong ones.
- [0:59:12] Defines **cost** (price paid to use the power — economic, physical, intellectual, mystical), **flaw** (a limitation the reader is meant to expect will eventually be fixed/overcome), **limitation** (a permanent boundary of the system not meant to be "solved," just worked within/around), and **hindrance** (a line the character *could* cross but the story leaves ambiguous whether they should) as four distinct, useful categories.
- [1:00:20] Caution on numeric resource costs (mana/charges/"bullets left"): readers instinctively track a countdown, so if you use one, either let the character state their remaining reserve narratively ("I'm running low") or be deliberate about exact numbers — don't let resource-tracking feel like an unacknowledged authorial contrivance.
- [1:04:36] A flaw can be *reframed* mid-story into a limitation/hindrance you decide to keep rather than fix — "we could solve this, but something worse would happen, so we choose not to" is a legitimate and common pivot.
- [1:05:08] Matching a magic system's costs/limitations to a character's personal background/arc is where theme naturally emerges (Paul Atreides' water-scarce world vs. his water-rich origin, cited as the model).
- [1:06:19] Explicit warning against stacking too many flaws/limitations into one system — elegant, few rules beat many "interesting but finicky" ones; flagged as the seed of the Third Law.
- [1:08:30] Brief aside: the *best* stories often sit at the intersection of a flawed character and a flawed/limited magic system (Name of the Wind's Kvothe chasing a magic he structurally cannot master).
- [1:09:01] **Sanderson's Third Law** (only briefly covered, time ran out): *"Expand what you have before adding something new."* Origin: fan hype pressure to keep adding more magic systems/plotlines to Stormlight; realized restraint (fewer, deeper elements, reusing/rebranching existing systems — e.g. two religions as "cousins" of one root rather than two unrelated new religions) produces stronger books than breadth. Illustrated with a negative example: Daggerfall's 10,000 procedurally-repeated dungeons ("an ocean an inch deep") vs. modern Skyrim's fewer, hand-crafted ones.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Rule card" for every custom item/ability, before any prompt goes to Gemini**: one line stating the rule in plain language the player could learn by playing ("right-click to dash forward 5 blocks; costs 3 hunger; 10-second cooldown"). Directly implements Law 1 [0:18:30]: if the mechanic is going to let the player solve a puzzle or fight, the rule must be learnable and consistent — which is also literally what Gemini needs as an unambiguous spec to implement correctly. ~10 min per item.
- **"Powers vs. limits" split worksheet**: for every new item/ability/boss mechanic, kids fill two columns — what it DOES, and what it COSTS / CANNOT do (cooldown, resource, directional restriction, environment requirement). Directly from Law 2 [0:49:39, 0:59:12]. This is the single most concrete, most directly game-mechanical idea in the whole four-video batch — "limitation" in Sanderson's sense IS a game balance constraint, and forcing kids to write the limitation before the power stops the common new-designer trap of over-powered, unlimited items.
- **Four-box mechanic sheet**: literally use Sanderson's four categories as four boxes on a worksheet — Cost (what you pay each use), Flaw (a weakness meant to be fixed by a later upgrade/enchant), Limitation (a permanent rule, e.g. "only works underwater"), Hindrance (a tempting shortcut the player COULD exploit but the mod doesn't explicitly forbid — a genuinely fun exploit-design idea for a kid coder). From [0:59:12]-[1:02:26].
- **"Have I already got this?" check before adding a new mechanic**: before a kid asks Gemini to build a brand-new item/mob type, make them answer "could this instead be a new variant of something I already built?" (a new ore that works like an existing one but with a twist, rather than an unrelated new system). Directly from Law 3 [1:09:01] and the Daggerfall/Skyrim contrast [1:11:49] — a strong, concrete check against 20-week scope creep, since 8 kids each inventing unlimited unrelated systems is exactly the "ocean an inch deep" failure mode Sanderson describes.
- **"Foreshadow before you resolve" boss checklist**: any custom boss's win condition (weak point, phase trigger) must be shown to the player once, safely, before the final fight uses it — from the Helm's Deep "fifth day" case study [0:22:22]. Concretely: a tutorial-room or early encounter that demonstrates the mechanic the final boss fight will require.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- This lecture is the single strongest transfer point in the entire batch, exactly as flagged in the assignment brief: a magic system's rules, costs and limitations map almost one-to-one onto game mechanics (cooldowns, resource costs, directional/positional constraints, environmental requirements) — the vocabulary changes (mana → hunger/durability/cooldown) but the design logic is identical, and it happens to be the kind of unambiguous, rule-based spec Gemini can actually implement correctly, unlike vaguer "vibes" prompts.
- Cost/flaw/limitation/hindrance as four named categories is directly reusable as a mod-mechanic design worksheet, unmodified, because these are genuinely mechanic categories already (not narrative-only): cost = resource system, flaw = upgrade path, limitation = hard rule/edge case, hindrance = intentional exploit/tradeoff.
- The "expand what you have" law [1:09:01] survives cleanly as scope-management advice for a 20-week, 65-minutes-a-week class where kids will be tempted to invent unlimited unrelated systems — this is arguably as useful for classroom logistics as for design quality.

### Doesn't transfer
- The hard/soft magic spectrum concept itself [0:09:52] is about narrative *information control* (what the reader is allowed to know) — a Minecraft mod's mechanics are inherently "hard" by necessity (the player must be able to learn them to play), so most of the nuanced hard/soft craft discussion (Gandalf-style deliberate mystery, "lantern-hanging" on unexplained lore) applies to lore/flavor text at most, not to actual game mechanics, which should almost always be built hard/legible.
- The extended Q&A on foreshadowing red herrings, reversed cause-and-effect mysteries (Elantris), and "the character shouldn't look stupid" plotting [0:46:58 onward] is novel-mystery-plotting craft with no game-mechanic equivalent.
- Sanderson's own publishing-career detour (Mistborn's near-failure, cover redesigns, sales numbers) [0:53:57–0:56:36] is autobiography, not craft, though it's a good illustration that even his most iconic idea (Allomancy) came from years of iteration, not a first draft — possibly worth one sentence to kids as encouragement, not a worksheet.
- Man-vs-nature/hard-science-fiction framing (Dragonriders of Pern) [0:45:18] and the "Star Trek technobabble" trick for justifying unexplainable-to-the-reader complexity [0:43:36] are prose techniques for withholding technical exposition from a *reader* — a mod has no reader to manage information for; the player learns by playing, so this doesn't map.

## Honest caveats
- Sanderson explicitly frames all three laws as personal rules of thumb born from his own mistakes ("these are not laws everyone needs to follow"), not universal truths — worth keeping that hedge if this gets turned into official class material, i.e. present as "one working framework," not dogma.
- Law 3 gets only ~5 minutes of real coverage because the class ran out of time (explicitly promised more depth "next week," which is not in our batch) — our worksheet above ("have I already got this?") is a reasonable, direct extrapolation of the stated rule, but the lecture itself does not develop it as fully as Laws 1 and 2.
- The examples used throughout (LOTR, Mistborn, Dune, Name of the Wind, Twilight) are all adult-novel references our 11-13-year-old audience mostly won't know firsthand; any class-facing version of this material should swap in Minecraft-native or kid-media examples (e.g. redstone contraptions, existing vanilla enchantment limitations) rather than reusing Sanderson's references directly.
- My own extrapolation, flagged as such: nowhere does Sanderson discuss anything resembling "game balance" or player-facing UI — the mapping from his craft vocabulary (cost/flaw/limitation/hindrance) onto class worksheets is our synthesis, not something stated in the video. It's a strong fit, but it is our inference, not his claim.

# Why Character Design is about more than just Style (Blaze, 24.9 min)

**What it is:** A games-commentary essay using the 2024 hero-shooter flop Concord as a case study, arguing that
in fast-paced multiplayer games character design has a FUNCTIONAL job (telegraphing what a character can do at
a glance), not just an aesthetic one — contrasted against TF2's character design, which the video holds up as
near-perfect at this.

**Substance:** substantive on its central argument, though it's essentially one thesis (functional character
design vs. decorative character design) stretched across 25 minutes with a lot of repeated gameplay-footage
padding (long silent clips of Concord/Monster Hunter/TF2 footage) and a closing 10 minutes of tangential
side-arguments (price tag, diversity discourse, console availability) that aren't about character design at all.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:44] Central claim: "these characters look bad" is a vague, unhelpful critique on its own — the real
  question is WHY a design fails, and for action/multiplayer games that answer is usually mechanical, not just
  aesthetic.
- [0:03:17] Key distinction: in a turn-based RPG, character design carries little functional weight because
  players have unlimited time to read tooltips/menus; in a fast-paced action or competitive game, the player has
  no time to check a menu mid-fight, so the DESIGN itself has to convey capability instantly. (This directly
  reframes "character design" as also being UX/information design.)
- [0:04:57] Two functional jobs a design must do simultaneously: (1) convey what the character is capable of
  (big body = tanky/high health, scrawny = fast/fragile), and (2) be memorable/distinct enough to identify at a
  glance in a crowd or split-second decision.
- [0:05:30] TF2 case study: each of the 9 classes reads its role and combat style purely from silhouette/
  proportions before you know any of their weapons — Heavy (big/barrel-chested = tank), Scout (scrawny, socks
  emphasizing legs = fast/mobile). Held up as the video's gold standard example.
- [0:07:38] Live "guess the ability" exercise on Concord characters: shows a character (Roka), asks viewer to
  guess role from appearance alone, then reveals the real kit — repeatedly the visual gives zero or misleading
  signal (a "stealth-coded" muted outfit turns out to carry a giant rocket launcher).
- [0:09:50] Direct compare/contrast: Concord's flying character (generic space-helmet suit) vs. Overwatch's Pharah
  (gold beak-shaped visor evoking a bird, prominent jetpack wings) — argues Pharah's ability (flight) is legible
  from the design itself via a clear visual metaphor (bird), while Concord's equivalent gives no such cue.
- [0:12:36] Deadlock's fire character (red clothes, orange sunglasses, glowing tattoos, finger-guns instead of a
  weapon) as another positive example — the character reads "fire-based" even before literally being on fire,
  because the ability is baked into color/costume choices, not just an add-on weapon prop.
- [0:15:21] Explicitly states you don't need to copy an existing trope (doesn't have to look exactly like Pharah)
  to convey an ability — offers alternative design directions (helicopter-propeller wings, fighter-pilot helmet,
  giant boots for a slam ability) to show there are multiple valid ways to hit the same functional target.
- [0:17:04] Diagnoses the root failure as a PROCESS/pipeline problem, not an individual artist failure: character
  artists, ability/gameplay designers and writers needed tighter iteration and shared direction; disconnected
  silos produced characters whose visuals and mechanics were "designed in a vacuum" and stitched together late.
- [0:15:56] Secondary aesthetic argument: leaning toward photorealism ages badly and reads as less charming than
  a distinct cartoony/stylized art direction (TF2 from 2007 still looks good; hyperrealistic designs date fast).
- [0:20:54]-[0:22:32] Explicitly rules out competing narratives (diversity of character designs, lack of Xbox
  release) as the cause of the game's failure, using other successful diverse rosters (Overwatch, Deadlock) as
  counter-evidence. (These sections are meta-discourse defense, not design craft.)
- [0:22:32] Names price ($40 vs. free-to-play competitors) as a real contributing factor but argues via the open
  beta's low player counts that bad character design (and by extension weak overall appeal) was the dominant
  cause, not price alone.

## What the frames add
Long stretches of pure Concord/Monster Hunter/TF2 gameplay b-roll add little beyond what's narrated — not worth
detailed cataloguing. Two things the frames genuinely add: (1) the actual character "hero cards" of multiple
Concord characters shown full-body with weapon [0:08:00]-[0:14:24] and [0:17:04]-[0:19:52] — seeing these
confirms the video's complaint directly: the rosters ARE visually flat, muted, and near-indistinguishable
without reading name/ability text overlays, which is hard to fully appreciate from narration alone; (2) the
side-by-side Pharah vs. Concord-equivalent silhouette-adjacent comparison [0:09:50]-[0:10:23] makes the
"bird-shaped visor = flight" argument concrete and copyable as a teaching example (a genuine before/after).
Otherwise: mostly gameplay clips, store-page screenshots, and player-count graphs used to support the "this game
failed" framing, not design content.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Guess the Ability" swap game**: kid designs a mob/item and shows ONLY the appearance (no tooltip, no name)
  to a partner, who has to guess what it does mechanically. If the partner can't guess, or guesses something
  totally different, the design needs another pass. ~10 min per pair, repeatable every session a kid finishes a
  new mob. This is the single most directly reusable activity in the batch — it's literally the video's own
  demonstration format, and it requires no drawing skill, just Blockbench + a texture.
- **Ability-to-Silhouette worksheet**: before opening Blockbench, kid writes their mob's ONE core mechanic (flies,
  explodes, is tanky, is stealthy) and then brainstorms 2-3 concrete visual metaphors for it (wings vs. propellers
  vs. jetpack for "flies") before picking one. Directly modeled on [0:15:21]'s "multiple valid directions for the
  same function" idea. ~10 min.
- **Tank-vs-Scout Silhouette Pair**: as a diagnostic drill, have two kids design a "big slow tank mob" and a
  "small fast scout mob" side by side and check that they're readable as opposites at a glance (from the TF2
  Heavy/Scout comparison, [0:05:30]-[0:07:06]). Useful early-course exercise before kids design their full
  roster.
- **Pipeline discipline note for the teacher**: [0:17:04]'s root-cause diagnosis (visuals and mechanics designed
  by disconnected people, stitched together late) is a warning FOR THE COURSE STRUCTURE itself — a kid's
  Blockbench design and their Gemini-built mechanic need to be planned together from the trait-card stage, not
  bolted together at the end. Worth stating explicitly as a class rule rather than turning into a kid-facing
  worksheet.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- This video's whole thesis maps onto Minecraft mods almost perfectly, arguably BETTER than onto its own case
  study: Minecraft is exactly the "fast-paced, no time to read a tooltip" category the video describes (mobs
  attack in real time, items are used instantly), so "can a player tell what this does by looking at it" is a
  directly load-bearing design question for a kid's mod, not just nice-to-have polish.
  the video's own diagnostic exercise (show design, guess function) is trivially reproducible with a Blockbench
  render, zero extra tooling needed.
- The "convey ability via one clear visual metaphor" idea (wings=flight, glowing=fire) survives especially well
  into 16x16 textures and cube silhouettes, since it argues FOR simplicity/legibility over rendering fidelity —
  which is the only thing 16x16 pixels and blocky geometry can achieve anyway.
- The stylization-ages-better argument reassures kids (and Ben) that NOT going for realism is not a limitation
  to apologize for — Minecraft's whole aesthetic is the "distinct stylized" side of that argument, already
  validated by the medium itself.

### Doesn't transfer
- The extended discourse-defense segment (diversity discourse, Xbox release, price tag economics) is games-
  industry business/culture commentary, entirely irrelevant to 11-13-year-olds designing a personal mod — skip
  outright.
- Player-count graphs, Steam charts, and the whole "why did this AAA game commercially fail" framing has no
  classroom use; it's evidence for the essay's argument, not craft content to teach.
- The RPG vs. action-game distinction ([0:03:17]) is worth knowing conceptually but Minecraft combat is real-time
  by default, so there's no real "which genre are we in" decision for kids to make — the functional-design lesson
  applies almost unconditionally here.

## Honest caveats
- This is a single YouTuber's opinion essay reacting to one game's launch failure, not a vetted design-theory
  source; several claims ("I think... but I have no idea," his own words at points) are speculative, and he
  states upfront he's not a visual artist and can't critique color/anatomy directly — his analysis stays at the
  silhouette/role level by his own admission, which happens to be exactly what transfers to Minecraft, but that's
  a coincidence worth noting rather than treating the video as authoritative on visual craft generally.
- The video attributes Concord's failure heavily to character design while also admitting price and genre
  fatigue were real factors — treat "character design killed this game" as his argued thesis, not an established
  fact.
- No overlap with the other three videos in this batch on shape/color psychology; this is the one video in the
  batch that is actually about GAME DESIGN (function, not just appearance) — closest thing in this batch to the
  "what makes mechanics fun" content Ben separately flagged as missing, though it only addresses the
  legibility-of-mechanics slice of that question, not fun/balance/feel more broadly.

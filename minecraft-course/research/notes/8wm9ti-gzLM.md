# GOOD vs BAD Character Design: Tips and Tricks! (BaM Animation, 20.4 min)

**What it is:** Two professional animation artists build one deliberately bad character design, then walk
through fan-submitted redraws to teach three pillars: clarity of silhouette, clarity of palette, and clarity of
exaggeration, ending on "story" as the real foundation.

**Substance:** substantive for the first ~17 minutes: genuinely structured craft content with live Photoshop
demonstrations (value checks, color balance, silhouette reduction) on real submitted art. The last ~3 minutes
are channel outro/sponsor/patreon comedy bit, pure padding — the video sold as "20 min" is closer to 17 min of
content.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:00] States the thesis up front: good character design = clarity of silhouette + clarity of palette +
  clarity of exaggeration; a design that's clear in all three reads in any art style.
- [0:01:39] Diagnostic test: reduce the character to a pure black silhouette — it must still be recognizable.
  "There isn't a single famous character that doesn't follow this rule."
- [0:02:48] Big, identifiable shapes in the silhouette communicate personality via shape language (stable/
  trustworthy square, friendly/soft circle, dangerous/sharp triangle) — same theory as other videos in this batch,
  independently corroborated.
- [0:03:53] "Shape motif" technique: once you pick a dominant shape (e.g., triangle for a femme fatale villain),
  commit to making as many of the character's shapes as possible variants of that one shape — reduces clutter,
  reinforces read.
- [0:04:25] A unique silhouette detail on the HEAD specifically (a distinct shape/silhouette landmark) helps
  identify a character in a crowd and read facing direction at a glance.
- [0:05:04] Practical checklist before cleanup: hands look bad → improve silhouette; poses feel weak → improve
  silhouette. Silhouette is the first and repeated troubleshooting step, not a one-time check.
- [0:05:28] Palette rule: don't use too many colors; establish a color hierarchy — one dominant color, others
  support without competing (demonstrated: swapping a competing cool color to a warm one to stop it fighting the
  skin tone).
- [0:06:01] Value (brightness) matters independently of hue — two different colors can still read as "the same"
  if their values are too close; demonstrated via a flattened white/color toggle layer in Photoshop.
- [0:06:34] Practical tip: never design a character against pure white — use a mid-tone background so you can
  actually judge whether your lights/darks stand out from an environment.
- [0:07:22] Atmospheric perspective as a compositional clarity tool: lighten background-distance colors to push
  foreground contrast and readability.
- [0:08:32] Color language/color psychology (yellow=joy, green=safety, red=danger/sexy) is real but explicitly
  called "not as clear as shape language" — same color evokes opposite feelings in different contexts (yellow =
  happy sunshine OR sickly jaundice), and geographic/cultural color use varies (climates near the equator use
  more saturated color).
- [0:09:37] Core design philosophy: art connects to the viewer's human instincts; simplifying detail, pushing
  proportions and color lets the audience project themselves onto a character ("the masking effect," credited to
  Scott McCloud) — this is why simplified/iconic faces can read more emotionally than realistic ones.
- [0:10:44] Pose itself is a form of exaggeration and can telegraph traits (masculine/feminine, extroverted/
  introverted, lazy/focused) without any linework changes.
- [0:11:17] Reject "textbook anatomy for everybody" — deliberately vary body types (weightlifter vs. basketball
  player) to reflect a character's function/utility, not a generic default proportion.
- [0:11:56] Live redraw demo: exaggerating two already-decent submitted designs by pushing contrast between them
  (one thin/poised, one big/intimidating) to amplify a comedic power dynamic — also flags "tangents" (edges lining
  up awkwardly, flattening the sense of depth) as a specific error to fix.
- [0:12:28] "Appeal rulers": draw connecting lines between a character's three major body masses and check the
  spacing is dynamic/uneven rather than evenly spaced — evenly-spaced masses read as bland.
- [0:13:04] Practical tip: design characters in a lineup on the SAME canvas, not separate documents, so relative
  proportions stay consistent when compared.
- [0:13:36] "Character trios" exercise: deliberately design three characters maximally different in silhouette,
  palette, shape motif, body type and pose, and check that they could still plausibly exist in the same universe —
  builds a "roster" muscle, not just a single design.
- [0:15:11] Honest answer to "what makes good character design" — after all the rules, ultimately: gather real
  reference AND have an actual story to tell; technique without story doesn't produce lasting designs.
- [0:16:20] Advises against chasing a personal signature style — professional character designers deliberately
  learn to draw in MANY styles so the style can serve each project's story (South Park example: crude style
  matches crude humor; the reverse pairing would break the work).
- [0:16:53] "Simple idea, executed well" is the real trick — references the Beatles' "Blackbird" as a minimal-
  arrangement analogy; simple designs are also kinder to animators/collaborators, who don't have to fight extra
  detail.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Black-Silhouette Test tooltip**: a standing rule for every mob/item: render or trace it in solid black —
  if you can't tell what it is, redesign the shape before texturing. ~5 min check, run every session a kid
  touches Blockbench. Directly from [0:01:39]/[0:05:04].
- **Pick-One-Shape worksheet**: before building in Blockbench, kid circles one dominant shape (circle/square/
  triangle) for their mob and commits every added block/limb to echoing that shape family. ~10 min paper exercise
  using the video's own literal shape chart as a template. From [0:03:53].
- **Dominant Color + One Accent rule**: kid picks ONE dominant texture color and at most one accent color that
  doesn't compete in hue AND checks it in grayscale (screenshot desaturated) to confirm the values actually
  differ — this maps directly onto 16x16 texture painting where color real estate is tiny and must be spent
  carefully. ~10-15 min. From [0:05:28]-[0:06:01].
- **Trio Roster Challenge**: a later-course exercise — design three mobs for the same world that are each
  distinct in silhouette/color/shape but could plausibly coexist (e.g., three factions or three biome variants).
  Good for a kid who's already built one mob and needs a next project. From [0:13:36].
- **Function-over-default-proportions prompt**: instead of "just make a humanoid," ask what the mob DOES (fast?
  tanky? sneaky?) and exaggerate the Blockbench proportions (thin legs, wide torso, tiny head) to match — this
  reframes "make it look cool" into a concrete design question with an answer. From [0:11:17].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Silhouette-first design is arguably the SINGLE most important idea in this whole batch for the Minecraft
  constraint specifically: a mob seen from a distance in a blocky, low-poly, often dim-lit world is read almost
  entirely by outline. This isn't just "generic good advice" here, it's close to the actual physics of the
  medium.
- The value/color-hierarchy lesson survives and arguably becomes MORE important, not less, in 16x16 pixel
  textures: with so few pixels, a kid genuinely cannot afford competing mid-tones the way a full illustration
  can absorb them; "one dominant color, checked in grayscale" is a very buildable rule for texture painting.
- Shape motif (commit to one shape family) maps directly onto cube arrangement in Blockbench — cubes ARE already
  geometric primitives, so "make it mostly boxy vs. mostly spiky" is trivially achievable without drawing skill.
- "Simple executed well, not maximal detail" is probably the most important STANDING DISCIPLINE for this course:
  11-13-year-olds vibecoding for the first time will default to wanting maximal detail/complexity; the video's
  own message (simple is kinder to collaborators/animators) becomes "simple is kinder to Gemini and to your own
  16x16 texture budget."
- Body-type exaggeration for function survives well into Blockbench proportions (stretch/squash cube dimensions
  per limb) and is a good bridge into "game design" thinking (a tanky mob should probably look chunky).

### Doesn't transfer
- The live Photoshop value/color-balance correction workflow itself (adjustment layers, Color Balance dialog)
  is 2D illustration software craft with no Blockbench/texture-tool equivalent worth teaching.
- Atmospheric perspective (lightening distant background colors) is a painting/environment-art composition trick,
  not applicable to a single mob texture or a voxel world's built-in fog/lighting system.
- "Learn to draw in many styles, don't chase a personal style" is professional-illustrator career advice aimed at
  people who'll do client work; not relevant to hobbyist 11-13-year-olds each building one personal mod.
- The masking-effect/Scott McCloud theory of viewer projection is a legitimate concept but too abstract to turn
  into a class activity at this age without significant simplification; better used by the teacher as background
  reasoning than taught directly to kids.

## Honest caveats
- The video's own hosts admit uncertainty at the end ("what really makes good character design — I have no
  idea") after 15 minutes of confident rules — worth preserving that humility rather than presenting the three
  pillars as a complete formula.
- Practically all demonstrations are on ANIME/cartoon character fan art submissions (2D humanoid figures); none
  of it is tested against a game-asset or 3D-model context, so the "silhouette/palette/exaggeration" framework
  is being extrapolated to Minecraft mobs by us, not validated by the video itself.
- Color psychology is explicitly flagged by the video as weaker/less reliable than shape language — worth
  keeping that hedge when teaching color to kids rather than presenting red=danger, green=safety as fixed rules.
- Like the Kirbs video, this is 100% appearance/readability, zero mechanics/gameplay-feel content — reinforces
  that this batch alone will not cover Ben's "game design" concern.

# The one thing you need to finish your game (Developing 11) (Game Maker's Toolkit, 16.5 min)

**What it is:** Mark Brown's own devlog on his puzzle-platformer "Mind Over Magnet" — after 24 months and only ~9 months of actual work, he finally makes a concrete production plan and reports what changed once he had one.
**Substance:** substantive: it's a first-person account of scope planning actually working (level counts, world structure, feedback-driven cuts) with real screenshots of the plan itself, not just advice asserted in the abstract.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:16]-[0:02:46] Named root cause of stalled progress: "aimlessly noodling" — working without a concrete task list, just vibes and hope it "coalesces."
- [0:02:46]-[0:03:16] Explicit sequencing rule: avoid planning during early prototyping (protect creative freedom to experiment), but once the prototype has found what's fun, you MUST make a plan to reach a finished product — planning too early kills experimentation, planning too late (his mistake) wastes years.
- [0:03:16] A plan's core function stated directly: it tells you what IS in the game and, "crucially by extension," what is NOT in the game — scope-by-exclusion, not just a to-do list.
- [0:03:47]-[0:04:18] Two-level planning structure: a high-level list of big objectives (name, levels, story, store page) and then, per objective, a more detailed sub-plan.
- [0:04:18]-[0:04:51] Concrete scope decision shown live: "50 levels across 5 worlds" (10/world), each level defined tightly as "a single-screen puzzle chamber, 2-3 minutes" — turns a vague goal ("make a bunch of levels") into a countable, checkable target.
- [0:05:24] Explicit conservatism principle: given his track record of unfinished projects, he deliberately picked the SMALLER scope option on purpose ("as conservative as possible if I want this to ever go out the door").
- [0:05:24]-[0:05:55] World structure organized around teaching one new mechanic per world, with each mechanic getting a fixed small level budget (4 introductory levels for a new magnet, 3 levels for each smaller mechanic: tutorial level, real puzzle, twist/combo level) — a repeatable formula for generating a level list, not one-off design.
- [0:06:27] Once the formula was applied to his full mechanic list, the game basically filled itself out ("I've basically filled out the entire game just from the plan") — the plan generates the task list, not the other way around.
- [0:06:59] Direct causal claim: having the plan let him bang out 4-5 levels/day and ~40 finished levels (plus ~100 abandoned drafts) in about a month — a concrete before/after productivity number.
- [0:07:00]-[0:08:11] The plan still changed based on internal critique: realizing "10 levels with no magnet in a game about magnets" was structurally wrong (his own Portal analogy: no portal gun for 10 levels), so he moved mechanics between worlds and split one magnet into an easy "can't throw" version (world 1) and a harder "throwable" version (world 3) to ease the difficulty ramp.
- [0:08:41]-[0:09:17] The plan also changed from EXTERNAL feedback: showing in-progress levels to other designers/friends/randoms on Twitter produced ideas that expanded specific worlds beyond their planned 10 levels — he then had to rebalance by adding levels elsewhere to keep proportion, catching scope creep "before it got out of hand."
- [0:09:17] States directly: there's a real difference between adjusting a plan based on feedback/discovery and "randomly aimlessly noodling in the dark" — a plan tolerates change, it just isn't the ABSENCE of a plan.
- [0:10:18] Burnout-management use of the plan: when tired of one kind of work (cerebral level design), the plan lets him switch to a different task type (background art) instead of stopping entirely — the plan as a menu of different-brained tasks, not just a sequential checklist.
- [0:10:50]-[0:11:23] Naming struggled for 2+ years under a placeholder ("Untitled Magnet Game"); his own brainstorm and even ChatGPT's suggestions were rejected as bad; the actual winning name came from a friend's casual suggestion in a Discord.
- [0:11:56]-[0:12:28] Logo design iterated with community feedback (GMTK Discord) — the single best improvement (letters visually "attracted" to the magnetic letter) came from a fan suggestion, not the designer alone.
- [0:12:56]-[0:14:04] Concrete, unglamorous list of what a real Steam store page requires: a paid developer verification process (~$100 + a legal ID check), a written description, tags, system requirements, 5 screenshots, banner/capsule art, and a placeholder release date — shown as a genuine checklist, not advice.
- [0:14:37] Named parallel between video and game platforms: wishlisting on Steam feeds "Valve's algorithm" the same way commenting feeds YouTube's — a media-literacy aside, not really about game dev.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"What's IN, what's OUT" one-page plan (20-25 min, done once mid-course after the prototype/first Blockbench pass)**: kid lists everything their mod WILL have (3 mobs, 2 items, 1 structure) and explicitly writes a "not this year" list. Directly from [0:03:16]. This is the single most transferable structural idea in the batch.
- **Countable scope worksheet**: instead of "make a cool mod," kid fills in a grid like Sheet 2's world/level grid — "3 mobs x 2 behaviors each," "1 item x 3 upgrade tiers" — turning a vague ambition into a checkable box grid. Comes from [0:04:18]-[0:06:27].
- **Mechanic-teaches-mechanic sequencing**: for a kid designing multiple mobs/items, borrow the "world 1 = easy version of the core mechanic, later mobs = harder version" ramp (his Magnus/Max split at [0:07:34]) as a template for ordering which mob/item to build first in a 20-week course.
- **Weekly "which brain today" menu**: when a kid is stuck/bored on one task (e.g., can't figure out a mob behavior), let them switch to a different task type on their plan (texture painting, naming, lore writing) instead of stalling — direct application of [0:10:18], well suited to a 65-minute session where kids' attention varies.
- **Peer name/logo feedback round**: kids show 2-3 name options to the class/a partner (mirrors [0:10:50]-[0:12:28]) rather than deciding alone — cheap, concrete, and demonstrates that outside feedback beats solo brainstorming (including AI brainstorming, which the video shows failing at this exact task).

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The "count it, don't vibe it" planning method transfers cleanly and is probably the single highest-leverage idea for Ben's stated worry about scope: instead of "insane lore and mechanics," a kid's plan becomes "2 mobs, 1 item, 1 structure" with a level of specificity that a teacher can actually check off. The mechanic-teaches-mechanic ramp maps onto mod design well: a first mob/item should be simple (a passive mob with one property), and later ones can combine 2-3 behaviors, exactly as his levels combine mechanics via the "puzzle matrix" he references from an earlier episode. The Steam-page checklist (business/legal process) has zero transfer value but its SHAPE — "here is the exact unglamorous list of steps between 'done' and 'shipped'" — could inspire a simplified "how a real mod gets published/shared" closing lesson (a NeoForge mod's own real checklist: build, test, package, write a description).

### Doesn't transfer
The entire Steam/Steamworks business content (paid developer verification, wishlists, store page requirements, algorithm dynamics) is irrelevant — no kid is publishing a commercial product. The 2-year procrastination/burnout narrative and the YouTube-vs-gamedev time-management struggle are adult freelance-career problems that don't map onto a scheduled weekly class. The specific magnet-mechanic puzzle design (polarity switching, throwable magnets, drift jumps) is genre-specific puzzle-platformer craft with no direct Minecraft/mod equivalent.

## Honest caveats
This is one developer's self-report about his own single project, not a study — "having a plan let me do 4-5 levels a day" is a real but anecdotal, uncontrolled claim (motivation from finally making progress after 2 years of guilt could equally explain the burst). The video doesn't show what happens when a plan turns out wrong in a bigger way than moving levels between worlds — it only shows successful, moderate course-correction, so it may understate how often plans need real rewriting. It agrees strongly with Jonas Tyroller's video in this same batch (both show a written plan/checklist gating the start of "real" production, both show scope being deliberately kept small) — this convergence across two independent devlogs is worth treating as the batch's most load-bearing, cross-validated idea.

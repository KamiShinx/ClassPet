# What Makes A Great In-Game Shop? (Design Doc, 26.8 min)

**What it is:** A "floor by floor" escalating tour of shop design: basic buy/sell, money limits, rental systems, shops-as-skill-trees, quirky/interface shops, evolving shops, shopkeeper personality, stealing/fighting shops, and finally whole item-shop-management games.
**Substance:** substantive: the longest video in the batch but genuinely structured (an explicit complexity ladder), with each "floor" adding one new design lever; one sponsor read.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:58] Floor 1 — Basic Shops (Super Mario RPG): a plain buy/sell/consumables shop's real job is a safety net — if a player is stuck/underpowered, the shop guarantees a path to "just enough" to keep moving. (generic but foundational)
- [0:03:59] Real-life-style limits stop shops from breaking game balance: Elder Scrolls merchants have limited GOLD, so a player can't sell an unlimited pile of junk for unlimited money — a simple guardrail worth remembering any time a shop both buys and sells.
- [0:05:00] Rental vs. ownership as a pricing lever (A Link Between Worlds): cheap rental of almost every item from the start (lose it all on death) vs. an expensive permanent buy that lets you upgrade later — turns currency collection into a long-term goal tied to risk tolerance.
- [0:06:01] Shops can replace what would otherwise be an abstract skill tree menu (Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown sells amulets/upgrades as shop items) — same system, different "skin," which matters for how a game FEELS (a physical shop vs. a menu).
- [0:07:03] Currency-collision warning: if consumables and permanent upgrades compete for the exact same currency, new/struggling players get double-punished (Devil May Cry 1-3: healing items get MORE expensive the more you buy, at the same time you're saving for permanent moves). Devil May Cry 4 fixed it by splitting into two currencies (Proud Souls for upgrades, Red Orbs for consumables) [0:07:33]; DMC5 simplified further by removing basic healing items entirely [0:08:38].
- [0:09:10] Shops can be made deliberately obtuse/quirky for flavor and story texture (Lethal Company: type commands into an old terminal, shared team funds) — works because it fits the game's chaotic co-op horror-comedy tone; this is a stylistic choice, not a universal recommendation.
- [0:10:14] Tiny, randomized shops (roguelike shops with ~3 random items; Balatro's joker shop) create replayability via improvisation — "you don't know what you'll get, so you must adapt" — but only works if ALL possible items are generally useful; two useless options makes the shop feel pointless [0:11:16].
- [0:11:16] Randomized shops are a natural place to surface new/unfamiliar items, sparking curiosity ("I don't know what that is" is itself an incentive).
- [0:12:58] Shops can evolve over time as a long-term player goal: real-world-style hours of operation (Pokemon Gold/Silver's Goldenrod Tunnel — different shops different days); dynamic pricing that responds to player behavior (FFX's O'aka gives discounts if you "donate" to him periodically, and reacts if you tell him prices are too high).
- [0:14:33] Caution against over-designed "quirky" shop interactions: Star Fox Adventures' haggling minigame and Phantom Hourglass's yell-into-the-mic pricing are both called out as more annoying than fun — novelty for its own sake, with real drawbacks (Star Fox's haggling isn't worth the time it costs; the mic gimmick made playing in public embarrassing).
- [0:16:40] Shops can be systemically woven into other mechanics rather than being a side-menu: The World Ends With You ties clothing brand "trendiness" (which waxes/wanes per city district) directly to combat stat bonuses, so there's no single "best gear" — only a "best gear for today," and shopping loyalty itself unlocks better inventory at each specific store.
- [0:17:49] Secret/hidden shops work as a narrative/character moment (Animal Crossing's Crazy Redd sells rare/counterfeit art — most of its value is discovery flavor, not raw stats).
- [0:18:52] Shopkeeper personality (Deltarune's 5 distinct shopkeepers, NieR Automata's Emil cameo shop) can carry a game's tone even when the actual stock is unremarkable — the character IS the content.
- [0:21:00] Stealing from shops as its own subsystem, with real consequences: Link's Awakening — get caught once, permanently branded "Thief," shopkeeper recognizes you forever; Spelunky — shopkeepers are networked, steal/attack one and you're banned from ALL shops in the game, with a forgiveness mechanic in Spelunky 2 [0:23:10].
- [0:23:46] Fighting a shopkeeper as a secret boss (Cobalt Core): winning permanently closes that service for the rest of the run — a real, lasting trade-off, not a free win.
- [0:24:54] Whole "item shop" management genre (Recettear, Moonlighter) — running the shop itself becomes the core gameplay loop, with dungeon-crawling for stock as the other half. (Covered in more depth in the companion video also in this batch — see that note.)

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Design your trader" worksheet (25-30 min)**: pick ONE "floor" from the video's complexity ladder (basic buy/sell, rental, currency-split, evolving prices, or personality-driven) and spec a custom villager/trader NPC around it — what they sell, what currency, does price change over time or with reputation. Directly maps onto Minecraft's existing villager trading.
- **"One currency or two?" check (10 min)**: if a kid's mod has both consumables (potions, food) and permanent upgrades (gear, enchant scrolls) for sale, apply the Devil May Cry lesson [0:07:03] — should these compete for the same currency (emeralds) or be split? Concrete, spec-able balance question.
- **"Give your trader a face" tooltip (5 min)**: borrowing from Deltarune/shopkeeper personality [0:18:52] — even a one-line custom trade-offer flavor text or a distinct trader skin makes a custom villager feel designed, not generic. Cheap, high-payoff, fits Blockbench + a short dialogue string.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Minecraft already has a full trading system (villagers, wandering traders) that directly supports Floor 1 (basic buy/sell) and, with data-driven trades, Floor 2-ish ideas (limited "stock," price scaling with reputation via the existing villager reputation system) — genuinely easy bends of an existing system, well within Gemini's reach via custom villager profession + trade JSON. Randomized small shops (Balatro-style) map onto a wandering trader with a randomized trade pool, also native and buildable. The systemic "shop tied to another mechanic" idea (TWEWY's fashion-trend combat bonuses) and "steal from the shop, get permanently banned" idea would need real new bookkeeping (a per-player reputation/ban flag via NeoForge capabilities) — doable but a bigger ask than a standard trade table, good as a stretch mechanic for an advanced kid.

### Doesn't transfer
The deep "quirky interface" experiments (Star Fox Adventures haggling, Phantom Hourglass microphone pricing) are explicitly flagged by the video itself as failures — good as a "don't do this" caution, not as inspiration. The full item-shop-management genre (run the shop as the whole game) is a genre-level idea, not a mod feature; it belongs to the companion Moonlighter/Recettear video, not to a Minecraft mod component.

## Honest caveats
This is the most information-dense video in the batch (26.8 min, no real padding) and has the best visual references — flag it as a top resource for building a "design your Minecraft trader" worksheet, including screenshots of the UIs shown as inspiration (respecting the "at most one short quote, paraphrase everything" rule for the actual writing). One thing to flag: several of the video's "floors" (systemic trend-based pricing, permanently-locked-out-shopkeeper-after-fighting) are neat but describe entire dedicated game systems most kids won't have time to build in a 20-week, ~65-minute-a-week class — the batch synthesis should be honest that only the first 2-3 "floors" are realistic core-course material; the rest are enrichment/stretch only.

# Game Design School: Character Design (Pixel Pete / Sad Slime Games, 3.5 min)

**What it is:** A dev promoting his own game (Dwerve) rattles off five quick character-design tips over a slideshow of reference images.
**Substance:** thin: five one-line tips with zero worked examples of his own — every "example" is a screenshot of someone else's existing character (League of Legends, Dark Souls, Nintendo mascots, Disney silhouettes), and roughly a third of the runtime is the game plug / outro.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:31] A good artist can still make bland characters; a mediocre artist can make a memorable one — design beats rendering skill (generic, but useful framing for kids who "can't draw").
- [0:00:31] Pick one interesting character trait, usually tied to how they look + their weapon/tool (Teemo's hat + blowdart).
- [0:01:00] Give the character's gear a story reason instead of a generic weapon — Dwerve is a "tinkerer" so he fights with turrets and a crafting hammer, not a sword.
- [0:01:00] Swap the generic weapon for something creative/unusual (a ladder, a scorpion tail, a giant hammer) — pure brainstorm prompt.
- [0:01:29] Only ONE memorable trait per character — don't stack five gimmicks (generic, but a genuinely useful constraint).
- [0:01:29] Limit the character's color palette; cites Sonic/Kirby/Yoshi/Pac-Man/Spyro/Crash as all using 2-4 colors max, and says it's why they're memorable vs. a "guess how many colors" bit with busier designs.
- [0:01:59] Silhouette must read as unique and simple — shows the classic Disney/cartoon black-silhouette identification sheet as proof (see frames below).
- [0:01:59] Don't overcomplicate the silhouette — same logic as color: more detail = harder to remember.
- [0:02:08 approx] Pick 1-2 traits and exaggerate them slightly (Ziggs' oversized bomb, Braum's giant door-shield) — again "just a little," not overboard.
- [0:02:37] Bonus/final tip: combine two unrelated concepts for charm ("a slime and a knight" -> slime rider), credits Akira Toriyama (Dragon Quest) as good at this.

## What the frames add
Sheet 1 shows his actual research process on screen: Google image searches for "generic knight," League splash art, a robot/mecha sketch, Kirby image grid, a Crash Bandicoot image grid, and a "guess the character from 3 colored dots" quiz graphic (00:01:52) that visually proves the palette-memory point better than the narration does. Sheet 2's strongest single frame is 00:02:16 — a wall of ~50 classic cartoon character silhouettes (Disney/Looney Tunes/Pokemon style) used purely as black shapes, which is the clearest visual demonstration of "silhouette must read on its own" in the whole batch. Also a caricature sketch (00:02:24, exaggeration), a WoW dwarf turnaround sheet (00:02:48, palette+trait consistency across a cast), and a row of Dragon Quest slime variants (00:02:56) illustrating the "one simple shape, many recolors" idea, though the video never states that lesson explicitly — it's only visible in the frame.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Silhouette test worksheet (15-20 min)**: kid designs their mob/character, then fills the shape solid black (or we do it for them via a quick tool) and asks: "can you tell what it is without color or detail?" If two kids' silhouettes look the same, one has to change. Comes from [0:01:59] + the 00:02:16 frame.
- **3-color rule card**: a simple tooltip/checklist item — "pick 2-3 colors max for your character, write them down before you touch Blockbench." Comes from [0:01:29].
- **"One weird thing" brainstorm sheet (10 min)**: kid lists their character's job/backstory in one sentence, then answers "what's the ONE object/feature that shows that job?" (ladder-as-weapon style). Comes from [0:01:00]-[0:01:29].
- **Mash-up generator**: two random word lists (creature + role, e.g. "slime" + "knight") kids roll or pick from to force a Toriyama-style combo when they're stuck. Comes from [0:02:37].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Silhouette and color-limit advice survive almost unchanged and arguably matter MORE in Minecraft — a mob seen small, at a distance, in a blocky low-res world lives or dies on silhouette and a tight palette (Minecraft's own vanilla mobs already follow this: creeper's silhouette, 2-3 colors). The "one memorable trait, don't stack five" rule maps directly onto Blockbench where extra cubes cost effort and read as noise at 16x16. "Design from function" (tinkerer -> turrets) survives well and is a strong prompt for pairing a mob's *behavior* (the modded mechanic Gemini builds) with its *look*.

### Doesn't transfer
The exaggeration tip ("make the bomb too big to carry") is a 2D illustration/proportion trick that's harder to execute in cubes — you can make one Blockbench cube oversized, but it reads much cruder than a drawn exaggeration and risks just looking broken rather than stylish; needs testing, not a guaranteed win. The specific reference characters (League, Dark Souls, WoW) are adult/M-rated aesthetics irrelevant to what an 11-13-year-old should be citing.

## Honest caveats
This is the thinnest video in the batch — a sub-4-minute promo video padded with an outro and a Twitter handle card, five tips with no depth or process shown, only borrowed reference images. Every "example" is someone else's finished work, never the creator's own sketch-to-final process, so there's nothing here about HOW to arrive at a design, only checkpoints to judge one against. The color-limit and silhouette rules are well-established, uncontroversial game-design/animation advice (echoed independently by Proko and pikat later in this batch) so they're trustworthy despite the thin sourcing here; treat this video as the fastest possible summary of ideas other videos in the batch cover with more rigor, not a primary source in its own right.

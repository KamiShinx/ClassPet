# WORLD BUILDING for Video Games! (Alex Renard, 14.5 min)

**What it is:** An 11-tip listicle aimed specifically at game worldbuilding rather than novels, illustrated with named game examples (Stardew Valley, Dark Souls, World of Warcraft, Fallout: New Vegas, Elder Scrolls, Super Mario Galaxy).
**Substance:** substantive and the most game-native video in the batch — every tip is framed in terms of mechanics/level design/NPCs rather than prose — but it's a flat list of features to add, not a sequence for where to start, and the opener is a ~40s plug for his own game (Project Dagger).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:32] The world needs to "live outside the player's actions" — NPCs have schedules/routines independent of what the player does (Stardew Valley townsfolk example) — makes a world feel inhabited rather than staged.
- [0:01:35] Show, don't tell (generic storytelling rule, but given a concrete game-mechanic version): instead of a text explanation, demonstrate — e.g. a failed chop animation shows the player they need special axe access, rather than a pop-up telling them.
- [0:02:38] Characters need visible wants/needs/personality — in attire, expressions, dialogue, AND their quests/combat style, not just description.
- [0:03:42] Mechanics should reflect the fiction: shop prices should shift with regional scarcity (sparse farmland -> pricier produce, cheaper meat).
- [0:04:16] Difficulty/enemy toughness should be legible from the environment itself — a beautiful area trains players to relax, a hostile-looking one cues caution (Dark Souls contrast example).
- [0:04:48] Level design should physically embody the world's premise (his own game: a permanently-dark continent gets giant bioluminescent mushrooms instead of trees/sun-farming).
- [0:05:54] Place points of interest at "active elements" — visible physical evidence of the problems referenced in quests/dialogue, so players intuit stakes before being told.
- [0:06:25] Every faction/group needs a legible philosophy, shown through behavior/aesthetic rather than stated outright (NCR vs. Legion in Fallout: New Vegas as the model).
- [0:07:57] Mood: color palette, music key/instrumentation, and character design should signal tone together and consistently, so players learn to read danger/safety from atmosphere alone.
- [0:09:05] Level/world design should have a consistent "personality" via art style, scale, and shape choices (Mario Galaxy's whimsical, recognizably-shaped planets).
- [0:10:08] Leave some things unexplained on purpose — visible but unreachable landmarks (WoW's Maelstrom, Shadow of the Colossus's locked door) deepen a sense of a bigger world without lore text.
- [0:11:13] Avoid information dumps/single tutorial-NPC lectures; scatter discovery instead so players generate their own questions through play (his own game's bad old tutorial vs. his fix) — the most concrete, self-critical example in the video.
- [0:12:51] Hard vs. soft worldbuilding for games: hard = fixed protagonist/narrator (Halo), soft = player-authored character (Elder Scrolls); linear games suit hard, open/sandbox games — he names Terraria and **Minecraft** specifically — suit soft.

## What the frames add
Entirely gameplay footage of the named games cut to match whatever's being discussed at that second (Stardew Valley farm/town, a roguelike, a top-down survival game, Fallout: New Vegas exteriors, WoW zones, Mario Galaxy, Dark Souls/Elden Ring b-roll) — illustrative b-roll only. No diagrams, no on-screen lists or text summaries anywhere across all 4 sheets; the video is entirely audio-driven.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Faction philosophy card**: for two opposing mobs/villager factions in a kid's mod, write one line each on what they believe the world should be like, then translate it into ONE visible difference (texture/color, structure shape, block palette) instead of a paragraph — ~15 min, from the "show don't tell" + faction-philosophy tips.
- **Mechanic-matches-fiction check**: before Gemini writes an item/mob behavior, kid answers "if my structure/creature is in a [dry/cold/dangerous] biome, what should be rarer, harder, or more expensive nearby because of it?" — generalizes the shop-price tip into a reusable design-consistency tooltip.
- **"One thing you never explain" prompt**: kids place one landmark or locked structure in their build with no explanation and no way in — a 5-minute add-on rule, from the Maelstrom/Shadow of the Colossus example.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Extremely well — the only video in the batch built around games rather than prose, and it explicitly names Minecraft as a soft-worldbuilding sandbox where hard narrative isn't the fit, validating the class's actual medium. "Mechanics reflect world" and "show don't tell via behavior" map directly onto item/mob behaviors Gemini can build. NPC daily schedules (Stardew-level) are a heavier lift — full AI daily routines are a stretch for a beginner NeoForge mod, though a simplified version (a villager only present at certain times) is plausible.

### Doesn't transfer
The "scatter discovery" tutorial-design point assumes a much bigger, more polished game than 8 kids will build in weekly 65-minute sessions; the music-key/instrumentation mood analysis requires audio design skills outside scope; his own game's economy-balancing example is more systems-design depth than an 11-13-year-old needs in week one.

## Honest caveats
This is a flat list of "things good game worlds have," not a sequence — it never answers "what do you decide first," so on the class's specific special-focus question it reads closer to commentary than procedure, just game-native commentary rather than literary commentary. It has no checklist/sins framing despite being tip-shaped; the teacher would need to convert it into a worksheet.

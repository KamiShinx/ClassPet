# Creating Proactive, Relatable, and Capable Characters: Lecture #5 (Brandon Sanderson, 70 min)

**What it is:** A BYU lecture (filmed for the "Writing Science Fiction and Fantasy" course, republished for YouTube) laying out Sanderson's three-axis framework for what makes a character compelling: proactive, relatable, capable — plus a first pass at flaws/restrictions/limitations and simple "arcs."
**Substance:** substantive: one clear, reusable framework, worked through with many film/book examples and a long student Q&A that stress-tests it (prison characters, antiheroes, competing protagonists, depression/inactivity). Some of the runtime is scheduling chatter and movie-nostalgia riffing (Hudson Hawk, LOTR memes) that adds nothing.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:09:05] Core framework: compelling characters are a mix of three sliding-scale traits — **proactive** (drives the plot), **relatable** (likeable/sympathetic), **capable** (competent at something). Most characters are high on one, low on another, and growing on the third — rarely all three at once.
- [0:11:18] Proactivity is the single strongest lever for making a character "readable": we bond with whoever makes the story happen, and resent whoever stalls it.
- [0:15:39] The "villain problem": villains are often written more proactive than the hero, so audiences find them more interesting by default — the fix is either forcing the hero into action early, or building their proactivity growth into the arc.
- [0:20:38] (Vonnegut, cited) Give a character a want from the very first scene, even something small ("a cup of coffee") — motivation is what separates a wooden character from a real one.
- [0:23:11] Sanderson's claim: ~60% of "this character didn't work for me" reader complaints trace back to unclear or inconsistent motivation, not dialogue or prose.
- [0:26:02] "Small things evoke the larger": establish a small, concrete stake (Mulan and her father) before the character is asked to do something huge (save the kingdom) — the small thing has to land first.
- [0:27:09] Show proactivity in miniature before the plot needs it big — Mulan solving the chicken-feeding problem cleverly signals "this is a problem-solver" before the war arc starts.
- [0:28:44] For a character trapped/limited from acting (prison, illness): proactivity can still show through small attempted actions and plans, even failed ones — it's the *trying* that reads as proactive, not the scale of the action.
- [0:29:51] Hardest case: a character whose own mental state (e.g. depression) kills their will to act. Sanderson's fix from his own book: give them a smaller internal quest (searching through beliefs one at a time) so there's still forward *motion*, even if it's motion downward — static is what's unreadable, not "bad" motion.
- [0:38:06] "Save the cat / kick the dog" (cites the book): one small act of kindness (or cruelty) is an efficient shorthand for establishing a character's moral alignment fast.
- [0:40:42] Relatability trick: show a character's good qualities through *another character's* eyes rather than their own internal narration — self-praise reads as arrogance, external admiration doesn't.
- [0:42:30] Self-awareness of a flaw, especially if the character is visibly working on it, buys them a "pass" from the audience (Mr. Darcy example).
- [0:43:37] Relatability also comes from small universal traits/annoyances unrelated to the plot (Garfield hating Mondays) — but flag that "universal" traits aren't universal to every audience member, so pick genuinely common ones.
- [0:45:52] Villains gain strength (Magneto > generic villain) when given one relatable *reason*, even if their actions stay monstrous.
- [0:48:35] Spider-Man villain pattern: they start relatable, take one step past a moral line, and become progressively less relatable/more "crazy" as the story goes on — relatability crashes as the character justifies worse acts.
- [0:49:10] Antihero definitions: classic literary antihero = a protagonist who refuses to act (this doesn't map for us); pop-culture antihero = a low-relatability, high-capability, high-proactivity character who mostly fights people worse than themselves (John Wick, Venom, Punisher).
- [0:55:52] Tropes/archetypes are useful as a shortcut the audience already trusts — then a character becomes interesting by not quite fitting the box (a quirk expands the trope rather than being random "wackiness" bolted on).
- [0:58:36] Introduces flaw/restriction/limitation as three different kinds of "weakness" (flaw = something to overcome as an arc; restriction = a self-imposed limit you don't want them to break, e.g. Superman's moral code; limitation = an external, often physical limit the story is about working around, not fixing). Full treatment deferred to a later lecture ("Sanderson's Second Law").
- [1:00:10] A flaw is relatable early, but becomes a liability/annoyance if the character keeps failing to progress on it too long (Luke Skywalker example).
- [1:01:16] The "Michael problem" (Lost): a character's single defining trait/motivation, if repeated with no variation or texture, goes from relatable to actively hated by the audience — over-indexing on one trait kills the character.
- [1:02:56] Capability: audiences enjoy competence for its own sake (Dirk Pitt / James Bond) but leaning too hard into invincibility reduces relatability — Casino Royale deliberately made Bond fail more to restore relatability.
- [1:06:11] Capability doesn't have to be combat/skill-based — Samwise Gamgee's "superpower" is loyalty, and that single trait is what resolves the plot.
- [1:07:53] Arcs, defined simply: a character moving up or down on one of the three axes over the story (most commonly relatability, by resolving a flaw, or capability, by learning a skill).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Three-slider mob card"**: for any mob/boss/NPC the kid designs, they fill in a one-page card with three sliders (Proactive / Relatable / Capable, 1-5 each) and one sentence justifying each score. ~15 min. Comes directly from the [0:09:05] framework. Output: a design doc Gemini can be handed almost verbatim ("this mob is high-proactive, low-relatable, medium-capable, meaning it attacks on sight, has no sympathetic tell, but only in melee range").
- **"What does it want?" tooltip**: one sentence, "my mob wants ___ (even something small)", applied to every custom mob before any Java gets written — directly from the Vonnegut/motivation beat [0:20:38, 0:23:11]. This becomes the actual AI-goal spec Gemini needs anyway (a Minecraft mob's `Goal` classes are literally "what it wants to do and when"), so the craft lesson and the technical spec are the same document.
- **"Save the cat / kick the dog" behaviour tell**: kids pick one observable *behaviour*, not backstory, that signals whether their mob is friendly or hostile before the player gets close (a villager-type mob that gives you an item unprompted vs. a mob that attacks a nearby animal). Comes from [0:38:06]; forces the "relatable" trait into something Gemini can implement as an actual in-game action, not prose.
- **Boss motivation-in-behaviour worksheet**: since bosses can't deliver dialogue easily, kids write the boss's one-line motivation, then translate it into 2-3 concrete attack patterns/phase changes that "show" it (e.g. a boss "protecting its hoard" flees to defend a treasure block instead of chasing the player) — from the Michael/Sam Gamgee/capability beats [1:01:16, 1:06:11].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Proactive/Relatable/Capable maps cleanly onto **mob design**, not novel character work: Proactive → does the mob initiate (attacks, gives quests, triggers events) or only react? Capable → what mechanical thing is it actually good at (one clear ability, à la Sam's loyalty, not a laundry list of stats). Relatable → a single legible behavioural tell, because a mob has no interiority to narrate.
- Flaw/restriction/limitation is a genuinely useful **game-design** lens even though it's a craft term: a mob's flaw = an exploitable weak point (fire-vulnerable), a restriction = a rule it won't break even to its own detriment (won't leave its lair), a limitation = a hard mechanical constraint (can't swim). All three are things Gemini can implement as boolean checks/conditions in mob AI code, which is a nice accidental fit.
- The "small thing before the big thing" beat [0:26:02] survives as sequencing advice for boss fights/questlines: show the mob's small, low-stakes behaviour before the big set-piece behaviour, so its later dramatic move (fleeing, roaring, transforming) reads as consistent instead of random.

### Doesn't transfer
- The entire antihero/grimdark/moral-complexity discussion [0:49:10–0:54:40] is prose-craft for adult novel protagonists; a mob has no arc of complicity or audience-shifting sympathy to build.
- "Michael problem" as stated is about a human character repeating one line of dialogue for 20+ episodes of television; not directly actionable for a mob with a handful of possible lines/behaviours, though the underlying warning ("don't make the single trait annoying by overusing it") is fine as a caution for NPC dialogue if the class ever adds villager barks.
- Long tangents on antihero genre history, Lost casting trivia, Dune/Paul Atreides moral complexity, and the audience Q&A about depression-as-plot are adult-novelist-specific and do not map to 65-minute class sessions with 11-13 year olds.
- Tropes/archetype subversion as a craft move [0:55:52] is real but abstract; most kids at this level will not yet have enough genre literacy to consciously subvert a trope — better used passively (teacher points it out in examples) than as an assignment.

## Honest caveats
- The framework is presented as Sanderson's personal teaching tool ("this is my framework"), not a research-backed model — treat it as one working heuristic, not a settled theory of character.
- Sanderson repeatedly defers the flaws/restrictions/limitations framework to a future lecture ("Sanderson's Second Law") — our batch doesn't include that lecture, so our worksheet above is our own extrapolation from the one-paragraph summary he gives here, not a fully worked Sanderson method.
- Several examples (Last Jedi, Rings of Power) are opinionated pop-culture takes presented as consensus fact; fine as illustration, not evidence.
- My own extrapolation: nothing here addresses *player-facing* mechanics (cost/limit/rule systems) — that's the magic-systems lecture (3Y9p53C1lP4) in this batch, which is the stronger transfer target for actual mod mechanics; this lecture is best used for mob/boss *flavor and behaviour design*, not systems design.

# How Can I Stop Item Hoarding In Games? (Design Doc, 17.5 min)

**What it is:** Diagnoses why players hoard consumables/currency/crafting mats out of fear of "needing it later," then catalogs design techniques (caps, spend pressure, regeneration, run resets, removing consumables entirely) that reduce hoarding.
**Substance:** substantive: a genuinely causal argument (five compounding design choices that create hoarding in FFIX, each with its own targeted fix) plus real counter-examples where a fix backfires; two sponsor reads (start and middle).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:46] Definition: hoarding = avoiding use of a limited resource out of fear of a future, unclear worse moment ("what if I need it later?"). Applies to any limited resource: consumables, MP, currency, crafting mats.
- [0:03:46] **Five compounding causes (FFIX case study)**: (1) very high inventory caps (99 per item) remove use-it-or-lose-it pressure; (2) top-tier items are extremely strong, so using one "wastes" it; (3) rare items are hard to restock; (4) low difficulty means players rarely feel forced to spend; (5) unclear how close the game is to ending, so players can't judge when it's "safe" to spend. None is fatal alone — together they create heavy hoarding.
- [0:05:52] Fix #1 — hard caps on individual items, scaled to power (Shin Megami Tensei IV: 30 cap on weak heals, only 5 on Full Heals) — forces use of the strongest items faster.
- [0:06:25] Fix #2 — a small TOTAL inventory space, not per-item caps (RE4's Attache Case): forces genuine trade-offs about what to carry, but only works if the game paces item drops carefully — a stingy drop rate + a tiny inventory clash and just make the game unpleasant [0:06:57].
- [0:07:27] Fix #3 — make resources drain fast and refill fast in near-equal amounts (DOOM 2016 ammo: scarce-looking but not scarce-feeling because pickups roughly match spend, plus enemy/weapon-specific drop bonuses that push weapon-swapping instead of hoarding one favorite).
- [0:08:28] Fix #4 — replace consumables with a short-term RECHARGING resource (Dark Souls' Estus Flask: limited uses, but refills fully at every bonfire) — turns "hoard forever" into "manage a short, learnable cycle," and becomes a legitimate skill/upgrade path.
- [0:09:00] **Caution**: recharging resources can create new problems. Dishonored's mana regenerates only if you WAIT ~5 seconds between casts — this discourages exactly the fast ability-chaining the game's combat/stealth is built around, turning flashy play into "cast, wait, cast, wait."
- [0:10:36] Crafting-game hoarding is broader — nearly EVERY material could theoretically matter later. Fix: maximize in-game information (Monster Hunter World's Field Guide shows drop locations/rates; multiple side-systems — Botanist, Palico Safari, Argosy — spread out how you get mats so no single material feels irreplaceable; a "meant to be sold" tag flags junk explicitly).
- [0:12:39] Named anti-pattern: FFVIII's Junction system — spells are consumable AND directly wired to character stats, so using a spell in battle immediately makes you weaker. This is described as "so pro-hoarding it hurts": it manufactures the exact fear-of-loss that causes hoarding.
- [0:13:11] Named anti-pattern #2: Paper Mario Sticker Star — every attack is a single-use consumable sticker, and boss fights/puzzles often require ONE specific "Thing" sticker; waste it in a random encounter and you must backtrack. Actively punishes experimentation, the opposite of what a fun combat system wants.
- [0:14:43] Structural fixes that remove hoarding by design, not by patching: roguelikes (Isaac, Gungeon, Spelunky) don't carry progress between runs, so "save it for later" has no "later" — hoarding evaporates because the fear it's based on doesn't apply; survival games (Don't Starve, MGS3) put an expiration timer on items, making stockpiling actively wasteful.
- [0:16:20] Most radical fix: remove consumables entirely (Xenoblade Chronicles ties healing to an ability cooldown, no potions/MP at all) — "hoarding isn't a problem" because there's nothing to hoard.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Why would a player hoard this?" worksheet (15-20 min)**: for any custom consumable a kid adds (potion, food, tool charge), walk the five FFIX causes as a checklist — is the cap too high, is it too strong to "waste," is it too rare, is the game too easy, is the endpoint unclear — and pick ONE fix (a scaled cap, a decay timer, a recharge-at-a-station mechanic). This is the most concretely spec-able idea in the batch for a custom item's balance pass.
- **"Charges, not stacks" tooltip (5 min)**: introduce the Estus Flask pattern — "X uses, refills at a specific spot" — as the default recommended pattern for any custom healing/buff item, since it's the cleanest fit for NeoForge NBT/capability tracking (see below).
- **"Don't punish experimentation" rule (generic but well-anchored)**: when a kid designs a boss fight or puzzle gated behind ONE specific consumable (echoing Sticker Star's mistake), flag it in review — bosses/puzzles should accept multiple valid tools, not force hoarding a single "correct" item.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
This bends Minecraft's existing systems well rather than needing new ones. Minecraft already has inventory stack caps (64, or 1/16 for some items) — a kid can tune a custom item's max stack size down (SMT IV-style scaled cap) with a one-line data component change, trivial for Gemini. A charge-based item (Estus Flask pattern) maps directly onto NeoForge's item NBT/component "uses remaining" pattern, refillable at a custom block (a "recharge station") — squarely buildable, and this is probably the single best "make a custom healing item that doesn't feel hoardable" pattern to teach. A decaying/expiring item (Don't Starve pattern) is also buildable via NBT tick-down, similar to vanilla's existing food-spoilage-adjacent mechanics in recent Minecraft versions. The roguelike "no progress carries between runs" fix does NOT fit a persistent-world Minecraft mod at all — skip it as a direct lesson, though the underlying idea ("if loss doesn't matter, hoarding vanishes") is worth keeping as a design PRINCIPLE.

### Doesn't transfer
FFVIII's Junction system and Paper Mario's Sticker Star are too mechanically specific (whole custom battle systems) to be worksheet material beyond their one-line cautionary lessons already captured above; don't ask kids to study the systems themselves.

## Honest caveats
This is one of the more rigorous, causally-argued videos in the batch — it isolates five distinct, independently fixable causes rather than offering one vague tip, and is worth citing directly in the batch synthesis. One real tension worth flagging for Ben: the video's own favorite fix (Estus Flask-style recharging resource) is explicitly shown to backfire in the WRONG game (Dishonored) if the recharge condition fights the game's intended pace — so "add a recharge mechanic" is not a drop-in fix, it needs matching to how kids actually want their custom item used (fast combat vs. slow exploration). This nuance should carry into any worksheet we build from this video, not get flattened into "just make it recharge."

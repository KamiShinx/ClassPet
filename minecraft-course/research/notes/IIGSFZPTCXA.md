# How To Worldbuild on Earth? - Lecture #9 (Brandon Sanderson, 74 min)

**What it is:** Live student Q&A following Lecture #8, nominally on "worldbuilding on Earth" (real-world/urban
fantasy settings) but the title question is answered in the first ~9 minutes; the rest ranges freely across
worldbuilding, viewpoint, pacing, battle design, naming collisions, and how his own writing class/business works.

**Substance:** mixed: the opening answer and several mid-Q&A answers are substantive and concrete, but roughly a
third of the runtime is meta content about Sanderson's own BYU class logistics, book recommendations, and personal
pet peeves (dialogue paragraphing, past perfect tense) that has no use for this project.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:38] When writing in/near the real world you can shortcut explanation for anything readers already know
  (readers don't need American football explained) — same "genre convention" shortcut idea as Lecture #8.
- [0:03:44] Getting small real-world facts wrong (he cites Chicago geography, military details) can break a
  reader's trust in the whole book, because "armchair experts" on real subjects are common; the more real your
  setting, the higher the research bar.
- [0:05:21] "Bonehead character" trick: giving your POV character a plausible reason to not know a domain (e.g.
  doesn't like horses) lets you get away with under-researching that domain without breaking trust.
- [0:06:26] Real-world worldbuilding carries appropriation risk (his example: using real Navajo beliefs as a fantasy
  magic system) — do it consciously and with research, not accidentally, and be ready to own the choice.
- [0:09:08] "Contrast is your friend": defining your world/setting by what's different from ours (or from a
  baseline) is an efficient way to make a premise legible fast (his example: a world exactly like ours except the
  Beatles never existed).
- [0:09:42]-[0:14:06] Viewpoint craft (Robert Jordan/George R. R. Martin technique): let each character's own voice
  color how they describe the same setting, and open a scene/chapter naming the POV character first so readers
  anchor immediately. (generic craft, prose-specific)
- [0:15:12] For worldbuilding lore that matters to the plot but not to the character: signal its importance through
  narrative structure (an authority figure stresses it, or a cutaway scene shows it mattering) rather than through
  the character's own interest.
- [0:17:20] A meaningful fraction of readers skip prologues/epigraphs entirely — don't put load-bearing worldbuilding
  information ONLY there.
- [0:19:40] "Gorilla in the phone booth": introducing a big new surprise very late (he cites cutting a
  three-quarters-mark plot twist from his first novel) distracts from the climax you've built toward; late
  additions should tie off known threads, not introduce new ones.
- [0:21:21]-[0:22:29] Research strategy: the first weeks of researching a topic get you disproportionately far
  (rough 50% competence for relatively little effort); going from "good enough" to "expert" takes far longer with
  diminishing returns — so front-load just enough research to avoid major errors, then use expert readers to catch
  what's left. (He cites twice being overconfident, on DID and on G-forces, requiring late rewrites.)
- [0:24:46] Uses a wiki tool to track his shared worldbuilding "bible" (linked entries per location/character) so he
  doesn't contradict earlier descriptions of the same character/place across a long series. (generic continuity
  tool, prose-specific in his description but the underlying need — a shared reference doc — is general)
- [0:35:36] Gives his "gorilla in the phone booth" concept more examples (Last Jedi, Wheel of Time prologue, an
  unpublished student novel) — distinguishes a *promised* big reveal (fine) from an *ambushing* one (bad).
- [0:38:47] On writing full in-world history/encyclopedia books: warns they demand huge amounts of supporting art
  and sell a fraction of the main series — a "want, not need," only worth it if you're personally passionate about
  the lore-for-its-own-sake the way Tolkien or George R. R. Martin are; he isn't. (Relevant caution: pure lore
  compendia are a side project with a real cost, not core value.)
- [0:44:48] Real technique for inventing non-analogous geography: get basic literacy in geology (rain shadows,
  continental divides, why lakes form where they do) so you have "rules to break intentionally," not accidentally.
- [0:46:27] "Sanderson's Zeroeth Law": start from the coolest thing you want in the story/world and justify it
  backward with internal logic, rather than extrapolating forward from real-world plausibility like hard sci-fi
  does. Explicitly the opposite process from real research-first worldbuilding.
- [0:47:35] Popular-science/pop-nonfiction reading (he names a well-known "big idea" survey book) as a spark
  source for setting ideas — read for the inspiring image or angle, not the deep technical accuracy.
- [0:47:35]-[0:49:12] His two best-known settings originated from real sensory experiences (hiking slot canyons;
  driving through a fog bank) — go looking for a specific striking image/feeling, then build backward from it.
- [0:56:23]-[1:00:14] For worldbuilding believable battles/large-scale conflict: either (a) invest real effort to
  become genuinely knowledgeable (he claims ~80% competence on historical warfare) and then deliberately break one
  or two real-world constraints on purpose (he removed cavalry, added magical food resupply) so critics can't
  nitpick logistics, or (b) the cheap shortcut — take a real historical battle, flip/mirror its geography, and
  reuse its beat structure as your scene's skeleton.
- [1:09:01] "Magic did it" is a legitimate, not lazy, answer for physics-breaking elements, as long as the internal
  logic of the magic system stays consistent — but if a plot's climax hinges on an unexplained "why," that's a
  cop-out.
- [1:11:12] On accidental name collisions with existing IP: judge by how large/iconic the colliding term already is
  (a small overlap is fine and common; something as saturated as a famous franchise term is not) and always run
  names past outside readers, who catch things you won't (his editorial team catching an accidental Greek-mythology
  pun in a title).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Contrast Card worksheet**: from [0:09:08]. Kid writes one sentence: "My world/biome is exactly like [a real
  place/Minecraft biome] except ___." Fast (5 min), forces a legible hook instead of a vague pile of ideas — good
  opener for a session.
- **Cool-Thing-First worksheet**: from Sanderson's Zeroeth Law [0:46:27]. Kid names ONE awesome thing they want in
  their mod (an item, a mob ability, a structure) before anything else, then answers "why does this exist in my
  world" in one or two sentences. Directly matches "spec before prompting" (the course's existing Gemini-prompting
  discipline) — this IS the spec-writing step, just framed as backward-justification instead of forward research.
- **One Real Detail spark**: from the slot-canyon/fog-bank anecdotes [0:47:35]-[0:49:12]. Ask each kid: "what's one
  real place, weather, or texture that felt cool/creepy/awesome to you?" as a seed for a biome or structure design.
  ~10 min discussion, good for the biome-design week specifically.
- **Battle/Boss Shortcut**: from [0:56:23]-[1:00:14]. For designing a boss fight or mob encounter, "take something
  you've fought or seen in another game and change one big rule" is a usable simplified version of his
  flip-a-real-battle technique — easier for 11-13-year-olds than deep historical study.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Contrast and Zeroeth Law both survive well: Minecraft worldbuilding is inherently about picking one strong,
  buildable idea (a biome, a mob, a structure) and justifying it, not accumulating research depth — this matches
  the format better than Lecture #8's prose-craft content does.
- The wiki/continuity-document idea [0:24:46] transfers as "keep one shared doc per kid (or per class) listing
  their world's established facts" so a kid doesn't contradict their own mod's lore between weeks — useful low-tech
  practice, not a specific tool recommendation.
- "Magic did it" [1:09:01] transfers directly and usefully: Minecraft already runs on non-physical rules (mining,
  crafting, respawning); kids can lean on "it's magic/technology" for anything Gemini can't sensibly explain,
  as long as it's consistent within their own mod.

### Doesn't transfer
- Almost everything about prose viewpoint/pacing/dialogue craft [0:09:42]-[0:14:06], [0:52:59]-[0:55:46],
  [1:06:12]-[1:08:29] (scene/sequel pacing, paragraph-per-speaker dialogue rules, past perfect tense) — no prose
  narrator in a Minecraft mod.
- The "spend weeks/months becoming a domain expert" research model [0:21:21] doesn't fit 65-minute weekly sessions;
  even his own "faster" version (get to 50% competence) is more time than this class can spend per topic.
- Full lore-encyclopedia/world-bible-as-product discussion [0:38:47] — explicitly a big, resource-heavy side
  project even for a professional team; not something 11-13-year-olds should be steered toward instead of building
  the mod.
- All the meta content about his BYU class admissions, writing-group structure, grading, and book recommendations
  [1:00:14]-[1:06:12] — irrelevant to K-12 game design.
- Appropriation-and-research-ethics nuance [0:06:26] is real but too advanced/abstract a discussion for this age
  group in full; worth reducing to one simple guideline ("don't just copy a real culture's whole identity for your
  mob/faction without thinking about it") rather than teaching as-is.

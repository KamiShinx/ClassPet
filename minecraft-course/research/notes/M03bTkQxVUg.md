# Collectibles - How To Make Chores Fun In Games (Design Doc, 17.8 min)

**What it is:** Original title on screen: "Collectibles: Don't Do What Donkey Kong Does." Breaks collectible design into Extrinsic vs Intrinsic motivation, then covers the support tools (radars, hint systems, guides) needed to make collecting bearable.
**Substance:** substantive: a clear two-part framework (motivation type, then support tooling) with strong positive AND negative examples for each; one sponsor read.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:48] Extrinsic motivation = give a reward for collecting. Simplest form: pure coercion ("do it or no game for you" — Mario 64 stars gating progress). Coercion alone risks players quitting if they get stuck on one item.
- [0:02:23] Fix for coercion: let players CHOOSE which items count toward the gate, and don't require 100% (Mario 64 star menu; contrast with Rayman 1, which demands EVERY cage before the final boss — later Rayman games fixed this with a threshold).
- [0:04:01] Extrinsic collectibles work well as a breadcrumb trail toward a clear final prize when tied to character power/XP/cosmetics (Spider-Man side-quest tokens), or hybridized with a progression gate (Yooka-Laylee's bees both power you up AND gate the final level).
- [0:05:10] For "completionist" players, an elaborate, substantial reward for 100% completion (a whole extra campaign, e.g. Super Luigi Galaxy) makes the full grind feel worth it.
- [0:05:44] Named anti-pattern: Crash Bandicoot 4's gem system — punishing, not fun — three compounding problems: (1) huge stages, (2) gems hidden with poor visual cues/backtracking, (3) do-or-die sequences where missing one gem means a full redo with no way back. Clear example of "more collectibles ≠ better."
- [0:08:24] Intrinsic motivation = the player wants the goal for its own sake; start with basics — juice it (sound cue, sparkle, little dance) — cheap and effective, but shallow on its own.
- [0:08:55] Deeper intrinsic motivation: place collectibles as an excuse to test movement/exploration skill. Celeste's optional strawberries unlock NOTHING extrinsic — they're bragging rights placed in hard-to-reach spots, and work because failure has near-zero cost (frequent checkpoints).
- [0:10:00] Banjo-Kazooie's notes act as a breadcrumb trail that guides players toward higher-value collectibles (Jinjos, Jiggies) and toward interesting spots, supporting the level design rather than being scattered randomly.
- [0:10:34] Breath of the Wild deliberately does NOT expect 100% completion (900 Korok seeds far exceeds reasonable collection) — the design goal is "collectible as catalyst for exploring," not "collectible as checklist," and that's stated as fine even if most players only get "a little."
- [0:12:13] Named anti-pattern #2: Donkey Kong 64 — "more is more" collect-a-thon with 5 characters x 5 collectible types each, most only usable by one specific character, forcing repeated scouring of the same level up to 5 times; placement feels arbitrary rather than purposeful.
- [0:13:18] Even a well-designed collectible system fails without in-game support TOOLS: Pokemon's Pokedex (checklist + locator hints + lore + gallery, all in one); Spyro's dragonfly (on-demand radar to nearby gems, avoiding the "needle in haystack" problem); Mario Odyssey's two-tier hint system (Talkatoo names remaining moons, Hint Toad pinpoints for a fee).
- [0:14:51] Counter-example: Mario Odyssey's Purple Coins are unlimited-need, finite, and have NO tracking tool (short of a paid Amiibo) — flagged as the exception that shows what happens without a release valve.
- [0:15:28] Counter-example #2: Psychonauts' Figments — visually blend into backgrounds, essentially no in-game tracking tool — "obnoxious," not satisfying, a needle-in-haystack failure.
- [0:15:58] Mismatch failure mode: collectible criteria that fight the game's own core mechanics (Sonic Advance 2's hidden-rings bonus stage demands careful exploration in a game engineered for "go fast," with points of no return — actively unpleasant).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Extrinsic or intrinsic?" collectible-design worksheet (20 min)**: for a custom collectible item/mob drop a kid designs, decide which bucket it's in and justify with one sentence — if extrinsic, what does it unlock/build toward; if intrinsic, what makes finding it satisfying on its own (a cool look, a hard-to-reach spot, a juicy pickup effect). Directly spec-able for a custom loot item or "collect the 5 shards" quest.
- **"Give it a Pokedex" checklist (10 min, from the tools section)**: if a kid's mod has more than ~3 collectible items, require they design ONE simple in-game tracking aid (an advancement page listing what's found/missing, a compass-pointing item, or a chat message hint) — directly prevents the Purple Coins/Figments needle-in-haystack failure.
- **"Don't be Donkey Kong 64" gut-check**: before adding more collectible TYPES, ask whether each new type is actually distinct in purpose, or just padding (generic scope-control rule, but concretely anchored to a named bad example kids can look up).

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Minecraft already has natural collectible systems to bend: advancements (a checklist, like a mini-Pokedex), loot tables (rare drops), and structures (find X in the world). A kid's "collect these custom items scattered in structures/mob drops, track them via an advancement page" idea is directly buildable by Gemini using NeoForge's advancement + loot table systems — no new engine needed. A "radar/compass toward nearest collectible" tool (Spyro's dragonfly) is also buildable as a custom item with NBT-based nearest-block/entity search — a good stretch mechanic, moderately hard but plausible. The "final reward is a whole extra campaign" idea (Luigi's Galaxy) does not fit a mod-sized project; scale it down to "unlock a cosmetic skin, a secret room, or a bonus mob" instead.

### Doesn't transfer
The deep genre-specific failure analysis (Sonic Advance 2's rings clashing with its speed-focused movement, Crash 4's stage-size/backtracking math) is entertaining but not a lesson an 11-13-year-old needs beyond the one-line takeaway "match your collectible's difficulty to what your game is actually about" — no need to walk kids through the full case study.

## Honest caveats
Solid, un-padded video; the Extrinsic/Intrinsic split usefully echoes the same split used in the Level-Up-Systems video in this batch (see batch notes for the cross-video pattern) — both videos independently reach "extrinsic = reliable but shallow, intrinsic = deeper but harder to guarantee," so we should present that as one recurring idea across the whole progression-and-rewards theme, not two separate insights. One place the video slightly overclaims: it says Breath of the Wild's Korok seeds prove "you don't need everyone to finish the collection" — true, but that only works because BotW's world is otherwise satisfying to explore without the collectible; for a much smaller kid-made mod world, an under-supported collectible hunt is more likely to just feel empty, so this lesson needs the "give it a tracking tool" caveat to actually work at our scale.

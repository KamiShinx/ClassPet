# Customizing your Character: Lecture #6 (Brandon Sanderson, 75.5 min)

**What it is:** BYU in-person lecture (week 2 of "character"), heavy on live student Q&A. Covers "iconic" (non-arcing) characters, then the trio motivation/personality/values as the layer that presents a character to an audience, with a long back half of audience questions on villains, ensembles, dialogue, and writing outside your own experience.
**Substance:** mixed: real craft content in the first ~50 minutes (motivation-personality-values framework, iconic characters, villain motivation, distinguishing side characters), but roughly the last 25 minutes is student Q&A that drifts into adult-novel specifics (romance-novel pacing, indie publishing plug for next week's guest lecturers, "writing the other" with sensitivity readers, dialect/diction craft) with limited transfer value for our audience.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:08] "Iconic characters" (a term borrowed from Howard Taylor): characters written to *not* need a personal arc — James Bond, Dirk Pitt, Hercule Poirot — whose stories can be read out of order; their tension comes from external stakes ("is the villain too capable this time?") not personal growth.
- [0:06:01] Three-part presentation layer, distinct from last lecture's proactive/relatable/capable: **motivation** (what do they want), **personality** (how do they go about getting it), **values** (what will they sacrifice to get it).
- [0:07:10] Sanderson's own process: he outlines plot/world heavily but discovers character motivation while drafting, often rewriting outlines once the character is "found" (generic, his own workflow — not a rule for kids).
- [0:09:21] Suggested self-interrogation questions to find motivation: "what doesn't this character want people to know", "what failure will they never repeat", classic Hollywood set (what do they want / why can't they have it / what do they need / what must they sacrifice).
- [0:12:43] "Fundamental rules" metaphor: decide a few core things about a character early so any future decision about them can be answered consistently (directly parallels his own magic-system design advice — same underlying idea he applies to systems, applied to people).
- [0:14:56] Villain motivation trick: give a concrete, specific "why" instead of generic "wants to rule the world" (Pat Rothfuss quip cited); Magneto's backstory as the model of a specific, sympathetic why behind an extreme act.
- [0:16:05] Not every villain needs deep motivation — "iconic villains" (Sauron) can stay a faceless force IF the story also has a second, motivated antagonist figure (Gollum) to carry the relatable/explained side.
- [0:18:55] Viewpoint = instant empathy: showing any character (including a nonhuman/alien one) through their own eyes humanizes them fast, regardless of how alien their actions are, as long as the underlying motivation reads as relatable (cites Vernor Vinge's group-mind aliens).
- [0:22:37] Common alien/nonhuman design shortcut: take one human trait and dial it to 10 (Klingons = warriors, Vulcans = logic) (generic sci-fi-writing advice, flagged as historical pattern not an endorsement).
- [0:23:09] Distinguishing side/background characters: give each one visual signifier + a distinctive, recurring behavioural quirk the reader/viewer can latch onto before they know the character's depth; warns this reads as stereotyping at first, and should be deepened over time via a viewpoint scene or a "why are you like this" beat.
- [0:27:37] Rule of thumb on cast size: viewpoint count roughly multiplies story complexity/length; 1-2 viewpoints is much more manageable for less experienced writers than large ensembles (not directly relevant to a 65-minute class, but generic "start small" is reusable).
- [0:34:16] Key reframe: what makes a character "annoying" to an audience isn't the trait itself, it's a character who stalls the plot's progress; an annoying character can work if the story acknowledges the annoyance (has another character react to it) or if the trait pays off later.
- [0:36:33] "Love to hate" villains: work when they're competent/proactive but their cruelty stays just shy of unbearable; a character built purely "to hate" works best by attacking a hero's established weakness/limitation, not through generic cruelty.
- [0:38:15] Character arcs are best understood as **shifting motivations**: establish the starting want, show it as short-sighted/limited, and gradually replace it with a larger want the story is steering them toward (Kuzco wanting a palace -> wanting friends).
- [0:50:59] Personality = *how* a character pursues their motivation (aggressive vs. passive vs. silent), demonstrated through action and dialogue rather than stated; recommends starting a character as a legible archetype/stereotype, then adding nuance (explicitly likens "MBTI"-style typing quizzes to horoscopes — useful only as a shorthand, not science).
- [0:53:53] Dialogue exercise: write a 3-person scene with no dialogue tags or setting description and see if the reader can still tell who's talking from voice alone — a genuinely concrete craft drill.
- [0:55:30] Caution against lazy "smart character" signals (no contractions, big words) — real intelligent people organize thoughts with more structure/rhetoric, not vocabulary flexing (illustrated via his college roommate, Ken Jennings).
- [0:58:44] Quirks work best when they reinforce a character's core motivation/personality rather than being decorative (a bad-at-metaphors trait that "worked" vs. a star-rating quirk that "felt tacked on" in two of his own books, an honest self-critical example).
- [1:00:24] Cohen-brothers-style side-character trick: exaggerate one trait by roughly 10% rather than 100% (parody), so it reads as a "loving exaggeration" rather than caricature.
- [1:05:14] Ensemble-cast trick: contrast is the fastest way to differentiate a group — put characters in one shared situation and show how each responds differently (Fellowship of the Ring's reactions to the Ring).
- [1:10:08] Writing a background/identity you don't personally have: read primary sources (especially "what media gets wrong about X" pieces), use sensitivity/expert readers, and expect it to require real revision — treated as a serious responsibility, not a checkbox (adult-novelist concern, but the underlying "ask someone who actually knows" instinct is generically sound).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Mob motivation-personality-values card**: extend the three-slider card from lecture 5 with three one-line answers — "what does it want", "how does it go after it" (aggressive/sneaky/passive → maps to actual AI behaviour type), "what won't it give up" (a restriction it never breaks). From [0:06:01]. This is the actual spec sheet Gemini needs to write mob AI goal-selection code.
- **"One trait, one behaviour" villager/boss quirk worksheet**: kids pick exactly one recurring, observable tic (an item it always drops, a sound cue, a specific attack telegraph) and write why it reinforces the mob's motivation, not just because it's "cool" — directly from the David/Reckoners vs. Frugal Wizard comparison [0:58:44], explicitly framed as "quirk that reinforces motivation" vs. "quirk for quirk's sake" so kids can self-check their own design.
- **Ensemble contrast worksheet, for factions**: when a kid designs 3-4 related mobs/NPCs for one faction, put them all in one shared "situation" card (what does each of them do with the same trigger — e.g. the player enters their territory) and write one different reaction per mob. From the Fellowship/Ring contrast beat [1:05:14]; gives natural variety across a faction's mob roster without needing deep lore per mob.
- **Villain "why" one-liner**: before designing a boss, write one specific, concrete reason it does what it does (not "it's evil") — from the Magneto/Rothfuss beat [0:14:56]. Doesn't need to appear in-game; it's a design constraint that should shape 2-3 concrete boss behaviours (what it protects, what triggers its rage phase).

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Motivation as an actual **spec for mob AI**: "what does it want" cleanly becomes NeoForge goal-priority code (a zombie's want = "reach and attack player"; a custom villager-like mob's want = "keep its trade stock", etc.) — this is a case where the craft term and the technical spec are nearly identical, a stronger fit here than in most of the lecture.
- "Iconic vs arcing" character split [0:02:08] survives loosely: most mobs in a mod are iconic (no arc — a zombie is a zombie forever), and only a handful of "named" mini-boss characters would ever need an arc-like state change (e.g. changes behaviour/phase as it takes damage). Useful for the class to explicitly decide which mobs get "personality design" time and which don't, to avoid every kid over-speccing every single mob.
- "One trait dialed to 10%" (Cohen brothers) [1:00:24] survives as visual/behavioural design advice: exaggerate one silhouette feature or one behaviour trait rather than piling on several, which also happens to match the "silhouette-driven" Minecraft aesthetic named in CONTEXT.md.

### Doesn't transfer
- The entire personality/dialogue/viewpoint apparatus (MBTI-style typing, dialogue-tag-free scene exercises, diction/dialect craft, "in late, out early" scene structure, Cohen Brothers script analysis) is prose-writing craft for characters that speak and think on the page. A Minecraft mob has no dialogue and (mostly) no interiority — none of this converts into a game-design worksheet without heavy reinterpretation, and most of the reinterpretation would be our own invention, not Sanderson's.
- Ensemble cast-size math (1 viewpoint vs 2 vs 10, word counts, Stormlight's 400k words) [0:27:37] is novel-structure planning, irrelevant to mod design.
- The last ~15 minutes (romance-novel pacing, guest-lecturer plug, indie publishing, "writing the other" with paid sensitivity readers) is entirely adult-novelist/publishing-business content with no path into an 11-13 Minecraft class.
- "Signposting a betrayal" [1:06:56] and Sixth-Sense-style reveal structure require sustained narrative the class's mod lore likely won't build (needs a plotted story with pacing across sessions, not compatible with 20 weekly 65-minute sessions per CONTEXT.md).

## Honest caveats
- Much of this lecture (especially post-50-minute mark) is unstructured Q&A responding to whatever a specific student asked that week — it is not a curated "top ideas" lecture, so density of transferable material is lower than lecture 5's more structured presentation, despite being a similar length.
- Sanderson repeatedly flags his own techniques as personal preference/workflow ("this is my framework", "I don't know if I want to call them pseudoscience"), not universal rules — worth keeping that hedge in any class material we adapt.
- My own extrapolation: the "motivation = AI goal spec" mapping is the single most direct transfer point in this video, but Sanderson never discusses systems/mechanics at all — that link is entirely our inference, not something the lecture claims.

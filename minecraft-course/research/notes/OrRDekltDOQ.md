# First Prototype to Actual Game in 22 Days! (Jonas Tyroller, 9.9 min)

**What it is:** A solo/duo indie devlog (Jonas + Paul, making "Throne Fall") compressing ~50+ days of pre-production (art style search, naming, planning) and the first days of production into a 10-minute highlight reel.
**Substance:** mixed: genuinely shows real iteration (multiple failed art styles, an honest friend's harsh feedback, a messy production spreadsheet) but it's a devlog/wishlist ad first — narration is fast, casual, and skips *why* decisions were made almost as often as it states them.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:00] Art style gets decided FIRST, before code, because for a small/simple game visual distinctiveness is the main way to stand out (generic but stated as their actual priority order).
- [0:00:32] Rapid throwaway exploration: both devs independently mocked up scenes with store-bought assets in an afternoon just to "throw stuff at the wall and see what sticks" — cheap parallel experiments, not one person polishing one idea.
- [0:00:32]-[0:01:04] Iteration by overpainting a screenshot in Photoshop (not re-doing it in-engine) to quickly test if an idea ("ground the buildings with texture/fake AO/outlines") is worth building — cheap 2D mockup before committing engine time.
- [0:01:04] After the 2D mockup looks better, THEN port the change into the actual engine to confirm it's actually feasible/cheap to implement.
- [0:01:29] Show early work to an honest outside critic (a better artist friend) who bluntly said "this is a start, not finished" — explicit value of a critical outside eye over the team's own excitement.
- [0:01:36] Named house philosophy: "be cheap so you can be generous" — content must be fast/cheap to produce if you want a lot of it; art style choice is partly an economic decision, not just aesthetic.
- [0:02:07] Changing camera angle (top-down to isometric) was a "big leap" tested first as a 2D Photoshop overpaint comparison before touching the engine — same cheap-test-first pattern as the art style change.
- [0:02:40] Even after art + camera direction felt locked, they still built one small 3D block-out (river, cliff, castle) in the real engine before committing to full production — a validation step between concept art and production.
- [0:02:52]-[0:03:48] Naming brainstorm shown as a literal list of silly options (bullet list of names, several jokes) before landing on the real name — treated as its own separate task, not automatic.
- [0:03:48]-[0:04:00] "Gameplay check, art style check, vision for product check" — three named pre-production exit criteria shown on screen as a literal checklist before starting production (see frame [0:04:00]).
- [0:03:48] Explicit research step before production: "playing video games for an entire day" as deliberate research/inspiration gathering, framed as legitimate work.
- [0:04:00]-[0:04:12] A full production plan/calendar was built in one morning covering the next 2-3 months before writing any production code (visible as a colour-coded spreadsheet/calendar, frame [0:04:00]).
- [0:04:52] Build order in production: pathfinding/movement first, then combat (ranged then melee), then allied units, then the building/upgrade system (split so each dev owns a different system in parallel).
- [0:05:56] Reused code from an earlier prototype (enemy spawning system) copy-pasted into the new project with quick tweaks rather than rebuilt from scratch — prototypes aren't thrown away entirely, useful parts get salvaged.
- [0:05:56]-[0:06:28] The prototype's level layout was "boring and lacked personality"; deliberately made the real level's castle grow and interweave with the landscape instead of a generic square, because "things get more interesting the more specific they are" (a craft principle, stated directly, not generic filler).
- [0:06:28] "Block-out" defined on screen: fast, ugly placeholder geometry used just to nail scale/proportions (wall/tower thickness) before any real art pass.
- [0:07:00] Play-testing surfaced a specific balance complaint from a co-dev ("this back line feels unprotected") which they fixed with a simple readability trick — a wall of mountains, so the "safe direction" reads instantly without new mechanics.
- [0:07:34] Kept a running master bug/feature list and worked through it incrementally ("checking things off every day") rather than trying to fix everything at once.
- [0:07:34]-[0:08:06] Explicit, deliberate combat simplification: removed a ranged auto-attack (felt too passive, "Vampire Survivors" style) and replaced with a manual melee attack button — chose "stupidly simple" combat on purpose so skill floor is near zero and "almost anybody can play."
- [0:08:06] Enemy variety created by combining 2-3 simple stat/behavior axes (fast+chase-only, flying+melee-immune-ish, siege-vs-buildings, tanky melee) rather than unique mechanics per enemy — a cheap way to generate apparent variety.
- [0:08:38] Balancing pass added a new enemy type specifically to make an existing system (walls) matter more — game systems get iterated based on what playtesting shows is currently pointless.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Ugly box test" worksheet (15-20 min)**: before touching Blockbench, kid draws or arranges plain colored cubes/rectangles to test if their mob's silhouette and scale work in the world — mirrors the grey-box/block-out step at [0:06:28]. Ends with a rough shape they either commit to or scrap.
- **Three-check gate before "production"**: a literal checklist a kid must tick before they're allowed to start their multi-week build: "1) I know what my mob/item DOES, 2) I know what it looks like, 3) I can say my mod idea in one sentence." Comes directly from the on-screen checklist at [0:04:00].
- **Name brainstorm sheet (10 min)**: list 8-10 silly/serious name options for their mob/mod, cross out bad ones together as a class, pick one and move on — comes from [0:02:52]-[0:03:48]. Useful as a light, low-stakes warm-up activity, not a big lesson.
- **"Show a friend" critique round**: pair kids up mid-project to give blunt "is this finished or just started?" feedback before they think they're done — comes from [0:01:29]. Needs a light script/sentence starters so 11-13-year-olds give useful (not just "cool!") feedback.
- **Mini production-plan calendar**: each kid sketches which 3-4 weeks they'll spend on what (design week, Blockbench week, texture week, Gemini/testing week) — a simplified, kid-sized version of the spreadsheet at [0:04:00].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The block-out/grey-box idea survives almost perfectly and matters MORE for Minecraft: Blockbench IS a block-out tool by nature (cubes first, detail/texture later), so "get scale and silhouette right with plain cubes before you texture" is a native workflow, not an adaptation. "Be cheap so you can be generous" maps directly onto the class's reality: 8 kids, 65 min/week — reusing/duplicating simple mob templates (their own version of "hunterlings," "flying enemies," "tanky melee") to get apparent variety cheaply is exactly the kind of shortcut this class needs to keep scope real. "Combine 2-3 simple traits for enemy variety" is a genuinely good, concrete worksheet for designing a small family of mobs instead of one over-engineered boss. The "readability fix over new mechanic" idea (wall of mountains instead of a new system) is a good design lesson: prefer fixing player confusion with layout/visual clarity before adding new code for Gemini to build.

### Doesn't transfer
The parallel two-person division of labor (one dev on units, one on buildings, simultaneously) doesn't fit a solo 11-13-year-old working alone on their own mod — the "cheap parallel experiments" pattern needs to be redesigned as sequential steps for a single kid, or done as a paired/group activity instead. The Steam wishlist / commercial framing (name-brand-building, algorithm strategy) is irrelevant — there's no publishing goal here. The full 2-3 month, spreadsheet-driven production plan is real studio project management; needs heavy simplification to survive a 20-week, once-a-week class (a wall calendar with 4-5 blocks, not a granular Gantt chart).

## Honest caveats
This is a compressed highlight reel, not a tutorial — it never explains HOW they decided an art style was good (just "we knew"), so a lot of the actual judgment/decision-making is invisible, only the artifacts of it are shown. The "22 days" title is misleading: the video itself says pre-production alone took ~50 days before "day 1" of production even started, so the real timeline shown is closer to 70+ days total, not 22 — worth flagging if Ben repeats the number. Everything about combat/balance is explicitly "stupidly simple by design" for THIS specific low-skill-floor game; it's a reasonable model for what an 11-13-year-old's first mod mechanic should look like (simple, forgiving) but shouldn't be oversold as universal game-design wisdom.

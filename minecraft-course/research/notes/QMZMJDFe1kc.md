# FFX VS FFXIII - Two Linear Games, Two Outcomes (Design Doc, 15.7 min)

**What it is:** Comparative video essay on why FFX's linear story/levels feel fine while FFXIII's feel oppressive: FFX hides its linearity behind pacing, minigames, NPC "pit stops" and hidden items; FFXIII's levels are long corridors with no detours, and its Crystarium (leveling) punishes branching out.
**Substance:** thin for this class: it's a level-design and leveling-system comparison built entirely around two turn-based JRPGs' fixed story maps — a genre and structure Minecraft (open-world, player-built) doesn't share. One idea (give players fake or real choice within a linear structure) is generic enough to be worth a one-line mention.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [7:52] FFX hides its linear maps with 4 tricks: fast pacing (short 2-3 screen areas), level gimmicks/minigames, "pit stop" NPCs with optional side info, and hidden items — generic level-design craft for scripted corridors.
- [9:27] FFXIII's levels lack all four of those, so its linearity reads as "fancy hallways"; treasure is always guarded and obvious rather than hidden, killing the sense of exploration.
- [11:34] FFX's Sphere Grid is a branching skill-tree/board hybrid: characters start on their own path but CAN cross into other characters' branches, giving player agency even though the story is linear.
- [12:37] FFXIII's Crystarium is a near-straight line per combat role, and jumping to a different role/branch costs 5x the points — the game "punishes" experimentation, which the video ties directly to why players feel the whole game is restrictive.
- [14:15] Overall thesis: "linear" isn't one thing — it's environmental design, pacing, story delivery (guided narration vs. buried datalogs), AND leveling freedom, and a game can be linear in the map sense while still feeling generous in the other three.

## What the frames add
Direct visual contrast is the whole point of the video (title cards alternate FFX/FFXIII footage in a pink/orange split-screen theme throughout) but it's mostly gameplay b-roll illustrating what's already narrated — corridors vs. detour rooms, the two skill-tree/grid UIs (Sphere Grid at [11:34], Crystarium at [13:00]) shown side by side, which does make the "board with branches" vs "straight line with branches on branches" contrast legible faster than the narration alone.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
Nothing worth building a standalone activity around — this is level-design theory for a fixed, scripted map, which doesn't exist in a kid's Minecraft world (the world itself is open and player-explored; there's no author-controlled "corridor" to hide).

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The Sphere-Grid-vs-Crystarium contrast (branching, optional-detour skill trees feel better than forced-linear ones) is a real progression-system point, but it duplicates ground the teacher's earlier progression/rewards batch (N) almost certainly already covers with a cleaner example — not worth re-deriving here.

### Doesn't transfer
Everything about hiding a fixed story's linearity (pit-stop NPCs, minigame detours mid-corridor, guarded vs hidden treasure placement in an author-built dungeon) assumes a designer-authored critical path. A kid's Minecraft mod adds systems to an open world the player already roams freely — there's no "corridor" to disguise, so the entire central comparison doesn't apply.

## Honest caveats
This is one of the lowest-relevance videos in the batch: a genre-specific comparison of two console JRPGs' fixed-camera dungeon design and menu-based leveling, for a class where students add mechanics to an open sandbox that has neither. Flagging fast rather than stretching for relevance, per the brief.

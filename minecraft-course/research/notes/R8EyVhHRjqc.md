# How Do You Make Fun Unlockables? (Design Doc, 24.0 min)

**What it is:** A two-part breakdown of unlock systems: WHY games use unlocks (sequencing, easing complexity, gating, disguised progression, giving concrete goals) and HOW to build one (Criteria x Prize, mainly Accumulate vs Achieve), closing with a deep dive on the evolution of Smash Bros' unlock design across 6 games.
**Substance:** substantive: the Criteria/Prize framework and the Accumulate/Achieve split are genuinely reusable design tools, backed by many concrete examples; one sponsor read.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:51] Unlock systems' most basic job: sequencing — force players through content in a designer-chosen order (tutorial before main mode, weapon before its upgrade). (generic but foundational)
- [0:02:56] Unlocks ease new players into complexity by adding options one at a time instead of dumping everything at once (WarioWare Get It Together adds its 20 unique characters one per stage).
- [0:04:31] Unlocks can gate content inappropriate for new players (Devil May Cry's brutal "Heaven or Hell"/"Hell and Hell" difficulties are late unlocks, functioning as both reward and built-in skill check).
- [0:05:03] Unlocks can impersonate a skill tree/leveling system: many small optional unlocks (Psychonauts 2's ability points from collectibles) give a sense of progress without being mandatory content-gates.
- [0:06:36] Unlocks can be pure signposts of a game's skill ceiling: an achievement for "beat it in 2 hours" or "rocket jump" implies that's possible and nudges players to investigate systems they might not have found alone. Sometimes the unlock itself barely matters — the existence of the goal is the real prize.
- [0:07:40] **Core framework**: every unlock = Criteria (what you do) + Prize (what you get); the art is harmonizing the two, not picking either in isolation.
- [0:08:46] Two main criteria styles: **Accumulate** (gather X over time — easy to design, easy to make grindy) vs **Achieve** (one self-contained task, done).
- [0:09:51] Accumulate criteria pair best with prizes that unlock MORE content, and should be spread across multiple small tiers, not saved for one big payoff at the very end (Streets of Rage 4's meter-based character unlocks; battle passes).
- [0:10:55] Accumulate criteria fail when the player has no way to discover them naturally (Sonic Mega Collection: booting specific games 20-30 times with zero in-game hint = players look it up and grind mindlessly — not satisfying, not a real challenge).
- [0:12:02] When you have too many small unlocks to hand-pair with specific criteria, add an intermediate currency (points/money you spend on whichever unlock you want) instead of matching every prize to its own criteria.
- [0:13:06] Achieve criteria are a great place for genuine bonus content (Super Mario World's hidden Star Road/Special World) — works when partially-hidden exits are findable through normal exploration, not needle-in-haystack.
- [0:14:50] Watch for the "certificate, not coupon" trap: rewards for the hardest Achieve unlocks are often USELESS because you already had to be strong enough to beat the hardest challenge to get them (FF7's Ruby Weapon reward, FF8's "Proof of Omega"). Better: reward hardest unlocks with pure bragging-rights (a crown, a title) or with game-breaking joke content (cheat codes, big-head mode) since by that point nothing can "unbalance" a player who already mastered everything.
- [0:16:54] Case study across 6 Smash Bros games: unlock design should signal early that secrets exist (silhouettes, fuzzed-out character select), spread unlocks across early play so new players find something quickly, and offer backup/accumulative criteria alongside harder achieve criteria so "everyone eventually gets everything" [0:19:36]—[0:20:12] even if some criteria are missed.
- [0:21:44] Warning example: Smash 3DS/Wii U's random-drop "Custom Moves" (376 of them, no player control over which you get) is called out as an unsatisfying gacha-style unlock — lack of player agency over the outcome undercuts the system even though it's technically "more content."

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Criteria + Prize" unlock-design worksheet (20-25 min)**: for any custom item/mob/structure a kid wants to gate behind progress, have them fill in two boxes — Criteria (Accumulate or Achieve — pick one) and Prize — then answer "does the prize match the effort?" using the certificate-vs-coupon test [0:14:50]. Directly spec-able and this is the single most reusable framework in the batch.
- **"Signal the secret" tooltip (5 min, from the Smash case study)**: before finalizing a hidden unlock, ask "how will the player even know this exists?" (a locked recipe book entry, a silhouette, an advancement hint) — prevents the Sonic Mega Collection mistake of a completely undiscoverable unlock.
- **"Spread it out" review**: when a kid plans multiple unlocks for one system (e.g. 5 tiers of a custom weapon), check they're not all dumped at the finish line — put at least one meaningful unlock early.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Minecraft's advancement system is already a real, working unlock engine (Achieve-criteria unlocks: "do X, get recipe/trigger/message") and vanilla loot tables/trading are Accumulate-criteria unlocks (gather emeralds, get better trades). A kid's mod bends this directly: custom advancements that unlock a crafting recipe, a structure loot table, or a villager trade tier are squarely buildable by Gemini (advancement JSON triggers + reward functions), no new system needed. The Criteria+Prize worksheet maps onto this cleanly. What does NOT map without new work: an in-game currency-to-spend-on-any-unlock system (the "too many small prizes, use one currency" idea) — Minecraft doesn't have a generic "unlock points" system, so this would need custom scoreboard/NBT tracking, which is a bigger ask for NeoForge but plausible as a stretch mechanic.

### Doesn't transfer
The deep Smash Bros historical unlock-design comparison (6 games, silhouette teasers, gacha custom moves) is genre trivia more than a transferable lesson beyond the 3-4 bullet points above — no need to relitigate the whole case study with kids. The FF7/FF8 "reward is a certificate not a coupon" nuance is a fun idea but too abstract for a one-page spec; fold it into the simpler "does the prize match the effort" checklist instead.

## Honest caveats
This is the strongest single framework in the batch (Criteria x Prize) and should be the backbone of any "design your mod's rewards" worksheet — flag it as a top pick in the batch synthesis. One overreach: the video claims coercive "do X to unlock the rest of the game" unlocks are "not a great long-term strategy" [implied ~0:01:51] without much nuance — Minecraft's own advancement-gated recipes (already in vanilla) are exactly this pattern and work fine at small scale, so this caution matters more for a kid designing a whole game than for a single gated recipe. The Smash catalogue, while long, ultimately repeats the same Accumulate/Achieve/currency ideas already established earlier — the batch note should credit this video for the framework, not for volume of examples.

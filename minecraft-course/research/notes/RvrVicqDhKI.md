# How Do You Design a Cast of Enemies? (Design Doc, 17 min)

**What it is:** explains "bestiary design" (the full roster of enemies in a game) around one core idea, "novelty over time" - keeping enemies visually and mechanically fresh across a whole playthrough - with case studies of what works (Halo, Pikmin) and what falls flat (Final Fantasy X/XII palette swaps, Breath of the Wild's repetitive common enemies, Paper Mario: The Origami King's mechanically-identical enemies).

**Substance:** substantive: one real organizing idea (novelty over time, split into visual novelty and mechanical novelty) applied consistently across seven+ games, including honest criticism of beloved games (Breath of the Wild, Origami King) rather than just praise. One sponsor read (Skillshare), clearly separated.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:13] Framing: a "bestiary" is the enemy roster; it needs to be deliberately crafted and balanced start-to-finish, not just filled with whatever monster ideas came to mind.
- [0:01:54] Core thesis: "novelty over time" - each enemy should feel new/exciting, AND the game needs to keep delivering that freshness across its entire length, not just in the first hour.
- [0:02:28] Visual design matters on its own: striking, varied enemy silhouettes and designs pull players forward into new areas ("what am I going to fight next?").
- [0:02:59] Dragon Quest example: turn-based combat (abstracted into stat blocks/menus) frees the artists to make wildly goofy/varied monster designs, because the fight's internal logic doesn't have to visually "make sense" the way an action game's does.
- [0:03:33] Negative example: Final Fantasy X and XII reuse the same handful of enemy models/animations with palette swaps and different stats across a dozen "different" monsters - named as a real cost-cutting shortcut that visibly hurts the feeling of discovery ("how many different wolves did you have to deal with?").
- [0:04:40] Enemy design can carry narrative/lore even without exposition: Halo's Covenant enemies (Grunts/Elites/Brutes/Hunters) have distinct silhouettes AND a hierarchy that mirrors the game's backstory, readable purely from how they look and act.
- [0:05:43] Harder case for realistic shooters (Call of Duty, Uncharted): human enemies in similar uniforms tend to visually blur together; designers have to work harder (crazy gear, unique weapons) to make any one enemy stand out.
- [0:06:15] Second axis, separate from visuals: MECHANICAL novelty - new AI behaviors, new interactions, new required tactics - can deliver "novelty over time" just as well as new art.
- [0:06:48] Strong example: Pikmin's bestiary ties directly into the game's core resource-management mechanic - each enemy demands a different tactic (ambush a sleeping Bulborb, use elemental-immune Pikmin, throw Pikmin to release stolen items from Breadbugs) - enemies aren't just reskinned damage-sponges, each one teaches or tests a specific system.
- [0:09:25] Negative example, and the most useful caveat in the video: Breath of the Wild has roughly as many enemy types as other 3D Zeldas, but FEELS smaller because the same 3 common enemies (Bokoblin/Moblin/Lizalfos) appear everywhere with only minor color/stat variation, so most fights fall into one memorized pattern - depth exists in the systems but isn't surfaced by the actual enemy placement/behavior.
- [0:12:12] Key distinction, stated directly: "reskinned mechanics aren't new mechanics" - Paper Mario: The Origami King's enemies look varied (nice paper-craft designs) but are functionally near-identical (same 1-2 hit pattern regardless of type), so the game's novelty runs out fast even though its BOSS fights (each with unique rules) remain great - visual variety without mechanical variety is a trap.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"What does fighting this teach?" one-liner (5-10 min per mob):** for each custom mob a kid designs, they write one sentence: what specific thing does the player have to DO differently to beat this mob compared to a vanilla zombie/skeleton? If they can't answer, the mob is a reskin, not a new enemy (directly from the "reskinned mechanics aren't new mechanics" idea).
- **Silhouette/look pass (10 min, pairs well with the worldbuilding/character-design half of the course):** before texturing a mob in Blockbench, kids check it's recognizable in silhouette alone (black shape, no color) - reused from the Halo point about readable-from-a-distance enemy shapes.
- **Class bestiary board (ongoing, low-effort):** as kids finish mobs across the 20 weeks, keep a shared list/board of "what does each mob require the player to do" - visually shows the class whether their COLLECTIVE mod is falling into the Breath-of-the-Wild trap (everyone's mob plays the same) or has real variety. Useful for Ben as a teacher check, not just the kids.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The mechanical-novelty half survives very well and is squarely within NeoForge's reach for one custom mob at a time: giving a mob one distinct required counter-tactic (weak to a specific tool/effect, has a tell before an attack, flees at low health, summons help) is realistic scope for Gemini to implement and for an 11-13-year-old to design on paper first. The visual-novelty half (palette swaps vs. unique designs) is really a budget/art-pipeline problem for studios shipping dozens of enemies - with 8 kids making ~1-2 mobs each over 20 weeks, the class will naturally avoid the FFX palette-swap trap simply by not having enough mobs to reuse; this half of the video is more relevant to Ben as a "don't let the shared asset library become a wall of the same reskinned model" planning note than to any single kid's worksheet.

### Doesn't transfer
The cross-game, whole-bestiary analysis (is this 15-hour RPG's ENTIRE roster of 30+ enemies novel across the whole runtime) doesn't apply at the scale of one kid's mod, which might have 1-3 custom mobs total. "Novelty over time" as literally stated needs a large cast and a long game to even be a problem - reframe it for class as "does my one mob require something different from the player," not "is my whole game's enemy roster varied over 20 hours."

## Honest caveats
Like the other Design Doc video in this batch, this is critique/analysis of finished AAA games rather than a build-from-scratch tutorial - genuinely useful for teaching kids to LOOK at a mob design and judge it, less useful as a direct "how to build mechanical variety" instruction manual. The video's Breath of the Wild critique is a minority/contrarian take (it's a beloved, critically acclaimed game) presented as the presenter's own read ("at least it seems that way to me," his words) rather than a settled fact - worth keeping that hedge when reusing the example with kids, since some of them will likely disagree and that's a fine, healthy design discussion to have.

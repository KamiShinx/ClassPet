# How Level Design Can Tell a Story (Game Maker's Toolkit, 18.2 min)

**What it is:** A craft essay (Mark Brown, narrated over dozens of AAA game clips) on three ways environment design communicates story without dialogue/cutscenes: understanding (environmental storytelling), feeling (emotion via space/color), and identity (what the space tells you about who you are in it).
**Substance:** substantive: a genuinely structured framework (a three-level pyramid: environmental storytelling / level design / world building) backed by named GDC talks and named designers, not just a clip reel of vibes — though it's built entirely from AAA console/PC examples with no discussion of how an amateur or a blocky game would pull any of it off.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:04]-[0:01:12] Framing thesis: Bioshock's entire backstory (utopia collapsed into civil war on a fixed date) is legible from the environment alone, with zero cutscenes or dialogue needed.
- [0:01:44] "Environmental storytelling" defined and sourced: set-dressing that creates small, optional, self-contained narrative vignettes (a term coined by ex-Disneyland designer Don Carson, popularized by a GDC talk).
- [0:02:19] Named benefit: environmental storytelling makes the player an active investigator (deductive reasoning, connecting details) rather than a passive audience — and it's skippable if a player just wants the "shooty stuff."
- [0:02:52] Environmental storytelling isn't only static objects — it extends to overheard dialogue, environment animations, and text (books/notes/emails/item descriptions).
- [0:03:26] Environmental storytelling can also show the PLAYER'S impact after the fact (kill a shopkeeper in Deus Ex, the shop becomes a permanently sealed crime scene later) — environment as consequence, not just backstory.
- [0:03:26] Environmental storytelling has gameplay functions too, not just narrative: object placement can hint at mechanics (a saw blade near a corpse suggests a weapon use) or warn of danger (a fried enemy on a fence).
- [0:03:58]-[0:06:06] The core framework: a three-level pyramid — HIGH = environmental storytelling (micro vignettes), MEDIUM = level design (individual rooms/places: architecture, layout, scale reveal who uses a space, e.g. Dishonored 2's verticality literally placing the working class beneath the powerful), LOW = world building (factions, history, major plot points) that the other two levels are built from.
- [0:05:33] Practical craft note from IO Interactive: they aim for spaces that are "credible" (meets basic expectations for how the space works) rather than perfectly logically simulated (doesn't need real bedroom/bathroom counts for hundreds of NPCs) — a deliberately lowered bar for believability.
- [0:06:06]-[0:07:12] The three pyramid levels should "echo" the same theme up and down the stack (Deus Ex: Mankind Divided example — low-level theme of anti-aug oppression shows up in mid-level train-car segregation AND high-level graffiti/emails); flagged as genuinely hard in practice because different levels are often built by different people on a large team, needing deliberate cross-team alignment.
- [0:07:43] Second use of environment: EMOTION, not just information — scale/shape/color evoke feelings directly (Naughty Dog designer quote: tightening corridors and low ceilings to build tension before releasing into an open vista for triumph).
- [0:08:17]-[0:09:22] Concrete emotion-through-space examples: God of War narrows the path, adds dead ends/backtracking and fog to build panic, then opens up and lifts fog when the danger resolves; Portal shifts from sterile white test-chamber geometry to warm orange "lived-in" maintenance spaces to mark the story's tonal break.
- [0:09:57] Tomb Raider reboot: character starts low with environment "bearing down" in dark green, gradually rises to warm orange sunlight as the story arcs from despair to hope — a full level's color/verticality arc mapped to one character's emotional journey.
- [0:10:29]-[0:12:06] Concrete production TOOL from a BioWare GDC talk: "Emotion Charts" — break a level into narrative sections, decide the theme + target emotion for each section, then choose environment details (vehicles/calm Krogan = confidence; explosions = chaos; statues/murals = mystery; open city vista after dark catacombs = awe) to evoke it.
- [0:12:06] Named caveat: the right environmental trick depends on genre/mechanics context — darkness reads as fear in horror but as safety/power in a stealth game; the same visual choice can mean opposite things.
- [0:12:40]-[0:13:12] Second BioWare tool: "Intensity Charts" — plot a target intensity curve per section, then playtest and compare actual player-reported intensity against the target; when a torch-lit catacomb fight tested as too intense, they cut the fight entirely to protect the intended "mystery" beat, and added a hope-boosting cutscene elsewhere to hit the target curve.
- [0:13:12] Named target shape: a classic three-act intensity curve (rising action, low despair point, climb to victory) — explicitly the design target BioWare charts against.
- [0:13:49] Celeste's literal level topology mirrors the emotional graph — you climb a mountain with small setbacks, and the story's single lowest emotional beat (the "Reflections" level) is also the level's literal lowest point.
- [0:14:23] Journey uses color coding per zone to carry emotion with zero text: orange (calm mystery), dark green (spooky), white (cold), bright blue (rebirth) — cited as the strongest pure example in the video.
- [0:14:58]-[0:15:29] Third use of environment: IDENTITY — what actions feel "in character." Comparison: stealing/killing felt natural in Bioshock's ruined, insane-splicer-filled Rapture, but felt wrong in Bioshock Infinite's still-functioning, populated Columbia — same mechanics, opposite moral affordance, purely from environmental context.
- [0:16:01]-[0:16:31] Hitman's designed "rules of behaviour": IO splits spaces into public (open, any costume) vs. private (locked down, needs a costume/ingenuity), each further graded from vague to strict social rules — players read real-world social cues to know where they're "allowed" to be, and the best levels mix area types richly.

The pyramid diagram itself appears three times as an actual graphic ([0:04:12] single triangle "environmental storytelling," [0:04:36] two-tier "high/medium," [0:06:00] full three-tier "high/medium/low = environmental storytelling/level design/world building") — this is the single most useful visual for building a class handout, since it's a literal ready-made diagram. [0:16:36] shows the "public"/"private" split as a literal blue-vs-red-tinted side-by-side screenshot from Hitman, a strong visual aid for that idea. [0:12:48]-[0:13:36] shows the actual BioWare bar-chart "Intensity Chart" (planned vs. actual intensity per level section) and the resulting smooth target curve graphic — concrete proof that professional level design math can be genuinely simple (a bar chart, a line graph) and worth mimicking as a class worksheet. ## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Silent story test**: kid builds one small Minecraft structure (a room, a ruin, a camp) and a partner has to guess "what happened here?" using only what they see — no text, no talking. Direct application of the "vignette" idea at [0:01:44]. 15-20 min, produces a testable, concrete build.
- **Three-level echo worksheet**: kid fills in one sentence per pyramid level for their mod's world — "big idea" (low/world building), "what one place looks like" (medium/level design), "one small detail/object" (high/environmental storytelling) — and checks the three actually agree. Directly from [0:06:06]-[0:07:12], simplified into a fill-in-the-blank sheet a teacher can grade in a glance.
- **Mood-through-blocks mini-challenge (20 min)**: give kids a fixed small area and ask them to make it feel "scary" or "safe" using ONLY block choice, lighting, and space shape — no mobs, no text. Comes from [0:07:43]-[0:09:22]; Minecraft's native light levels and block palette make this directly buildable, unlike most of this video's AAA examples.
- **Public/private space sort**: for a kid's structure (a village, a dungeon, a base), have them mark which rooms are "anyone can walk in" vs. "you need a key/quest to get here," then check the build actually signals that visually (locked doors, guarded entries, decor quality). From [0:16:01]-[0:16:31] — good, concrete, and re-usable across many kids' projects.
- **One-line "credible not perfect" rule**: teach IO Interactive's own stated bar ([0:05:33]) directly as a scope-management tooltip — kids don't need to build a full working kitchen/bathroom for every house, just enough detail to read as believable. Useful complement to the scope-discipline ideas from the other two GMTK videos in this batch.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The core three-level pyramid transfers almost perfectly to a Minecraft mod's actual scope (world lore -> a structure/biome -> a placed detail/loot item) and is a genuinely better worldbuilding organizer for this class than free-form lore writing, since it forces ideas down into a buildable object at the "high" tier. Environmental storytelling via block/light/color choice is Minecraft's native strength — arguably transfers BETTER here than in the source AAA games, since Minecraft already has a build-first, texture-second workflow. The "credible not perfect" bar is an important scope-protection idea for a time-constrained class. Emotion/Intensity Charts are a real, usable planning tool but need heavy simplification — a full bar-chart intensity curve is too abstract for most 11-13-year-olds; a simpler 3-step version ("calm start, scary middle, safe/exciting end") is more realistic.

### Doesn't transfer
Nothing here is about 3D modelling, mob design, or Blockbench at all — this video is 100% level/space design, zero character/asset design, so it does not address Ben's "3D design" worry directly; it only supports the "worldbuilding" and "game design" side. The specific AAA techniques (verticality signaling class in Dishonored 2, camera-driven pacing tricks in God of War, cinematic color grading) rely on lighting/rendering fidelity and camera craft Minecraft and an 11-13-year-old modder don't have — the PRINCIPLE (space shapes feeling) survives, the specific execution (fog, grading, camera framing) does not. The professional cross-team alignment problem ([0:07:12]) is irrelevant to a solo kid's project. The moral-affordance idea (identity via environment, Bioshock vs Infinite) is thematically interesting but relies on mature content (violence, moral ambiguity) that isn't appropriate or buildable for this age group's mod — worth noting as one to mention but not build an activity around.

## Honest caveats
This is a well-sourced clip essay (named GDC talks, named designers quoted directly) rather than the presenter's own untested theory, so the underlying craft claims are more trustworthy than average for this batch — but it never once addresses low-budget, blocky, or amateur-scale execution; every example assumes photorealistic or near-photorealistic rendering, lighting, and camera control the class's tools don't have. The video was flagged for its own violent examples ([0:17:32], the creator's own on-screen apology/content note) — several source clips (gore, corpses, jump-scares) are not appropriate to show an 11-13 audience directly; use the FRAMEWORK, not the reference images, when building class material. No contradiction with the other two GMTK/Jonas Tyroller videos in this batch; this one operates at a different layer (craft of WHAT to build) while the other two operate at scope/process (HOW MUCH to build and in what order) — complementary, not overlapping.

# Story Structures - Plot Theory: Brandon Sanderson's Writing Lecture #3 (Brandon Sanderson, 77 min)

**What it is:** A survey of named plot-structure tools: Hero's Journey/Story Circle, Yes-but/No-and, Try-Fail Cycle ("Points on the Map"), plot-hole handling ("gorilla in the phone booth"), Three-Act Structure/dramatic question, and building your own "Structure Model" from a favorite subgenre.
**Substance:** substantive: dense, example-driven, and the most directly reusable lecture in the batch for building worksheets, because each structure is a distinct, nameable tool rather than general philosophy.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:03:18] "The king died" is a fact; "the king died and the queen died of grief" is a story — struggle/reaction is what makes events into story, not the sequence of facts (generic but a good one-line test).
- [0:04:56] Test for any obstacle you add: it should **force growth, reinforce something learned, or show failure/lack of readiness** in the character or plot. If it does none of those, it's just an event, not plot.
- [0:07:05] **Story Circle (Dan Harmon's simplified Hero's Journey)**: comfort/stasis -> resistance to a call -> forced "crossing the threshold" -> road of trials (learn you lack skill) -> confrontation with a mentor/"divinity" figure -> transformation/reward -> return home changed. Explicitly a circle: the payoff is the character able to do at the end what they couldn't at the start.
- [0:10:29] Cautionary tale: George Lucas loved the Hero's Journey so much he treated it as a checklist (adding the "virgin birth" beat via Shmi Skywalker in the prequels) — a warning against using any structure as a rigid recipe rather than a diagnostic tool ("chef vs cook" again).
- [0:24:03] Structures can nest/repeat across a series: Star Wars original trilogy is one big Hero's Journey cycle (Luke becomes a Jedi) built from smaller ones (accept the call in film 1, fail and try again in film 2, succeed in film 3).
- [0:26:50] Tropes are unavoidable and not inherently bad; obsessing over avoiding them is unproductive — execution and specific character/setting detail is what makes a familiar structure feel fresh (echoes lecture 2's Hamlet/Lion King/Dune point).
- [0:30:39] **Yes-but/No-and**: at every plot beat, ask "does the character solve this problem?" Answer "yes, but [complication]" or "no, and [worse complication]" to keep escalating; best suited to shorter, fast-paced stories (his example: Star Wars' entire A-plot chain from "I need a droid" to "blow up the Death Star").
- [0:36:34] **Try-Fail Cycle / "Points on the Map"** (how Robert Jordan discovery-wrote The Wheel of Time): outline only a handful of major waypoints/events you know you're heading toward, then discovery-write the connective tissue between them; before a character succeeds at something hard, they should genuinely try and fail (ideally using a smart approach) at least once first.
- [0:44:56] **"Gorillas in the phone booth" (plot holes)**: an unexplained, attention-grabbing oddity that yanks readers out of the story if placed carelessly. Fix options: don't include it, explain it in one line, or "hang a lantern on it" (have a character acknowledge the oddity so the reader relaxes about it). Most "plot hole" complaints (he singles out CinemaSins) are answerable in one sentence and weren't actually a problem for readers in the moment.
- [0:54:55] **Three-Act Structure** (Hollywood formula, effectively 4 beats): Act I sets up character/need/status quo and asks the **major dramatic question**; an Inciting Incident starts a cascade; Act I ends with "life can never be the same" (Plot Point 1). Act II is "rising action" (things keep getting worse) with a **Midpoint** that reframes the story, escalates, or makes it personal; leads to an "All is Lost" low point. Act III is the final, no-second-chances attempt and the climax.
- [0:57:10] Quoting Avengers director Joe Russo: the single most useful tool he uses is identifying the story's **major dramatic question** up front ("will X succeed at Y?").
- [1:10:28] Learning-curve analogy (from a Magic: The Gathering streamer): a beginner can only hold one skill layer in mind at once; structural/dramatic-question thinking is something to add consciously only once basic habits (showing up, finishing things) are automatic — don't overload beginners with all these frameworks at once.
- [1:12:35] **"Structure Model"**: instead of a generic framework, study a specific beloved subgenre in depth (e.g. underdog-sports-team stories like Hoosiers/Cool Runnings/Pitch Perfect), extract what specifically makes its structure work, and transplant that structure into a new genre/setting. Sanderson used this to build the Bridge Crew arc in The Way of Kings.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Try-fail before try-win" build rule**: before a kid lets a mod boss/puzzle be beatable on the first try, they design one obvious failed attempt the player (or an NPC) makes first — teaches escalation and stakes without needing prose. Fits directly into quest/boss design worksheets, 15-20 min.
- **"Points on the map" mod outline**: kids pick 3-5 fixed "waypoints" for their world/quest chain (a starting village, a landmark structure, a boss room, an ending reward) before building, then fill in the connective content between them — a lightweight outline method suited to kids who don't want to plan everything up front. Good fit for a whole-course worldbuilding worksheet.
- **Gorilla-in-the-phone-booth check for mods**: a build-review checklist question — "is there anything in your world/mechanic that looks totally unexplained and would make a player stop and go 'wait, what?'" Either cut it, add one sign/NPC line explaining it, or lean into weirdness on purpose (hang a lantern). Concrete, fast (5 min), and teaches the same judgment call a professional designer makes.
- **"Major dramatic question" one-liner**: require every kid's mod/world pitch to answer one sentence: "what question is the player trying to answer by playing this?" ("Can I escape the flooding temple before the water god wakes?"). Cheap diagnostic for whether a mod idea has a spine.
- **Structure Model borrow-and-reskin exercise**: kids pick a favorite game/movie/book plot they love, list 4-5 beats that make it work, then reskin those same beats into their Minecraft mod's structure. Directly reuses Sanderson's own Way-of-Kings method and is very natural for 11-13-year-olds who already have favorite media.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The Try-Fail Cycle, Yes-but/No-and escalation, and "points on the map" outlining all map well onto quest/dungeon design — they're structural tools about sequencing challenges and raising stakes, which is exactly what a quest chain or boss encounter needs, independent of whether the "text" is prose or gameplay. The Three-Act Structure's beats (inciting incident, midpoint reframe, all-is-lost, climax) also translate reasonably to a mod's main quest arc. The Hero's Journey/Story Circle is the shakiest of these for a game: it describes an internal, growth-of-character arc that's easy to write in prose but hard for a kid's mod to actually deliver via items/mobs/mechanics (a mob doesn't "grow"); it would mostly reduce to "the player gets stronger gear over the run," which is already implicit in any game and doesn't need the Campbell apparatus.

### Doesn't transfer
Adaptation/medium discussion (book-to-film changes), pacing-in-word-count specifics (thriller chapter lengths, 70,000-word escalation ceiling), the Magic: The Gathering skill-layering analogy as delivered (useful as a teaching philosophy note for Ben, not as class content), and the deep dive into Hero's Journey's academic/folklore critique (irrelevant to 11-13-year-olds).

## Honest caveats
Sanderson repeatedly undercuts his own frameworks ("if you treat any of these as sacrosanct... you become a cook instead of a chef") — the lecture's real thesis is that all of these are diagnostic tools, not required recipes, which should temper any worksheet that presents them as a fill-in-the-blank checklist. He also flags Three-Act/Hero's Journey as Western-tradition-specific (implicitly, following on from lecture 2's explicit statement) and admits the Hero's Journey is "vague enough to fit almost anything," which is a real limitation worth keeping in the teacher's notes if this framework gets used with kids: it risks becoming an empty label-matching exercise rather than a real design tool unless anchored to something concrete like a waypoint list.

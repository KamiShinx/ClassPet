# Golf Story - Why Golf RPGs Work (Design Doc, 7.7 min)

**What it is:** Video essay arguing golf is uniquely suited to RPG progression because stat increases are immediately, visibly felt in play (your drive literally flies further), unlike most sports RPGs where a stat bump is a hidden percentage change.
**Substance:** substantive for its one core claim, thin everywhere else: the narrative/worldbuilding praise ("Golf Story has a good story, most sports games don't") is a value judgment with no method behind it, but the visible-feedback argument is specific, well-argued, and genuinely portable.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [2:51] Most RPGs' numeric progression fits their world because the world is already fantastical, so an invisible probability system (dice rolls, hit%) reads as "how the world works" (generic framing, but sets up the payoff below).
- [3:58] **Core idea**: a probability-based stat increase (e.g. shot% 80→85 in a basketball RPG) is real progress but is nearly undetectable moment-to-moment, because a 5% swing rarely changes the outcome of any single possession you're watching.
- [5:03] Fire Emblem case: early games' hit% was mathematically exact, but players hated missing a "95%" shot, so the developers secretly rolled twice and averaged, to make stated odds *feel* more reliable than they technically are (a real, citable example of a studio fudging visible randomness to match player feel).
- [6:10] Golf Story payoff: leveling up visibly changes ball flight distance, slice/backspin, and shot outcomes the player can watch happen — "your performance makes it clear that something has changed," which the video says is *why* golf RPGs feel more satisfying than other sports RPGs.

## What the frames add
Green-themed title/section cards match the golf motif but add no new information. Side-by-side gameplay clips of Golf Story, Mario Golf, NBA2K, Madden and Fire Emblem [4:00-6:45] just illustrate the "visible vs invisible stat" contrast already stated in narration — nothing the transcript doesn't already say.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Can you SEE it work?" item-design check (10 min, spec-review step)**: for any stat-boosting item/enchant a kid designs (extra damage, extra speed, extra luck), have them describe what a player would visibly see change in the game world, not just a number going up in a menu — e.g. "faster mining" should show visibly faster block-break animation, not just a silent tick counter. Directly modeled on the Golf Story vs. hidden-percentage contrast.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
This is a genuinely Minecraft-relevant design principle and buildable: NeoForge lets you tie an attribute change to a visible in-world effect (particle trail, faster swing animation, longer knockback distance) rather than a number that only shows in a tooltip. It's a real instance of "bends an existing Minecraft system" — most vanilla enchantments already do this well (Sharpness = visibly bigger numbers; Efficiency = visibly faster breaking), which gives kids concrete vanilla examples to point at.

### Doesn't transfer
The "sports RPG needs narrative + world + numeric system all three, or it's not a real sports RPG" argument is a genre-classification exercise about golf/sports games specifically and has no Minecraft mod application.

## Honest caveats
The video's central claim (visible feedback beats invisible probability) is well-argued but not really about golf, or even about RPGs — it's a general feedback/juice principle from game-feel design, illustrated using a golf RPG. Worth crediting to this video's Fire Emblem example since that's the sharpest, most citable piece of evidence in the batch.

# Terrible Game Missions (Design Doc, 22.1 min)

**What it is:** A catalogue of mission-design failure modes across many games — grouped by category (over-demanding physics, control/design mismatch, tailing, escort, unlikeable characters, nonsensical impossible objectives).
**Substance:** substantive as a "what not to do" reference: specific, well-explained failures with clear root causes, not just a highlight reel of bad moments. ~1 min sponsor read ([0:01:13]-[0:01:44]).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:40] Framing thesis: games have almost no leverage to keep a frustrated player playing (unlike a bad job you need money from) — a single clunky mission can break the whole player relationship, so failure patterns matter more in games than in other media (generic-adjacent framing, but sets up the rest usefully).
- [0:02:15] Failure type 1 — **demanding too much precision from imprecise physics**: Mario Sunshine's Poison River stage combines twitchy boat controls, a fast current, and full restart-on-mistake punishment; explicit lesson: "imprecise physics and harsh punishment for small mistakes are never a good combination."
- [0:05:03] Failure type 2 — **a control scheme that's fine for the base game breaks under a stricter side-mission**: We Love Katamari's "roll up exactly one specific cow/bear" mission fails because Katamari's controls/camera were never built for precision object-identification, and the game's loose definition of "cow-shaped object" (traffic cones, costumes) turns the mission into a troll-filled minefield. Named lesson: "what the game asks you to do and what the game is good at letting you do" must match.
- [0:07:55] Failure type 3 — **unpolished, mismatched controls for a whole minigame**: Yooka-Laylee's minecart stages (Kartos) have a rigid camera, fixed jump height, overcharged boost, fixed-forward cannon and long cooldowns, laid over obstacle placement designed as if the controls were tighter — stacking problems until the whole minigame is simply not fun regardless of the underlying concept's merit.
- [0:11:07] Failure type 4 — **tailing missions**: BotW's "Lost Pilgrimage" shrine quest has you follow an NPC at a distance with no on-screen feedback about detection range, so the only safe strategy is "hang back and do nothing" for 7 minutes — named as boring specifically because of a total lack of feedback and near-total lack of player agency.
- [0:12:47] Failure type 5 — **escort missions**, broken down into specific causes across examples: bad pathfinding making the escortee walk into danger (Spyro 2), a forced narrative excuse to retread already-explored content (Zelda: Skyward Sword's Scrapper volcano climb), too many escortees + zero checkpoints (Jak 2's 3-person sewer escort), and escortee AI with no self-preservation compounding as group size grows (Dead Rising) — the throughline: no example of a well-loved escort mission exists, because there are too many independent ways for the mechanic to break.
- [0:16:37] Explicit combined lesson from the escort section: "bad pathfinding, bad self-preservation, bad checkpointing, and bad comedy can easily wreck the fun" — treat each as an independent risk to check off.
- [0:16:58] Failure type 6 — **unlikeable characters bleeding into gameplay mechanics**: Ace Attorney's "Moe" witness punishes the player for pressing statements that trigger his unfunny tangents, with no way to know in advance which statements are safe — turns a normally-rewarding mechanic (press for more info) into trial-and-error punishment, because the writing and the mechanic actively fight each other.
- [0:19:57] Failure type 7 — **an objective that's impossible without warning**: Shadow the Hedgehog's bomb-disarming mission has some bombs scripted to explode no matter what the player does, with zero feedback distinguishing "you failed" from "this was never possible" — named as the purest form of a no-win, no-communication design failure.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Mission failure checklist" worksheet (20 min):** turn the 7 failure types into a one-page checklist a kid runs their OWN quest/mission idea against before building it: does it demand precision the mod's controls/physics don't support? does it ask the player to babysit an NPC with bad pathfinding? does it have a no-win state with no feedback? This is a review tool, not a generative one, and pairs naturally with the "Spec a side quest" worksheet from the yTJ_RjfhGVQ notes.
- **"Playtest and break it" exercise (15-20 min, pairs activity):** kid A designs a mini time-limited or escort-style challenge for their mod idea (on paper), kid B tries to find the failure mode from the checklist above that would ruin it. Cheap way to teach critique without needing a working build yet.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The lessons here are almost entirely about EXECUTION quality, not content ideas, so they transfer as warnings rather than activities. Two are directly actionable for a NeoForge mod: (1) "an escort NPC needs decent pathfinding or the mission is doomed" — Minecraft mob pathfinding (via vanilla Brain/goal-selector systems) is a real, known-hard problem, so any kid who wants an "escort a villager" quest should be told upfront that pathfinding is the single biggest risk, matching the video's own diagnosis. (2) "a mission needs clear feedback, not silent failure" — directly maps onto advancement toasts and particle/sound cues Minecraft already has for free; a kid's custom quest should always give the player a visible signal (sound, particle, chat message) rather than a Shadow-the-Hedgehog-style silent no-win state.

### Doesn't transfer
The precision-platforming and camera/control-polish failures (Mario Sunshine, Yooka-Laylee) are about first-party engine/control feel that an 11-13-year-old using Gemini to write NeoForge Java has zero access to tune at that level — not a one-page spec problem, more a "this is why professional game controls are hard" aside. The Ace Attorney dialogue-punishment example is about writing/mechanic mismatch in a genre (visual novel courtroom drama) with no Minecraft equivalent; keep as a one-line moral ("don't punish the player for doing the normal, expected action") rather than a worksheet.

## Honest caveats
This is a well-reasoned "failure taxonomy" video, not padded — every example gets a specific, different root cause rather than just "this was annoying." No claims here need fact-checking since it's all direct commentary on shipped games. It's consistent with and complements the other Design Doc videos in this batch (yTJ_RjfhGVQ's Paper Mario Trouble Center criticism is basically failure type 5's "forced retread" pattern recurring). One thing to flag: this video is a pure warning-list with no generative "here's how to build a great mission" content — pair it with yTJ_RjfhGVQ (the positive framework) rather than teaching it alone, or kids will only learn what NOT to do.

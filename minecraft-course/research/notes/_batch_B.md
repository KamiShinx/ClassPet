# Batch B summary — Character Design (4 videos)

Sources: Kirbs "Every Assignment I Had in My Character Design Class" (j9cHhwmeNRM), BaM Animation "GOOD vs BAD
Character Design" (8wm9ti-gzLM), Blaze "Why Character Design is about more than just Style" / the Concord essay
(5eymH15AfAU), Drelix "Character Design Is Easy, Actually" (nkoJTc_WzFI).

## The 5-10 strongest ideas across the batch

1. **Silhouette is king, and it survives the move to Minecraft almost undiminished — possibly the single
   strongest idea in the whole batch.** All four videos independently converge on this (Kirbs' hero-asset test,
   BaM's black-silhouette check repeated as a troubleshooting step, Drelix's four-tools framework, Blaze's TF2
   role-from-outline argument). A mob seen at a distance, in low light, rendered as cubes, is read almost
   entirely by outline. Becomes a standing class rule: render/screenshot every mob in silhouette or full black
   before finalizing texture, every single week.

2. **Shape language (circle=soft, square=stable, triangle=dangerous) is corroborated by THREE separate videos**
   (BaM, Drelix, and implicitly Kirbs). This is not one creator's pet theory — treat it as safe, well-established
   convention. It maps directly onto Blockbench cube arrangement: a kid can build a "soft" mob from rounded cube
   clusters or a "sharp" mob from thin angular protrusions with zero drawing skill, using geometry alone.

3. **Write before you draw (trait card / Who-Want-Why) is the best entry point for non-drawing kids.** Kirbs'
   professor tip ("write descriptive words/backstory before designing") and Drelix's Who/Want/Why Venn diagram
   say the same thing from different angles. This produces BOTH the tooltip/lore flavor text and the mechanical
   direction in one 10-minute, zero-drawing-skill pass — probably the single best first worksheet of the course.

4. **Function-first design is the batch's most game-relevant (not just appearance-relevant) idea, from Blaze's
   Concord essay.** In fast-paced games, a design's job is to telegraph capability at a glance because the
   player has no time to read a tooltip mid-fight — exactly Minecraft's situation (real-time combat, instant
   item use). The video's own diagnostic game ("show the design, guess the ability") is directly reusable as a
   classroom activity and needs no extra tooling.

5. **Value/color-hierarchy discipline (one dominant color, checked in grayscale, no competing mid-tones) from
   BaM becomes MORE important, not less, at 16x16 pixels.** A full illustration can absorb some competing colors;
   a 16x16 texture cannot. "Pick one dominant color, verify it isn't competing in value" is a genuinely tighter
   constraint here than in the source medium, which makes it a good, buildable rule rather than aesthetic
   moralizing.

6. **"Overdesign is the #1 mistake" (Drelix) and "simple executed well beats maximal detail" (BaM) are the same
   warning from two sources, and this is probably the most important STANDING DISCIPLINE for this specific
   course.** 11-13-year-olds vibecoding for the first time will default to wanting maximal complexity; both
   videos argue the opposite is not just tasteful but practically necessary (redrawability in the source medium;
   Gemini's build budget and 16x16 texture budget in ours).

7. **The "guess the ability from the design alone" peer-check game (Blaze) plus the "guess the silhouette" game
   (Drelix) are ready-made, repeatable classroom activities**, not just theory — both need zero extra tooling
   beyond a Blockbench render and a partner, and both directly test whether a design is doing its job.

8. **Drelix's five diagrams (Who/Want/Why Venn, Setting 101 Venn, the four-tool silhouette wheel, the color
   wheel, the character-design chart) are essentially a ready-made worksheet packet** — more reusable as literal
   teaching material than anything in the other three videos, which mostly narrate rules rather than diagram
   them.

## Where the videos agree / contradict

- **Strong agreement** on silhouette-first and shape-language across all four — this triangulated agreement is
  the strongest evidence in the batch for anything, and should anchor the course's design-approach language.
- **Mild tension on where design starts.** Drelix insists design should start from WRITING (setting + personality)
  before any visual work; BaM's closing message is nearly the same ("story" is the real final ingredient) but
  arrives at it only at the very end, after 15 minutes of visual technique; Kirbs' class structure starts
  students on pure ANATOMY/visual technique first and only introduces "write first if you're stuck" as a later
  tip for one specific villain assignment. Net read: writing-first is the stronger, more load-bearing claim for
  THIS course (it's also the only approach that works for kids who can't draw), but it's worth remembering Kirbs'
  actual class taught technique first and narrative second.
- **No real contradiction on color**, but a real difference in confidence: BaM explicitly flags color psychology
  as "not as clear as shape language" (context-dependent, culturally variable) while Drelix presents a long,
  confident rundown of color meanings with many examples. Recommend teaching color psychology with BaM's hedge
  attached, not Drelix's confidence.
- **Function vs. appearance is a real fault line the batch doesn't resolve, and Ben should know about it.**
  Three of four videos (Kirbs, BaM, Drelix) are 100% about how a character LOOKS/READS. Only Blaze's video is
  about what a design's appearance COMMUNICATES MECHANICALLY. This confirms Ben's stated suspicion: character-
  design content alone, even done well, does not cover "what makes mechanics fun to play." Blaze's video is the
  closest thing in this batch to that territory, but it only covers the legibility slice (can you tell what a
  mechanic does by looking at it), not balance, feel, or fun more broadly. A dedicated game-design source is
  still needed.

## Ideas to cut

- **Caricature drawing (Kirbs)** — needs real drawing/likeness skill, no clean Minecraft analogue, not worth
  class time.
- **The full 7-color psychology rundown (Drelix)** — too long/reference-heavy for an 11-13 lecture; distill to a
  3-4 color one-page cheat sheet instead of teaching the video's full version.
- **Live Photoshop value/color-balance correction workflow (BaM)** — real 2D illustration software craft, no
  Blockbench/texture-tool equivalent.
- **Style imitation / learning many art styles (Kirbs, BaM)** — professional-illustrator career advice for people
  doing client work; irrelevant to hobbyist kids each building one personal mod.
- **All of Blaze's discourse-defense tangent (diversity discourse, Xbox release, pricing, ~10 minutes)** — games-
  industry commentary, zero classroom use.
- **Costume progression / multiple outfits per story beat (Kirbs)** — real assignment, but too much scope for an
  8-kid, 20-week course where each kid needs one working mod concept; stretch goal at best.

## Note on visuals across the batch

Frame density mattered a lot here and confirms the brief's warning. BaM's video (8wm9ti-gzLM) is the most
visually load-bearing of the four — its live Photoshop demos and silhouette/proportion charts are genuinely
teaching content beyond the narration. Drelix's video (nkoJTc_WzFI) is the second most useful visually: its five
recurring diagrams are close to ready-made worksheets. Kirbs' video (j9cHhwmeNRM) is mostly a cartoon-avatar
talking-head with occasional glimpses of his real homework (the evil-queen trait card and the two monster
designs are the most useful stills). Blaze's video (5eymH15AfAU) is almost entirely gameplay b-roll and hero-card
screenshots; useful for confirming the "these designs really are visually flat" claim but not for diagrams or
technique demonstration.

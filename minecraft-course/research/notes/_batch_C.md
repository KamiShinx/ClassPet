# Batch C summary — character design (Cuora 4iw8XLusQ7s, 47 min; The Drawing Codex rm0wi0afoKM, 118 min)

Both videos are 2D-illustrator character design content, one dense theory essay (Cuora) and one loose real-time production demo (Drawing Codex). Neither touches game mechanics or game design at all — this batch does nothing for Ben's "game design is probably also needed" suspicion; that gap is confirmed, not filled.

## Strongest ideas for the class, across both videos

1. **Silhouette test.** The single best-supported idea in the batch — both videos independently treat a design's readable black silhouette as the core quality bar (Cuora states it as theory, [4iw8XLusQ7s 0:12:38]; Drawing Codex actually builds one as a production step via Quick Mask before adding color, [rm0wi0afoKM 0:57:13]). This maps almost exactly onto a Blockbench mob seen from a distance in a blocky world — it is the single most directly transferable idea in the batch. **Activity**: export the finished mob, screenshot it small/far away, can a classmate guess what it is in 3 seconds.

2. **Shape language (circle/square/triangle → primary/secondary/tertiary shape).** Cuora only [0:09:22]-[0:12:40], but strongly grounded (real-world danger-sign psychology) and an almost literal description of how a cube-based Blockbench model is assembled. **Activity**: pick a primary/secondary/tertiary shape for the mob before opening Blockbench.

3. **Check the design at the scale it will actually be seen.** Cuora doesn't say this directly, but Drawing Codex repeats it twice as a hard rule ([0:27:47], [1:20:24]) — zoom out / view small before judging. For a Minecraft mob (seen small, at a distance, in motion) this is *more* true than for either source's own illustrations.

4. **Small, deliberate, shared palette.** Cuora's 60/30/10 dominant/secondary/accent split plus color-harmony-vs-contrast [0:18:00]; Drawing Codex's habit of reusing the same few eyedropped colors across a whole cast so it reads as one faction [1:10:23]. Both push toward *economy* — exactly what a 16x16 texture forces anyway. **Activity**: 3-color palette card per mob; require factions/worlds to share 2-3 signature colors across every mob and item in that group.

5. **"Does this element earn its place?" purpose check.** Cuora's practical/symbolic/rule-of-cool scale [0:24:33]. Good as a design-review question before a kid prompts Gemini to build a mechanic for that item/ability — cheap and concrete.

6. **Generate several rough options before committing.** Cuora's mind-map brainstorm [0:04:56]; Drawing Codex's "do 20-30, expect the first few to be generic, don't judge too early" philosophy [0:16:05]. Agreement across a theory video and a working professional's actual practice is a fairly strong signal. Compress to "sketch/describe three rough shapes, pick the least generic one" for a 65-minute class.

7. **Environment/culture-fit test.** Cuora only [0:19:38]: if a design needs five layers of explanation to make sense in its world, that's a tell rather than show. Good tie-in to the worldbuilding unit — "can you explain your mob's look in one sentence from your world's lore?"

## Where the two sources agree vs. pull apart

They agree strongly on silhouette-first thinking, shape/color economy, and generating quantity before quality. The one real tension: Cuora argues every design element should justify itself (rigor, [0:24:33]); Drawing Codex spends much of its runtime happily leaving elements unexplained ("no idea what that is, might delete it later," [1:16:48]) and chasing "happy accidents." Both are legitimate but for different moments — present rigor for anything tied to a game mechanic Gemini will implement (it needs to make functional sense), and looseness for first-draft brainstorming (don't force an 11-year-old to justify every doodle before they've found the idea worth keeping).

## What we'd cut

- All of Drawing Codex's actual software technique (Quick Mask silhouette building, Alt-click flat-color hue-shifting, hard-light shading layers, "spotting blacks" comic inking, contrast squares) — Photoshop-specific 2D painting craft with no Blockbench/pixel-texture equivalent. Interesting to watch once as an illustrator's workflow, useless as a course activity.
- Cuora's line-quality/converging-lines-guide-the-eye material [0:35:12] and Drawing Codex's real-time figure/anatomy construction (Loomis ribcage-and-spine building) — both need actual drawing skill our kids don't have and Blockbench doesn't require.
- Cuora's extended anime/gacha-game design critique tangents (is Chasca bad, is Bayverse Transformers overdesigned) — evidence for his points, not reusable content.
- Cuora's specific detail-hierarchy percentages (70/30, 60/30/10) and the "if a kid can redraw it" debate — both explicitly flagged by Cuora himself as one example/opinion, not a rule; don't hand kids a formula.
- Nearly all of Drawing Codex's middle 35 minutes of silent-ish linework redrawing — it is the process behind idea #1 above, but watching it contributes nothing beyond what's already captured in that one bullet.

## Bottom line
Cuora is the batch's real teaching source — dense, theory-first, and (aside from the anime-critique tangents) close to ready to adapt into worksheets. Drawing Codex is worth mining for the handful of process habits above (silhouette-then-color, check-at-scale, shared palette, don't over-judge first drafts) but should not be assigned to watch — at 118 minutes for roughly 15-20 minutes of extractable content, it's a poor return for classroom time.

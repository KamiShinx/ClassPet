# Batch D — worldbuilding videos: strongest ideas for the class

## The special-focus question, answered up front
Of the five, only two give an actual **starting procedure** rather than commentary: **Stoneworks** ("A Comprehensive Guide," juxoTahjPkk) and, more narrowly, **Hello Future Me** ("Hard vs. Soft Worldbuilding," gcyrrTud3x4). The other three are commentary/checklist videos: **Joriam Ramos** promises "where do you start" in its title but spends a third of its runtime on general writing philosophy before giving one usable technique; **Alex Renard** is a flat list of game-worldbuilding features with no sequence; **Large Lads' "7 Deadly Sins"** is a checklist of what to avoid, not what to do first — useful as tooltips, not a procedure.

On the sins video's core question — does its checklist apply to a kid's Minecraft mod or only to published fiction — the answer is split: **Pride (lore dump), Lust (rule of cool needs consequences), and Gluttony (world builder's disease) transfer directly and are genuinely useful**; **Greed (monetizing your world) does not transfer at all** (no audience, no publisher, no Patreon in an 8-kid classroom — cut it outright); **Wrath (edgelord escalation) and Envy (cloning) are weak, partial fits** better reframed than used as-is.

## 5-10 strongest ideas across the batch
1. **Pick a theme in a few words before anything else** (Stoneworks, [0:08:59]). A short vibe phrase ("barbarians on war dogs, everyone wears fur") immediately answers concrete design questions and rules out infinite other options. This is the single best "where do you start" activity in the batch — concrete, fast, and solves the blank-page problem 11-13-year-olds will hit in week one.
2. **World builder's disease is the real risk for this course** — named explicitly and independently by **both** Stoneworks ([0:03:37], [0:10:47]) and the Sins video's Gluttony sin ([0:15:16]): planning/lore-writing feels productive but can consume all the time meant for building. Given the course structure (a "platform" for lore + 20 weekly 65-minute sessions), this is the single most important idea to bake into class discipline, not just a worksheet.
3. **Start with one region/one room, not the whole world** (Stoneworks [0:10:47]; independently, Joriam Ramos's "pick a scale" [0:05:54]). Two unrelated sources converge on this. It fits Minecraft's medium even better than prose — a kid physically cannot build "the whole world" in 65 minutes, so the rule matches a real constraint, not just craft advice.
4. **Cool ideas need consequences** (Sins video's Lust, [0:11:58]; Stoneworks' Rule of Cool, [0:18:37]; Alex Renard's "mechanics reflect the world," [0:03:42]). Three sources converge: if a kid adds a cool item/mob/structure, something else in the world (a price, a mob behavior, a rare block) should visibly change because of it. This is the strongest ready-made worksheet in the batch — pairs a fun design prompt with real systems thinking, and Gemini can actually implement the "something changes" half.
5. **Lore should be earned by the player, not front-loaded** (Sins video's Pride, [0:01:07]; Hard-vs-Soft's closing 3-step method, [0:15:56]-[0:16:11]; Alex Renard's "show don't tell," [0:01:35]). Three independent sources agree: explain only what's needed right now, show the rest through play rather than text. Convert into a single filter question kids apply to any lore they write: "does the player need this right now?"
6. **Leave one thing permanently unexplained** (Hard-vs-Soft [0:10:38]; Alex Renard's unreachable landmarks, [0:10:08] — two sources independently give the same concrete rule). Cheap, fun, and immediately buildable: a locked door, an unreachable structure, a mob with no explanation.
7. **Reverse-engineer logic from a cool idea, don't front-plan it** (Stoneworks [0:19:07], [0:08:59]). Matches how vibecoding will actually work — kids will describe a cool mechanic to Gemini first and only need to justify "why" afterward. Friendlier to 11-13-year-olds than "plan the whole system before building anything."
8. **Tonal/difficulty consistency once established** (Stoneworks' Rule of Consistency [0:20:04]; Sins video's Wrath [0:06:33]). Reframed for a kid's mod as a game-balance idea rather than literary tone: if every mob is a boss-tier threat, none of them feel special.

## Where videos agree or contradict
The strongest agreement in the batch is the **three-way convergence on "build small, don't over-plan"** (points 2 and 3 above) — Stoneworks, the Sins video, and Joriam Ramos arrive at the same warning from three different framings (a guide, a sins checklist, and a craft essay), which makes it the most trustworthy idea in the set. There's a real **tension worth flagging to Ben**: he wants the course to produce "insane lore and game mechanics" by year's end, but three of five sources independently warn that unchecked lore-writing is the most common failure mode in worldbuilding communities, and the fourth (Hard-vs-Soft) argues explicit, exhaustively-documented lore actively hurts immersion compared to a few well-chosen essential facts. The "platform" concept should build in a hard cap on how much pure lore-writing time is allowed before build/vibecode time each week.

## What to cut
Cut Joriam Ramos's literary "joy of writing"/emotional-bait framework (reader psychology and prose technique — no equivalent in a Minecraft mod). Cut Stoneworks' TTRPG section and ~17-rule real-world map-geography lightning round (both assume hand-drawn 2D maps or tabletop play; Minecraft terrain is built or generated, not drawn). Cut the Sins video's Greed sin entirely (online monetization has no analog in an 8-kid classroom) and treat Wrath as optional/reframed rather than literal. Cut Alex Renard's music-key mood analysis and full NPC-schedule simulation (both exceed what a beginner NeoForge mod or an 11-13-year-old's time budget can support) — keep the simpler "faction philosophy" and "show don't tell" pieces from that video instead.

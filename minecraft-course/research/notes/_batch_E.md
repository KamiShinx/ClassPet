# Batch E — Worldbuilding (5 videos)

Videos: Large Lads Studios "Seven Levels" (vFMnz75y5y4), Curious Archive "moldy worldbuilding" (iHSEFMYjbnE), Adam Millard "Videogames Have Terrible Worldbuilding" (bups0ZUQdvc), Hello Future Me "Videogame vs Normal Worldbuilding" (2eKD0_60lfA), Rick's Creations (hkVGTHLgOIQ).

Only two of five (Millard, Hello Future Me) are actually about *game* worldbuilding; Large Lads and Rick's are general/hobbyist worldbuilding with games as occasional examples; Curious Archive is games-heavy but about one narrow technique (ruins/decay). For the class's central question — player vs reader — Millard is the load-bearing source, Hello Future Me a close second, the rest supporting or off-topic.

## Strongest ideas across the batch (ranked)

1. **Cap in-game text hard; lore answers questions, doesn't pad volume.** Millard: unanswered questions/"seams" break trust fastest; a short worldview beats a long biography (Alpha Centauri vs Civ: Beyond Earth). Teach kids a ~2-sentence limit on any in-game text.
2. **Build small and deep, not big and thin.** Three videos converge independently: Millard's "dense beats broad" (Prey, Bioshock), Rick's "worldbuilder's disease" warning (zoom to one village, not a continent), Curious Archive's layered-ruin technique (depth from stacking detail in one place). Strongest idea for an 8-kid, 20-week course: one small, dense POI per kid beats a sprawling half-built world.
3. **Consistent "surface identity" kit per faction/mob is cheap and high-payoff.** Large Lads' Level 3, Rick's naming methods, and Hello Future Me's "organizational substructures" all converge, and it maps directly onto Blockbench + 16x16 textures: one naming pattern, one block palette, one motif per faction.
4. **Hook before lore.** Millard's "psychological beachhead" — give players something to care about before any backstory lands, or it's noise. A planning step before any Gemini prompting.
5. **Ruins and items carry history with zero exposition.** Curious Archive's "a ruin is a story and a question it never answers" plus Millard's item-flavor-text examples (Bloodborne, Pokédex) both map onto systems Minecraft already has (mossy blocks, item lore lines, loot tables).
6. **Peer "annoying question" review as a class ritual.** Millard's rule that worldbuilding must survive a curious player's blunt questions, turned into an activity: a partner interrogates the design after building; unanswerable holes get cut or fixed.
7. **Landmark and sightline placement is worldbuilding, not just level design.** Hello Future Me's horizon-blocking and landmark-on-skyline tricks are buildable in Minecraft with zero new assets — a game-only technique with no book equivalent.
8. **Numbers-vs-mystery as a deliberate tooltip decision.** Hello Future Me: exact stats vs staying vague is itself a worldbuilding choice — a concrete decision for kids to make and justify per item/mob.
9. **Seed of joy + idea capture as the week-1 ritual.** Rick's habits (pick one thing you'll never get bored of; write down every idea immediately) are medium-independent and cheap — good as the course's first activity, before touching Minecraft.

## Where videos agree or contradict

- **Agreement, strong:** "small and dense beats big and sprawling" appears independently in Millard, Rick's, and Curious Archive (see #2 above) — safe to treat as near-consensus and build the course structure around it (one POI/village per kid, not a continent).
- **Agreement:** surface identity / naming / culture consistency is cheap and valuable across Large Lads, Rick's, and Hello Future Me (see #3).
- **Contradiction:** Large Lads' Level 6-7 (infrastructure, institutions, self-sustaining world) implicitly treats *more* systemic depth as the ceiling of good worldbuilding, while Millard explicitly shows more lore volume can *hurt* a game (Starfield's unexplained factions, Civ: Beyond Earth's flatter-despite-longer lore). For a small mod class, side with Millard: skip institutional depth almost entirely.
- **Contradiction/self-undermining:** Hello Future Me praises Morrowind's marker-less, description-only navigation as superior design, then admits he personally quit its main quest as a 12-year-old because of exactly that friction — effectively a confession the technique fails for an audience the same age as this class. Invert it: kids need clarity and landmarks, not deliberate navigational obscurity.

## Ideas to cut

- Large Lads' Levels 6-7 (infrastructure/institutions, generative self-sustaining world) — no mechanical hook in a small mod, no writing throughput possible in a 65-minute session.
- Rick's painting/ink-mapmaking process and book-compendium framing — wrong medium; Minecraft/Blockbench replaces "draw a map" with "build the terrain."
- Curious Archive's "rot corrupting a belief system" (Kingdom of the Planet of the Apes) — too literary/abstract to become a mechanic; keep only the visual/environmental layer of the idea.
- Millard's Persona-5-commute and Half-Life-2 forced-interaction examples — genuinely great but need scripted-sequence complexity beyond this course's tooling and time.
- Hello Future Me's Warhammer 40k "player actions become canon" story and Cyberpunk's million-word-script scale — inspiring but need a scale of community or writing volume 8 kids can't produce; keep as a fun anecdote to tell, not an activity.

## Note on Millard's implied answer to the batch's framing question

Millard's video is effectively a direct answer to "does videogames-have-terrible-worldbuilding change what we ask kids to produce": yes — it means the course should optimize for *tight, answerable, player-facing* lore (one hook, one memorable structure, short tooltips that survive a blunt question) rather than encouraging kids to write pages of backstory that never surface in play. That should be the throughline connecting this batch to the character-design batch and to any "game design" module Ben adds later.

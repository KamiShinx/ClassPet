# Batch F summary — Brandon Sanderson's 2025 BYU Plot lectures (5 videos, ~335 min)

All five are unedited BYU lectures/Q&As, talking-head or desk-talking-head throughout. Across ~6 hours the whiteboard is used only twice for anything beyond an unreadable agenda list (a tiny path sketch in lecture 2, a bulleted outline in lecture 4) — **the contact sheets add essentially nothing visual in this batch.** Every idea below is from the transcripts. These are adult-novelist lectures; the honest headline is that lecture 1 (career philosophy) is mostly irrelevant, and lecture 4.5's video-game question is disproportionately more useful than anything else here.

## Strongest ideas across the batch

1. **Promise / Progress / Payoff** (lecture 2, core of the batch). A promise is a visual/mechanical cue, progress a player-visible currency (clues found, floors descended, keys collected), payoff the delivery. Strong enough to spine the course's quest-design unit — arguably maps onto games *better* than onto prose, since games deliver promises through visible objects and progress through countable state, which a novel has to fake.

2. **Making mechanics carry the story, not just decorate it** (lecture 4.5, Borderlands' "diegetic death"/Mass Effect). The single most game-native idea in the batch, and it's five minutes buried in a bonus Q&A. Directly answers Ben's worry that worldbuilding alone isn't enough — closer to real "game design" content than the other four lectures combined. Should be pulled forward, not buried.

3. **Chef vs. cook** (lecture 1). Understand *why* a choice works so you can adapt and debug it, not just copy a pattern. The best available metaphor here for what directing Gemini and judging its output actually requires from a kid — arguably more relevant to the course's real bottleneck than any plot structure.

4. **Try-Fail Cycle / "Points on the Map"** + **Yes-but/No-and escalation** (lecture 3). Lightweight outlining tools (pick a few waypoints, fill in between; escalate every "does the plan work?" beat) suited to a kid who won't build a full outline. Good fit for quest-chain design without novel-length planning.

5. **"Gorilla in the phone booth" / hang a lantern on it** (lecture 3). Fast design-review question for any mod feature: is this weird thing explained, ignorable, or will it stop a player cold? Cheap, immediately usable as a checklist item.

6. **Chekhov's Gun / "spinach in the smoothie" exposition** (lecture 4). If you place something notable, it should matter; teach lore through doing, not text dumps. Both are naturally easier in a game than in prose.

7. **Worldbuilding is the least important of the three** (plot/character/worldbuilding, lecture 2). Useful pushback on the course's current center of gravity: great worldbuilding with no promise/progress/payoff in the quests will still feel hollow to play. Worth surfacing to Ben directly.

8. **Descriptive, not prescriptive feedback** (lecture 1) + **"where were you bored," not "what should I change"** (lecture 4). Ready-made house rule for peer playtesting among 8 kids — keeps critique low-stakes and non-hijacking.

## Where videos agree or contradict

- Lectures 1-4 independently repeat the same "chef vs. cook, don't treat any framework as sacrosanct" warning — every structure taught (Hero's Journey, Three-Act, even promise/progress/payoff) is meant as a diagnostic tool, not a checklist. Any worksheet built from this batch should keep that framing, or risk becoming the rigid exercise Sanderson warns against (his own example: Lucas turning the Hero's Journey into a literal checklist, producing the "virgin birth" beat in the prequels).
- No real contradictions; lecture 4.5's try-succeed-cycle content (Dune, Dirk Pitt) is an explicit, deliberate widening of lecture 3's try-fail emphasis, not an inconsistency — Sanderson frames it that way himself.
- Lecture 1's career-philosophy content barely touches lectures 2-4's plot craft; treat it as a separate, mostly-skippable source rather than part of the "plot theory" throughline the other four form.

## What to cut

- **Nearly all of lecture 1** except chef/cook and "ideas are cheap, execution matters." Career odds, manuscript-revision workflow, writing-group logistics, survivorship bias — no path to an 11-13-year-old mod class.
- **Three-Act Structure's Hollywood vocabulary** (Inciting Incident, Plot Point 1, Midpoint, All-Is-Lost). The escalation logic is fine informally; formal screenwriting terms are a poor use of a 65-minute weekly slot versus simpler Try-Fail/Points-on-the-Map.
- **Hero's Journey/Story Circle as a taught framework.** The vaguest, most "fits anything" tool here by Sanderson's own admission; describes internal growth a mod has no native way to deliver; risks becoming label-matching busywork.
- **Book-length/word-count scaling, prose-vs-film craft, revision workflow, publishing/career material, cross-novel series-promise management.** No Minecraft-mod analogue; the largest and least useful chunks of runtime.
- **Academic Hero's Journey/folklore tangent and most Q&A audience banter** (Civ VII, outlining software, map-drawing) — entertaining, zero class value.

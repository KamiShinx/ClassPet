# Batch H summary - Brandon Sanderson, BYU worldbuilding lectures (2025)

Three linked videos from one lecture sequence: #8 (lecture, 76 min), #9 (live Q&A, 74 min), #9.5 (bonus studio
Q&A, 25 min). All talking-head/whiteboard footage aimed at adult aspiring novelists writing 100k-word books over
years. Almost none of it is about "worldbuild on Earth" specifically despite #9's title — treat all three as one
continuous worldbuilding-craft source, weakest on the real-world-settings angle CONTEXT.md flagged.

## The 5-10 strongest ideas for our class

1. **Worldbuilder's disease + the time-boxed iceberg** (#8 [0:20:52], #9.5 [0:00:32]-[0:01:37]). The single most
   load-bearing idea across the whole batch. Every worldbuilding category is a rabbit hole; without a hard deadline
   kids will plan forever and never touch Blockbench. Directly diagnoses the risk in Ben's "insane lore and game
   mechanics" ambition for an 8-kid, 65-minute-a-week class. Turn into a running class discipline (a time-boxed
   worksheet each session), not a one-off lesson.
2. **The two-column brainstorm** (#8 [0:13:05]-[0:20:17]): physical setting (biomes, mobs, weather, hazards) vs.
   cultural setting (structures, loot, factions, tooltips). This is a literal, ready-to-run worksheet format, and
   it maps cleanly onto what a Minecraft mod actually needs (biome/mob design vs. structure/item/text design).
3. **Concrete detail over ambient description, a.k.a. "show me the spice"** (#8 pyramid of abstraction
   [0:34:46]-[0:43:29]; #9.5 [0:04:57], [0:06:03]). Amateurs over-explain with proper nouns and lore paragraphs;
   the pro move is one specific sensory/behavioral detail that implies the rest. This is the correct model for
   Minecraft tooltip writing, which is inherently short — a much better fit than prose description technique in
   general.
4. **Character/mob-anchored lore, not lore-as-lecture** (#8 [0:45:37]; #9 [0:15:12]; #9.5 [0:06:03], [0:08:48]).
   Worldbuilding must not stand in the way of the actual thing being made; deliver lore through what an NPC/mob
   *does* or *wants*, not through exposition. Gives a concrete rewrite drill: turn a flat lore sentence into an NPC
   behavior or a locked/guarded item.
5. **Cool-thing-first, justify backward ("Sanderson's Zeroeth Law")** (#9 [0:46:27]). Start from the one awesome
   item/mob/mechanic a kid wants, then write one or two sentences of why it exists. This is a better fit for this
   class than research-first worldbuilding (#9's real-world-research advice, #9.5's ~10%-of-wordcount planning
   ratio) because it matches the existing "write a spec before prompting Gemini" discipline already decided for
   the course — arguably the clearest bridge between Sanderson's material and the vibe-coding structure.
6. **The Superman Problem — weakness as design, not just lore** (#9.5 [0:21:32]). Define an overpowered
   mob/boss by what it specifically *can't* do. This is the one idea in the whole batch that is simultaneously
   real worldbuilding AND a real game-design/balance mechanic. Worth flagging directly to Ben: it's a small
   existence-proof that "game design" content can be smuggled into the worldbuilding lane rather than needing an
   entirely separate video list.
7. **Contrast as a fast premise hook** (#9 [0:09:08], [0:09:42]): define a world/biome by what's different from a
   baseline (a real place, a familiar Minecraft biome). Cheap, fast, produces something pitchable in one sentence
   — good session-opener worksheet.
8. **One-line name-and-check** (#8 [0:48:47]-[0:53:55]): pick a real-language sound or one repeatable trick
   (alliteration, symmetry), then always sanity-check the result. Simple, bounded, produces usable output (item/
   mob/place names) in one sitting.

## Where videos agree / contradict

- All three agree on the same core anti-pattern under different names: worldbuilder's disease (#8), info-dumping
  showing-off (#9.5), gorilla in the phone booth (#9) — different angles on "don't let planning or lore substitute
  for the actual product." This convergence is the strongest signal in the batch and should anchor the class's
  house rule about pacing lore vs. building.
- Mild tension on research depth: #9's real-world-worldbuilding answer leans toward "build real expertise, get
  ~80% competence" for anything grounded in reality (battles, geography), while #8 and #9.5 both lean toward
  "time-box everything, 50% competence is usually enough, let the story reveal what needs more." For an 8-kid
  65-minute/week class there's no real tension in practice — the time-boxed, good-enough approach is the only one
  that fits, and the deeper-research advice (aimed at adults writing publishable historical/military fiction)
  should be read as not applicable here rather than contradicted.
- #8 and #9 each spend meaningful runtime on content that has zero transfer value to this class: #8's back half
  (~20 min) is pure prose viewpoint/tense taxonomy; #9's back half drifts into Sanderson's own BYU class logistics,
  book recommendations, and dialogue-formatting pet peeves. Neither contradicts anything, they're just noise for
  this brief.

## What I'd cut

- Viewpoint/tense (first/third/omniscient, past/present) — no narrator concept in a Minecraft mod. Cut entirely.
- Deep linguistics-based naming systems, decades-long conworld languages — too slow and too abstract for 65-minute
  sessions with 11-13-year-olds; keep only the two fastest naming tricks.
- Full lore-encyclopedia/"world bible as product" ambition (#9 [0:38:47]) — explicitly a resource sink even for a
  professional team with 40 artists; actively bad advice to point an 8-kid class toward if the real deliverable is
  the mod.
- The cultural-appropriation-in-naming discussion (#8 [0:50:31]-[0:51:04], #9 [0:06:26]) is a genuinely responsible
  point but too heavy an ethics unit for this age group in full; compress to one guideline line, don't teach as-is.
- Battle/military worldbuilding depth (#9 [0:56:23]-[1:00:14]) — a Minecraft mod for kids doesn't need historically
  grounded logistics; keep at most the "flip a familiar fight and change one rule" shortcut, drop the
  history-expertise track.
- All BYU-class-specific meta content (#9's admissions/writing-group Q&A, #9.5's discovery-writing process
  details) — not usable material for a K-12 course design at all.

## One thing not covered by this batch

Nothing here teaches what makes a game *mechanic* fun to play (Ben's separate, explicit worry). The Superman
Problem (#9.5) is the closest brush with game design and is worth keeping for that reason, but this batch is a
worldbuilding/narrative-craft source, not a game-design source — that gap is real and this batch doesn't close it.

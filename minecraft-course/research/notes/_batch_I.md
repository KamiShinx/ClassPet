# Batch I: Game Design / Mechanics - synthesis

Six videos: Game Maker's Toolkit x2, Design Doc x2, one Sakurai lecture, one Extra Credits explainer. Substance uneven: five are dense and well-argued; Perfect Imbalance is thin and mostly inapplicable at our scale.

## The strongest ideas across the batch

1. **MDA (Mechanics -> Dynamics -> Aesthetics), from "How To Think Like A Game Designer."** The best-fitting idea in the batch. A lightweight three-box check a kid can apply to any mechanic, however small: what does the code do -> what does that make the player DO -> how does that make them FEEL. It's the natural bridge between the course's two existing pillars: worldbuilding supplies the target feeling, MDA checks whether the mechanic actually delivers it. Should be the spine of any game-design content added.

2. **Risk/reward as a single dial, from Sakurai's "Risk and Reward."** A close second: danger and payoff should rise and fall together. Concrete and testable, maps directly onto combat NeoForge features (mob attack ranges, boss telegraphs, item cooldowns). Sakurai's own honesty that a beloved "genius" mechanic (the Nagoya shot) was actually a bug is a great hook for the class's existing "verify and judge what Gemini gives you" habit.

3. **Boss roles - skill test / narrative device / change of pace, from "Boss Battle Design."** A clean checklist kids apply before designing a boss (pick one job) and after (did I deliver it). Maps well onto phase-based NeoForge boss mobs.

4. **"Does fighting this teach something different?" from "Cast of Enemies."** The sharpest line in the batch: "reskinned mechanics aren't new mechanics." A fast gut-check for any custom mob - can the kid name one thing the player must do differently to beat it?

5. **Versatile verbs, scaled down, from "The Secret of Mario's Jump."** The video's real scope (inventing a whole moveset) is bigger than our unit of work, but the underlying trick - one interaction doing 2-3 things depending on press/hold/sneak - is a good spec-writing habit that matches real NeoForge item hooks.

6. **"Not the best at everything," from "Perfect Imbalance."** The one salvageable idea from the weakest video: make a kid name what their finished item is WORSE at than the vanilla equivalent.

7. **Silhouette/readability, from "Cast of Enemies" (Halo example).** A mob should read by shape alone before texture. Pairs with the character-design half of the course and Blockbench's low-res constraints.

## Where videos agree or contradict

MDA and Risk/Reward converge independently: Sakurai's "system side = mechanics, player side = strategy" is functionally the same split Mark Brown names explicitly - two traditions (Western game studies, Japanese studio practice) landing on the same idea, worth telling kids this isn't one guy's opinion. No real contradictions otherwise; the closest tension is visual vs. mechanical variety - "Cast of Enemies" praises striking looks (Halo, Pikmin) while "Boss Battle Design" and "Cast of Enemies" itself (Origami King) both show mechanical substance matters more than looks. Tell kids: looks matter, but only once the mechanic underneath is real.

## Ideas to cut

- **Perfect Imbalance's real subject** (cyclical imbalance, metagame evolution, live patch cycles) needs a competing player population and an ongoing balance team - neither exists in an 8-kid, non-competitive, single-player class. Keep only the "not best at everything" line.
- **MGS3's multi-solution boss design.** The best example in "Boss Battle Design" but too advanced (branching solutions, a system-clock exploit) for a kid's first boss.
- **Whole-bestiary, whole-playthrough novelty analysis.** "Cast of Enemies" is really about a 15-30 hour game's entire roster; our unit is 1-3 mobs per kid. Keep the per-mob gut-check, drop the "over an entire game" framing.
- **FF X/XII palette-swap critique** as kid-facing content - it's a studio art-pipeline problem, more useful as a note to Ben about the shared asset library than as a worksheet.

## Verdict: is game design the missing piece, or overkill?

Partly right, narrower than the hunch. Worldbuilding and character design tell a kid WHAT their world is; they don't tell a kid HOW to turn "a fire spirit boss" into attacks, phases, and a win condition - that translation step is genuinely missing, and these videos supply it. But "insane mechanics" oversells it: five of six videos analyze AAA games built by professional teams over years with dozens of interacting systems. The honestly transferable slice is smaller: pick one feeling (MDA), make danger and payoff rise together (risk/reward), give a mob one job and a phase change (boss roles), make sure it asks something different of the player (novelty gut-check), make one item branch 2-3 ways (versatile verbs). That's real and worth 20-30 minutes per unit as short checklists layered onto the existing character-design -> vibecoding step - not a new third pillar alongside worldbuilding and character design. Perfect Imbalance is the clearest evidence against a broad standalone "game design" module: this genre of content drifts fast into competitive/live-service territory that has nothing to do with 8 kids building solo mods.

# CHARACTER DESIGN - Step by Step Tutorial (Draw like a Sir, 12.0 min)

**What it is:** A manga-style illustrator (Marcel) walks through his numbered step system (Step 0 personality -> Step 1 base body -> Step 2 clothes/shapes/colors -> Step 3 bonus rendering) using a slide deck plus a live-drawn example ("Arizona").
**Substance:** mixed: the step framework and shape-language/color-scheme content (Step 0-2) is genuinely useful and well-illustrated with strong side-by-side examples; Step 3 (rendering/coloring/inking the final manga page) is craft demonstration with little new conceptual content, and roughly 2 of the 12 minutes are channel/Patreon/book promotion.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:36] "Step 0," before any drawing: decide age, gender, and especially personality/vibe (aggressive, arrogant, devious, confident...) — designing without an identity in mind produces bland, generic results (cites anime industry churn as a cautionary example).
- [0:01:39] Joke test: a bland character with no vibe reads as nothing memorable ("this guy's favorite food is toast... without jam, without being toasted") — memorable framing device for "no personality = no design."
- [0:01:39] "Step 1": apply the personality to the base body/pose BEFORE any clothes or detail — an expressive stance alone already conveys character.
- [0:02:15] Physique itself (not just pose) should reflect personality — cites One Piece's exaggerated/stretched body shapes as intentionally matched to each character's personality.
- [0:03:23] Even within "muscular," there are distinct physique types (slim, lean, bulky) — picking the same body type for every character makes a cast look "samey" (concretely shown as 3 different physiques side by side).
- [0:03:23] Core thesis, stated as the single takeaway: character design is not about piling on details to fake uniqueness — it starts at the base body/silhouette.
- [0:03:56] Warning: adding random stuff just to look "unique" produces a design that looks like generic NFT art — everything added should serve the chosen vibe.
- [0:04:30] "Baba and Zaza" sound-shape test (a well-known perception exercise, presented as an "age-old"/decades-old pro technique): sharp/angular shapes read as harsh, round/soft shapes read as gentle — use this to decide what shapes (clothes, hair, accessories) fit a character's vibe.
- [0:05:02] Worked example: designed Poseidon with sharp/pointed features (intimidating) vs. his wife Amphitrite with almost all soft/round features (gentle), down to accessory shapes (earring vs. necklace).
- [0:05:39] To avoid a character being ALL one shape type (too on-the-nose), mix in a small amount of the opposite shape as a "spice" — e.g., a mostly-soft character gets one pointier element to hint at hidden temper.
- [0:05:39] Ties shape language explicitly to silhouette uniqueness, citing classic (older-era) Pokemon design as simple base shapes + one added distinguishing feature.
- [0:06:11] The same soft/hard shape-language rule applies to facial features too, especially eye shape (plug for his own separate eyes tutorial).
- [0:07:15] "Step 2" colors: use a color wheel; simplest starting scheme is analogous (colors next to each other, e.g. all "warm" colors).
- [0:07:47] Complementary colors (opposite on the wheel) make a design "pop" and read as more memorable from a distance — shows a worked example (Hades: all-red vs. red+opposite-color accent) where the mono-color version blurs into one blob at a distance.
- [0:08:49] Beginner mistake: using too many colors, which (like too many silhouette details) muddies the read and fails to convey a clear emotion — recommends 2-3 dominant colors, kept muted/desaturated rather than max-saturation ("deep-fried") so there's still room to push brighter/darker later when shading.
- [0:09:56] Bonus/Step 3: design with the final medium in mind — for manga specifically, keep a strong black/white contrast ratio (dark elements needed or the character looks like an "empty outline" on the page); this is medium-specific craft, not universal.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Step 0 personality card (10 min, no drawing)**: before touching Blockbench, kid picks 1-2 vibe words for their mob/character (aggressive, sneaky, friendly, proud...) and writes one sentence for why. Directly reusable as a fill-in worksheet. Comes from [0:00:36].
- **Baba/Zaza shape sort (5-10 min, no drawing needed)**: give kids a sheet of random spiky vs. round shape doodles (or let them scribble 4 shapes each) and have them guess/assign which shape fits their character's vibe — a genuinely fun, fast, drawing-skill-free activity. Comes from [0:04:30].
- **"Pick your build" chooser**: instead of freehand body drawing, offer 3 base proportions (slim/lean/bulky, as simple pre-made Blockbench base rigs or reference silhouettes) for a kid to choose from as a starting point for their mob, avoiding blank-page paralysis. Comes from [0:03:23].
- **2-3 color rule + "spice" color worksheet**: kid picks 2 main colors for their mob (matching vibe: warm=friendly/aggressive, cool=calm/eerie) then optionally adds ONE contrasting "pop" color. Directly checkable against a printed color wheel. Comes from [0:07:15]-[0:08:49].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The core "personality/vibe drives shape before detail" idea survives essentially unchanged and is arguably the single best-transferring idea in this batch — it needs zero drawing skill, works as pure verbal/written brainstorming, and directly determines Blockbench proportions (a kid can make cubes taller/wider/spikier to match a vibe word without ever picking up a pencil). The soft-vs-sharp shape language and the "spice" (one contrasting element) rule map cleanly onto cube geometry — round-feeling silhouettes are approximated with blockier, more symmetric cube stacks, spiky ones with thin protruding cubes, and this is something Gemini/Blockbench can execute even for a non-artist kid. The 2-3 color + one pop-color rule transfers directly to a 16x16 texture, maybe even more cleanly than in illustration, since a limited pixel canvas already forces color economy. Complementary-color "pop from a distance" is highly relevant since Minecraft mobs ARE viewed from a distance in a blocky world with flat lighting.

### Doesn't transfer
The entire manga rendering/coloring/inking sequence (Step 3, roughly 00:08:56 to 00:11:20) is hand-drawing craft — pen inking, gradient shading, hand construction of eyes/hands — none of which exists in a Blockbench + 16x16 pixel-texture workflow; a kid does not ink or shade a mob by hand in this course. "Medium-specific black/white contrast for manga panels" is explicitly manga-page craft and has no Minecraft equivalent. Fine physique distinctions (slim/lean/bulky rendered through muscle definition and anatomy) are largely lost at cube resolution — Blockbench can vary a torso cube's proportions but can't render actual muscle definition, so this idea only partially survives as "make the base shape stockier or slimmer," not the nuanced physique-personality mapping shown here.

## Honest caveats
The Baba/Zaza effect (sound symbolism, aka the bouba/kiki effect) is real, well-studied psychology, so this video's claim that "pros have used this for decades" is credible, though the presenter doesn't cite anything and frames it as personal industry lore rather than research. The claim that "classic Pokemon" used simple-shape-plus-one-feature design while newer Pokemon "look like spaceships" is the presenter's own opinionated aside, not demonstrated in the frames, and reads as unnecessary editorializing — treat as an extrapolation, not evidence. Overall this is one of the stronger videos in the batch for direct classroom use because nearly every idea is illustrated with a concrete before/after graphic rather than just asserted; the promotional back half (Patreon, artbook, manga playlist, roughly 00:08:32-00:09:00 and 00:11:20 to the end) is filler and contributes nothing.

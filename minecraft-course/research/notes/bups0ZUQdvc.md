# Videogames Have Terrible Worldbuilding (Adam Millard - The Architect of Games, 26.7 min)

**What it is:** a craft-focused essay arguing games mostly get worldbuilding wrong by chasing lore *volume* instead of player *investment*, then walking through the specific techniques (dense small worlds, memorable vignettes, mechanical friction, subjective info) that the best games use instead.
**Substance:** substantive — the single most directly useful video in the batch for this class. Dense, well-argued, backed by dozens of named games, and it is explicitly about the reader-vs-player distinction the batch brief asks about.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:11] Problem statement: despite huge budgets, exposition dumps go in one ear and out the other, huge cities feel like hollow facades, and paradoxically some of the sparsest-written games immerse best.
- [0:03:17] Core thesis: developers and audiences over-prioritize lore *volume* (comprehensiveness, fully explorable continents) over making players care first; without emotional buy-in, background lore is just "informational noise."
- [0:04:57] Get players hooked fast — worldbuilding that plays before a player cares is wasted (FFXIV's ~100-hour slow burn vs FFXVI's immediate hook, even though FFXIV's underlying setting is arguably richer).
- [0:06:34] Best hook technique: use something players already care about — strong character writing — as a "psychological beachhead" into lore (Mass Effect: each companion personally embodies one lore idea — the genophage, the quarian war, biotics — instead of a text dump).
- [0:07:37] Mystery as hook: Kingdom Come's murder-mystery opening, Baldur's Gate 3's mind-flayer worm — instant stakes give an immediate reason to learn the setting.
- [0:09:46] The critical failure mode: nothing breaks a game's worldbuilding faster than an unanswered question or a visible "seam" reminding the player it's just polygons — a good setting must be ready to answer almost any question a curious player asks.
- [0:10:19] Dense beats broad: small, contained worlds (Prey's Talos I, Bioshock's Rapture) can answer nearly every question because every detail is doing double duty supporting every other detail.
- [0:11:25] For big worlds without infinite budget: a few unforgettable "highlight" moments/quests retroactively make an actually-patchy setting feel coherent (Cyberpunk's Night City: the cops are barely developed, but standout quests distract from that).
- [0:12:30] Bethesda's trick: stuff the world with small, tailor-made vignettes (funny NPCs, memorable one-off dungeons) that individually cover for a huge, inconsistent, mostly-empty open world (Skyrim's "Cloud District" joke papering over the lack of any real class system).
- [0:13:03] Biggest failure case: when a game visibly fails to answer its own questions, players feel punished for caring (Metaphor: ReFantazio's Stroll subplot goes nowhere; Starfield's unexplained colonists/factions/precursor race) — this is named as the single most damaging worldbuilding mistake in games.
- [0:14:39] More lore isn't better lore — over-explaining flattens atmosphere; a short, coherent *worldview* per character beats paragraphs of biography (Alpha Centauri's terse leader quotes vs. Civ: Beyond Earth's much longer but flatter lore).
- [0:15:45] Best-in-class games often deliberately avoid objective narration, giving every character a biased, partial account instead (Disco Elysium) — this forces the player to interpret rather than just receive facts.
- [0:16:49] Games-only mechanic: let players discover lore through consequences of their own actions, not text (Dishonored's plague visibly worsens with player choices; item flavor text references in-world people/places you can actually go meet).
- [0:17:22] "Homework" framing (a wall of codex text) is the weakest delivery — most players skip it; audio logs work better because they're voiced by an in-world character in a specific place and time, not an omniscient narrator.
- [0:17:55] Player-driven research tools make lore feel earned rather than delivered (Metroid Prime's scan visor; Pokédex entries unlocked by catching a creature).
- [0:18:26] The strongest games-only technique: worldbuilding through *mechanical friction* — physically inconveniencing the player teaches a world fact better than any text (Persona 5 forces you to literally commute through Tokyo; Half-Life 2's forced "pick up the can or the guard beats you" moment conveys Combine oppression better than a whole codex entry).
- [0:20:33] Conclusion: no amount of graphics, voice acting or AI-generated lore substitutes for making players care; games "have no choice but to tell," so the craft is finding clever indirect ways to do it — through mechanics, not just text.

## What the frames add
Genuinely load-bearing, not decorative: actual item-description text on screen (Bloodborne's "Stained Glass Window" flavor text [0:16:15]; a Pokédex entry for Hoppip [0:18:30]); Alpha Centauri's terse leader quote screen, "Resources exist to be consumed... Let us take what is ours" [0:15:30]; Warhammer 40k dialogue UI ("an accusation of heresy?"); named equipment in an item menu ("Gold-Hemmed Black Hood" etc [0:21:15]); a Fallen-London-style hyperlink-choice UI; objective-completed HUD text. These are concrete, reusable reference examples of exactly the tooltip/item-description/dialogue delivery mechanisms this class needs to imitate — worth pulling stills from directly for a slide.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Two-sentence max rule**: no in-game text block (item lore, sign, mob description) may exceed ~2 sentences — a hard writing constraint worksheet, ~10 min per item, mirrors the Bloodborne/Pokédex examples directly.
- **"Give me a reason to care in 10 seconds" planning card**: before any lore writing, kid states the one hook (mystery, threat, character) that makes another kid want to know more — done before opening Gemini at all.
- **Peer "annoying question" review**: after a kid builds a mob/structure, a partner asks 3 blunt questions about it ("why does this zombie farm sugar," "who built this"); unanswerable holes get cut or fixed on the spot. ~15 min, directly implements the "protect the investment" rule.
- **One mechanical-friction mechanic**: instead of a lore book, design one required player action that teaches a world fact physically (a mob that only drops loot after a specific interaction, an item that must be carried a certain way) — the single clearest bridge from "worldbuilding" to a buildable NeoForge item/mob-behaviour spec for Gemini.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Extremely well. Item tooltips (lore lines on custom items), mob loot tables, and one memorable "vignette" structure per kid map almost directly onto Minecraft's existing systems (item NBT lore text, structure loot tables, mob behaviour) — Gemini can implement essentially all of it as described.

### Doesn't transfer
Persona 5's slow-commute mechanic and Half-Life 2's forced-interaction moment are compelling but need scripted-sequence/animation complexity well beyond an 8-kid beginner course; keep as inspiration only, not a build target.

## Honest caveats
This is the most game-literate and most directly useful video in the batch for the class's central question (player vs reader) — treat it as close to required viewing for planning this unit. One place it may oversell for our context: it praises the "cover a patchy big world with a few vignettes" Bethesda/Cyberpunk trick as broadly transferable — but that trick exists to hide budget limits across a huge open world. For 8 kids each building a tiny mod, Millard's *other* recommendation (dense and small, like Prey or Bioshock) is far more achievable and should be the one the class actually teaches; the vignette trick is a AAA-scale workaround, not a beginner technique.

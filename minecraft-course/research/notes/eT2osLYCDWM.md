# Answering Your Worldbuilding Questions - Lecture #9.5 (Brandon Sanderson, 25 min)

**What it is:** Studio bonus video (not filmed in the lecture hall), Sanderson answering ~7 written follow-up
questions from the worldbuilding Q&A class, going deeper on points from Lectures #8/#9.

**Substance:** substantive: short and dense, no padding, each answer is a direct elaboration of a named concept
from the earlier two lectures (hollow iceberg, pyramid of abstraction, relevance/foreshadowing) with concrete
book examples.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:32]-[0:01:37] Two-part self-check for "too much worldbuilding": (1) is the amount of upfront worldbuilding
  stopping you from actually producing the story/work (a production-pace test), (2) are you including lore because
  it serves the story or just because you did the research and want to show it off (a motive test).
- [0:03:52] Symptom of under-built worldbuilding: reader confusion so severe they can't tell what to pay attention
  to (distinguish this from *intentional* productive confusion paired with strong character/plot pull).
- [0:04:57] "Pro vs. amateur" tell #1: amateurs front-load too many proper names/jargon terms early, overwhelming
  the reader before they're anchored in a character.
- [0:06:03] "Pro vs. amateur" tell #2: amateur worldbuilding reads like the author showing off research (info dump
  paragraphs); pro worldbuilding is delivered through a character having a problem and acting, e.g. citing a novel
  whose opening scene shows a character (with a personal limitation) struggling to debug a magic spell rather than
  a passage explaining the magic system. (This is the "grand skill" line repeated from Lecture #8: convey world
  through a character so the reader doesn't register it as an info dump.)
- [0:08:48] To make history/lore feel relevant rather than dumped: make it something a *character* cares about or
  is ignorant about first (attach it to relationship/emotional subtext), and know your ending in advance (or find
  it in revision) so you can seed only the details that will actually pay off later — avoid heavy-handed setup.
- [0:10:59] Give background lore a physical/visual anchor during the telling (his example: a storytelling character
  performs a visual effect while narrating; he also cites a visual motif technique from a well-known animated film)
  so it's a scene, not narration.
- [0:12:07]-[0:14:22] For nonlinear/multi-timeline structure: readers naturally bond hardest to whichever thread
  reads as the "main" one; give every other thread its own distinct hook, and drop small cross-thread callbacks
  early so readers register "these are going to connect" well before the convergence actually happens.
- [0:15:28]-[0:17:06] His own practice: worldbuilding is time-boxed by a self-imposed deadline, not by page/word
  count, done in mixed prose-notes-then-bullet-notes form, and roughly ~10% of a novel's final word count across
  all planning notes (his own observed ratio, explicitly "not a rule").
- [0:17:06] Concrete advice for worldbuilder's disease: you cannot anticipate everything you'll need before writing
  — better to start writing, then dip back into worldbuilding only when the story hits something that needs it,
  and avoid letting that detour eat the writing session.
- [0:17:39] On scene-setting frequency ("how often do you describe the spice"): every scene/chapter should
  re-establish the setting at least briefly, ideally colored by the POV character's current mood (same setting
  described differently depending on how the character feels that day) — and having a character physically
  interact with an object in the setting is a cheap, reliable way to pull the reader back into "being there."
  Static, single-location stories are explicitly NOT bad practice (he cites his own award-winning novella set
  almost entirely in one room).
- [0:21:32] For god/divine characters interacting with mortals without feeling disconnected or overpowered: give
  them a relatable want or an obstacle that thwarts them (motivation crosses power/species boundaries even if
  raw ability doesn't); optionally use "the Superman problem" — define the character by what they specifically
  can't do, which is often more interesting than an exhaustive list of what they can.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Is it working or is it showing off?" checklist**: from [0:00:32]-[0:06:03], turn the two-part litmus test into
  a 2-question self-review card a kid fills out before finishing a lore/tooltip write-up: "Did writing this stop me
  from actually building something in the mod this week?" and "Am I writing this because the game needs it, or
  because I think it's cool?" Both answers are allowed to be honest, but it surfaces the pattern early. ~5 min,
  end of session.
- **Character-anchored lore rewrite drill**: from [0:06:03] and [0:08:48]. Take a flat lore fact a kid wrote
  ("this sword was forged 200 years ago by the fire clan") and rewrite it as something a mob or NPC *does* or
  *wants* instead of states (an NPC won't sell the sword unless you prove something; a mob guards it because it
  fears something specific). ~15 min pair/small-group exercise, directly usable for NPC dialogue/quest text.
- **One Object, Changing Mood**: from [0:17:39]. Have a kid describe the same in-game location (their build) in
  one line under two different conditions (day/night, or before/after a boss fight) to practice that the *same*
  place can read differently depending on state — maps onto ambient tooltip/biome description text that changes
  with game state.
- **Superman Problem worksheet** for boss/mob design: from [0:21:32]. For any overpowered mob/boss a kid designs,
  make them answer "what CAN'T this thing do" (one weakness, one thing it fears or is blocked by) before finishing
  the design. Very short (~10 min), directly produces a game-balance hook (a weakness mechanic) as well as lore.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- The pro/amateur "names and jargon" tell [0:04:57] maps cleanly onto tooltip writing discipline: don't cram
  invented proper nouns into a 1-2 line item description before the player has any anchor — good, concrete,
  enforceable rule for 11-13-year-olds writing their first tooltips.
- The time-boxed, notes-are-only-for-you approach to worldbuilding [0:15:28]-[0:17:06] reinforces the
  worldbuilder's-disease discipline from Lecture #8 and is probably the single most repeatable rule across all
  three videos in this batch: build only what's needed for what you're making *this week*, dip back into lore only
  when the build needs it.
- The Superman Problem is a rare case in this batch of worldbuilding advice that doubles as a real game-design
  idea (a boss's weakness IS a game mechanic, not just flavor text) — worth flagging to Ben given his separate
  worry that "game design/fun" content is missing from the video list.

### Doesn't transfer
- Nonlinear/multi-timeline narrative structure advice [0:12:07]-[0:14:22] — no equivalent in a Minecraft mod;
  skip entirely.
- Divine/mortal power-scale writing as a genre problem is prose-specific in its given form; only the "Superman
  Problem" fragment survives (see above), not the broader discussion of god-characters as narrators.
- The ~10%-of-word-count planning ratio [0:16:34] is a novelist metric with no mod equivalent; don't try to port
  the number.

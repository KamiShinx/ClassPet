# Hard Worldbuilding vs. Soft Worldbuilding | A Study of Studio Ghibli (Hello Future Me, 17.9 min)

**What it is:** A scripted essay (co-written, credits a named collaborator) contrasting two worldbuilding philosophies — hard (Tolkien: explain everything, logical/consistent detail) vs. soft (Miyazaki/Ghibli: leave gaps, prioritize atmosphere and imagination over logic) — using Spirited Away, Howl's Moving Castle, Harry Potter and LOTR as case studies.
**Substance:** substantive — well-structured, sourced (a real 2002 Miyazaki interview, a Nebula-winning author's quote, JK Rowling's own public statements), and it ends with a concrete 3-step process rather than just vibes.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:15] Definition — hard worldbuilding = immersion via detailed, logical, internally consistent cultures/languages/geography (Tolkien's invented Elvish grammar as the extreme case).
- [0:02:27]-[0:03:44] Definition — soft worldbuilding = immersion via what's deliberately NOT explained (Spirited Away's river-spirit scene: no lore dump, viewer infers meaning from a bicycle pulled out of a "trash spirit").
- [0:04:26] Sourced: a 2002 Miyazaki interview quote — he intentionally omits explanatory shots (no window view during the train scene) because logic isn't the goal, feeling is.
- [0:07:24] Core split, stated directly: hard = concrete rules/consistency/transparency/realism; soft = the unknown/flexible rules/the audience's own imaginative involvement/atmosphere.
- [0:08:04] Strength #1 of soft worldbuilding: more creative freedom — the story never has to justify itself to the audience (flying whales, the unexplained hopping lantern).
- [0:09:15] Sourced: Rowling has said Harry Potter's world was built incrementally, book by book, to serve character arcs — only possible because so much was left open.
- [0:09:42] Strength #2: soft worldbuilding avoids exposition-driven pacing lulls in acts 1-2, since little is explained up front.
- [0:10:38] Strength #3 (quoting Rowling): fans build their own mental version of the world/characters in the gaps — the incompleteness IS the engagement mechanism, not a flaw.
- [0:12:24] Important reframe: soft worldbuilding isn't LESS depth, just less rationalized/justified depth — a grimdark world can come from a felt sense of "power bearing down," not documented resource-scarcity stats.
- [0:13:36]-[0:14:34] Case study of failure: Rowling's post-publication "clarifications" (wizard bathroom habits, retroactive backstory reveals) are criticized as over-explaining a world never built to be rationalized — the answers "feel like random conclusions" and undermine atmosphere.
- [0:15:56] Honesty beat: strange worldbuilding isn't automatically good — you can fail and just be illogical without earning the atmosphere.
- [0:15:56]-[0:16:11] Closing 3-step method (also shown on screen): (1) find a character-driven story, (2) figure out what lore is absolutely essential for the audience to follow that story, (3) build everything else loosely around the atmosphere/tone/meaning you want, without justifying it.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Essential lore only" filter**: before writing any lore, kids answer one question per fact — "does the player need to know this to understand what they're doing right now?" If no, cut it or turn it into an optional in-game detail (a sign, an item description) instead of a document. Directly lifted from the closing 3-step method; independently agrees with the "lore dump" sin in the Large Lads video.
- **"One unexplained thing" rule**: every kid's world must have at least one deliberately mysterious element (an unreachable structure, an unexplained mob, a locked door) — the same idea Alex Renard's video gives independently (two sources converge).
- **Character-first mini-brief**: before any lore, kids write one sentence on what their player-character or custom mob wants and is afraid of — matches "find a character-driven story first," and is something 11-13-year-olds can actually do without writing a novel.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The core idea — atmosphere and selective mystery over exhaustive explanation — transfers unusually well, arguably better than to prose: a mod shows almost everything through blocks/mobs/effects rather than text, so "soft worldbuilding" is close to the path of least resistance for a beginner builder. The "essential lore only" filter is a genuinely useful discipline for a class where kids will be tempted to write pages of lore instead of building.

### Doesn't transfer
The literary analysis itself (parsing a specific interview quote, Harry Potter's decade-long incremental-book structure, the retconned-lore controversy) is criticism of finished, professionally-written works — 11-13-year-olds don't need the critical apparatus, just the resulting rule. The "hard worldbuilding" pole (invented grammar/phonetics) is explicitly not something to aim for in this class.

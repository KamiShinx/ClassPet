# What Makes a Great Hub World? - How Mario 64, Spyro, and Hades Made Theirs (Design Doc, 18.4 min)

**What it is:** Essay defining hub worlds as "fancy menus" that can additionally serve tutorialization, story, style, exploration, and secrets — with paired good/bad examples per category.
**Substance:** substantive — a real taxonomy (5 categories) applied consistently across 10+ named games, both positive and negative case studies, plus one Milanote sponsor read (~90s, skippable).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:29] Reductionist framing: hub worlds are fundamentally menus; the best ones additionally deliver on some mix of Tutorialization, Story, Style, Exploration, and Secrets — not all are required.
- [0:03:04]-[0:03:35] Tutorialization: hub as low-stakes space to teach controls before real stakes (Spyro's Artisan world teaches jump height, glide, flame range via implicit micro-challenges, not a direct tutorial).
- [0:04:40] Named failure case: Ty the Tasmanian Tiger's hub is empty connective space between level portals that teaches nothing and feels "purposeless" — explicitly cited as what a hub looks like without tutorialization intent.
- [0:06:19] RPG hubs (Paper Mario) do story/denouement work instead — a grounding point between chapters that can hold an ongoing background subplot (a family's story) that progresses whether or not the player engages with it.
- [0:07:54] Hubs should visibly change with major story beats to avoid staleness (Super Paper Mario's growing void); negative counter-case again is Origami King's static Toad Town — the same static-NPC flaw named independently in the RPG-town video (uXleufh2mY0).
- [0:08:58] Hades' hub gives small rewards and new dialogue even after a failed run, directly tied to the roguelite loop — softens failure.
- [0:10:11] Cautionary case: Fire Emblem Three Houses' Garreg Mach hub is so large, with calendar-limited daily actions, that full completion becomes a chore on repeat playthroughs — bigger/denser hub isn't automatically better.
- [0:11:53]-[0:13:57] Exploration/secrets: Mario 64's varied level-entry gimmicks (paintings, stained-glass windows, hidden black squares) keep re-traversing the hub from feeling monotonous; secrets are placed to reward re-inspection of specific rooms.
- [0:16:10] Same-franchise before/after: Mario Galaxy's Comet Observatory is visually gorgeous but has almost nothing to find — cited as the "inefficient menu" failure mode even with high production values.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Hub job card" worksheet** (companion to the town one): pick which of the 5 categories (tutorialize / story / style / exploration / secrets) a hub structure should serve — 15 min.
- **"One empty room" audit**: kids check their own hub build against the Ty/Comet-Observatory failure mode — does every room/area have at least one secret, NPC, or mechanic being taught? Reusable weekly.
- **Reuse the 4-way taxonomy graphic directly** as a printed reference sheet — it's already built, no redrawing needed.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Hub-as-tutorial maps directly onto a "starter build" structure that safely demonstrates a kid's new item/mob before the "real" world — buildable as a designated safe zone plus pickups, no new systems needed. Hub-as-secrets (Mario 64) maps onto placing multiple hidden entrances into a kid's structure/dimension — straightforward structure-block work. "Hub visibly changes over time" is harder — needs an advancement-triggered block-state change; buildable but a stretch, flag as advanced/optional.

### Doesn't transfer
Three Houses' calendar/limited-actions system needs a full scheduling/UI layer that's out of scope for this course.

## Honest caveats
This video's negative examples (Ty, Comet Observatory, Three Houses) are as useful as its positive ones — a good basis for a "spot the bad hub" class exercise rather than just "copy the good hub." Nothing here is oversold; the cautions (bigger isn't better, pretty isn't enough) are stated plainly.

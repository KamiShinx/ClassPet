# How to Keep Players Engaged (Without Being Evil) (Game Maker's Toolkit, 10.8 min)

**What it is:** Mark Brown's essay on what makes players WANT to keep playing (pacing, novelty, mystery, long-term goals, difficulty) as distinct from dark-pattern "addictive" design he explicitly refuses to cover.
**Substance:** substantive: dense, well-structured, each point paired with a specific named example and a clear mechanism for why it works — the strongest "why do mechanics feel compelling" video in the batch alongside Extra Credits.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:08]-[0:01:42] "Gameplay pillars": a game's distinct activity types (combat, climbing, puzzles, cutscenes in Uncharted); switching between them before the player gets bored of any one keeps pacing fresh.
- [0:01:42]-[0:02:47] Modulate intensity as well as type — alternate calm and intense sections rather than sustaining max intensity (exhausting) or max calm (boring); Uncharted 2's chapter-by-chapter intensity curve given as a worked example.
- [0:02:47]-[0:03:17] Open-world games can't script pacing the same way — solution is giving the player many activity types so THEY can self-modulate their own pacing (generic but correctly caveated).
- [0:03:17]-[0:03:49] Novelty: constantly introducing new areas/mechanics/enemy types keeps players engaged even without gameplay-type diversity (Mario's stage-to-stage variety cited).
- [0:03:49]-[0:04:55] Mystery/anticipation/foreshadowing: tease something the player can't do yet (The Witness's unsolvable door, Dark Souls' Sen's Fortress gate, Hollow Knight's visible-but-locked upgrade gates) — this "burns slower" than actually dropping new content, so it's a cheap way to extend engagement between real content drops.
- [0:05:31]-[0:07:36] Long-term goals broken into components: implicit dream-goal (Stardew Valley's "build a great farm"), short-term milestones along the way, player-directed planning/strategy (not mindless grinding), and the "exponential growth" feedback loop (resources -> better tools -> more resources), cited via Stardew Valley and Factorio.
- [0:08:08] Skill trees work partly because players fantasize about the payoff moment of having everything unlocked, not just because unlocking is intrinsically satisfying.
- [0:08:20]-[0:09:09] Difficulty/flow: challenge must sit in the zone between boring and stressful; failure itself isn't bad if runs are short, players feel improvement each time, and each attempt varies (cites Tetris, Spelunky, and RE4's dynamic difficulty).
- [0:09:09] Different challenge types beyond reflexes: problem-solving, spatial awareness, decision-making — varying WHICH skill is tested also aids pacing.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Pillar map" worksheet (20 min)**: kid lists the 3-4 activity types their mod content touches (mine/fight/build/explore/trade) and marks which ones repeat too long without a break — directly usable alongside the earlier batch's checklists (MDA, risk/reward dial), and adds the piece those didn't have: pacing between activity types over a play session, not just within one mechanic.
- **"Teaser, not reveal" structure spec (15 min)**: kid designs one locked door/structure/ability gate in their mod that's visible early but needs something found later — cheap in NeoForge (a structure that spawns but requires a custom item to open), and gives a concrete answer to "how do I make my mod's content feel connected across weeks" rather than a pile of disconnected items.
- **"Short-term / long-term goal pairing" worksheet (15 min)**: for any mod system with a grind (custom ore, custom mob drops), kid writes one implicit long-term dream ("build the ultimate base") and 2-3 short-term milestones toward it, echoing Stardew Valley's structure — reusable for the class's overall "each kid's insane lore and mechanics by year's end" goal, not just one mechanic.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Very well — Minecraft already IS a pillars-plus-long-term-goals game (mine/build/fight/explore, with implicit long-term goals like "beat the Ender Dragon" or "build a mega-base"), so nearly every idea here describes an existing Minecraft structure a kid's mod content can plug into rather than invent from scratch. The exponential-growth loop (Factorio: resource -> better tool -> more resource) is exactly how a custom ore-to-tool progression chain would work as a NeoForge mod.

### Doesn't transfer
The "different challenge types" point (spatial/problem-solving/decision-making beyond reflexes) is true but too abstract for an 11-13-year-old to act on directly without a worked example the video doesn't provide — flag as a discussion point, not a worksheet.

## Honest caveats
No contradictions with other sources in this batch; this video's "novelty/mystery" point independently confirms Jesse Ross's (qIiViwg_tTg) observation that an empty, promising space creates its own hook — worth noting as cross-video agreement. Everything here is presented as observed pattern across successful games, not tested/measured — treat as strong design intuition, not proven science (same caveat as MDA elsewhere in this project).

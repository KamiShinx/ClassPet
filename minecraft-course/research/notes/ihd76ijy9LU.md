# Promise, Progress, Payoff - Plot Theory: Brandon Sanderson's Writing Lecture #2 (Brandon Sanderson, 76 min)

**What it is:** Sanderson's core plot-theory lecture: Big P/little p plot, and his central model that plot = promise + progress + payoff, with worked examples from his own books and well-known films.
**Substance:** substantive: this is the dense, structured lecture of the batch. Almost every minute ties back to the promise/progress/payoff framework with concrete before/after examples (Oathbringer rewrite, LOTR films, Guardians of the Galaxy).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:48] Big P Plot (the one-sentence "what's it about" answer) vs. little p plot (the chapter-by-chapter problems that keep a reader engaged) — two different scales of the same thing.
- [0:04:28] Plot exists to (1) create conflict and (2) give the reader/player structure — a sense that things are moving and building.
- [0:05:43] Sameness of underlying plot structure (Hamlet = Lion King = Dune) doesn't make stories feel unoriginal; the specific setting/character dressing is what makes it feel fresh. Reused structure is fine and expected.
- [0:09:09] Core model: **plot = promise + progress + payoff**, and this holds whether you outline or discover as you write.
- [0:11:14] Diagnostic use: when a story "isn't working," it is usually a mismatch between what you promised and what you're actually delivering (progress) — go back and re-promise, or re-align progress.
- [0:17:17] "Killing your darlings": most abandoned plot threads weren't actually that good; you tell yourself you'll use it elsewhere later, and 9/10 times you don't (and that's fine).
- [0:20:04] Promises matter more than a punchy first line — readers decide whether to continue mostly from the first few pages, not the first sentence. (He notes his own name/reputation lets him get away with a weak opening — a "trust" shortcut a new/anonymous creator doesn't have.)
- [0:25:01] **Tone promise**: the opening should signal what kind of experience this is (funny, dark, whimsical) through concrete details — naming choices ("Hogwarts"/"Dumbledore" signal whimsy), opening imagery, prologue mood.
- [0:29:52] **Cold open / "story in microcosm"**: a short opening sequence (e.g. Indiana Jones' opening heist) previews the shape of the whole experience in miniature before the "real" story begins.
- [0:33:41] A good opener often shows what the character wants, what they need, and why they can't have it yet — the seed of the central conflict.
- [0:41:59] Promises scale with length: a short piece must state its promise almost immediately and hit it hard; a long piece can spend multiple chapters/prologues making different promises (tone, plot, character) before the "real" start.
- [0:44:42] **Progress is fully author-controlled** — you decide how fast time passes and what "counts" as movement; a story can skip years in a sentence or spend 50 pages on one second. The skill is not making things progress, but making progress feel satisfying and signposted to the audience.
- [0:48:02] Different plots are tracked by different progress currencies, and you should match the currency to your climax: **mystery/information plots** track clues discovered/suspects eliminated; **relationship plots** (buddy movies, romance) track the relationship's ups and downs toward a climactic bond.
- [0:50:50] Long-form stories often run multiple plot layers at once (A-plot/B-plot) — identify which one is the "soul" of the story and pay it off at the true climax (Guardians of the Galaxy: ostensibly "stop the McGuffin," actually "can these people become friends" — the emotional plot, not the McGuffin, resolves the climax).
- [0:54:10] Diagnosing "boring"/"I got lost" sections: usually the signposts of progress toward the promised goal are missing — a subplot or diversion (he cites The Last Jedi's "Canto Bight"/horse-freeing detour) reads as pointless because it doesn't visibly serve the promise.
- [0:55:52] Sanderson's own Oathbringer example (drawn on the board): characters were diverted from point B to point C; readers found it boring until he added a promise-based reason the diversion mattered (a character believes someone will die if they don't reach point C) — turning a random detour into a stakes-driven choice fixed reader engagement.
- [1:04:34] **Twist mechanism ("toy car vs. toy plane")**: promise someone a plain outcome, then, through the story, make them start wanting a different outcome, then deliver the second thing — a satisfying twist, versus escalation (over-delivering on the same promise) or a straight fulfilled promise (no twist needed, e.g. Pride and Prejudice).
- [1:10:02] **Payoff = "surprising yet inevitable" fulfillment of a promise** — achieved via obstacles/escalations/red herrings that make the audience doubt the promised outcome will land, then delivering it anyway.
- [1:11:38] Worked example (LOTR films, Helm's Deep vs. Minas Tirith): the payoff that was explicitly promised and then nearly forgotten in the darkest moment ("Look for me on the morning of the fifth day") lands as a huge emotional beat; a payoff with no earlier promise (Army of the Dead at Minas Tirith) reads as merely convenient, not earned.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Promise card" for every mod feature**: before a kid builds an item/mob/structure, they fill a one-line card: "When a player sees/picks up/meets this, what am I promising them?" (e.g., a glowing sword promises power; a locked door promises a secret behind it). Directly operationalizes tone promise + tells them what Gemini needs to deliver on. 10 min per feature.
- **"Point B to point C" detour check**: a worksheet question for any quest/dungeon design: "Does every room/step visibly move the player toward what we promised them, or does it feel like a side errand?" If it's a detour, add a one-line reason tied to the promise (Sanderson's actual fix). Great fit for quest design review, 15-20 min.
- **Toy car / toy plane twist exercise**: kids pick one item or boss fight and design it so the player expects reward A (write it down) but by the end wants reward B more (signpost it along the way) — a concrete, buildable version of his twist mechanic. Good for an "advanced" unit once basics are down.
- **Progress signposting for exploration**: for kids building a dungeon/quest chain, require at least one clear signal to the player of "how close am I" (a torch count, an NPC line, a visual landmark getting closer) — mirrors "we're getting closer, Watson."

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
This is the strongest lecture in the batch for the class's stated goal. Promise/progress/payoff maps unusually well onto game/quest design, arguably better than onto prose: a promise is a visual/mechanical cue (glowing item, ominous structure, NPC dialogue), progress is measured in concrete player-visible currency (keys found, floors descended, mobs defeated) exactly like his "mystery = clues" and "relationship = ups and downs" currencies, and payoff is the loot/boss/reveal a level was building toward. "Worldbuilding is the least important of the three" (plot/character beat worldbuilding) is also directly useful pushback against the course's worldbuilding-heavy plan — he'd say a mod with amazing lore but no sense of promise/progress/payoff in its quests will still feel hollow to play.

### Doesn't transfer
The mechanics of prose craft specific to novels/films: managing promises across a multi-book series, chapter-length pacing (thriller short chapters vs. literary slow chapters), the Latinate-vs-Germanic word-choice register discussion (not in this lecture, but adjacent prose craft generally), and anything requiring the player to sit through pure narration — a Minecraft mod's "story" is delivered through play, not paragraphs, so the delivery mechanism (not the underlying promise/progress/payoff logic) has to be entirely reinvented as item descriptions, structure layout, mob behaviour and redstone/quest triggers.

## Honest caveats
Sanderson is explicit that this is a Western-storytelling-tradition model (a student asks directly [0:11:46] and he agrees, noting Eastern payoff conventions differ) — worth remembering before treating promise/progress/payoff as a universal law. His examples are all adult, R/PG-13-adjacent media (Game of Thrones beheading, Into the Woods) chosen for a college audience; none of the specific examples are usable with 11-13-year-olds as-is, only the underlying mechanic. My mapping of "progress currency" onto quest design (keys/floors/mob kills) is my own extrapolation, not something Sanderson says — he only discusses information and relationship currencies.

# Every Assignment I Had in My Character Design Class (Kirbs, 19.9 min)

**What it is:** A former animation-major recaps every assignment from his college "visual development and
character design" class (11 numbered assignments: anatomy, caricature, heroes, film/TV, vehicles & robots,
animals, villains, style imitation, environments, props, architecture, outfits, monsters), showing his own
homework plus reference images and a few named design tips.

**Substance:** mixed. It's a real syllabus with real structure, but each assignment gets 60-90 seconds of
talking-head recap plus 1-2 stills of his own sketches; there's no depth on execution, just "here's what the
assignment was, here's roughly what I made." Genuinely useful as a LIST OF PROMPTS, not as craft instruction.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:32] Assignment 0, Anatomy: draw a character in a form-fitting outfit to check anatomy/gesture skills before
  stylizing. (generic, standard life-drawing warm-up)
- [0:02:12] Assignment 1, Caricature: exaggerate a real person's (or existing character's) features; explicitly
  framed as training for shape-language / exaggeration skill, not just a joke assignment.
- [0:03:50] Names "shape language" directly: circles = soft/friendly, squares = solid/sturdy, triangles =
  pointy/dangerous. Cites Inside Out's emotion designs as the clean example. (generic but foundational)
- [0:04:25] Assignment 2, Heroes: design a "hero asset" — the character the player/viewer looks at constantly.
  Defines the test as: does the character read from silhouette alone, zoomed out? Optional twist: design a
  hero + a sidekick/helper as a contrasting pair.
- [0:07:08] Assignment 3, Film & TV: design one character for film, one for TV, aimed at a stated target audience/
  demographic; his professor's diagnostic question for a failing design was simply "who is this for?"
- [0:08:48] Assignment 4, Vehicles & Robots: design a vehicle or robot; tip is "greeble" (small mechanical surface
  detail) to sell a design as high-tech without needing real engineering knowledge.
- [0:11:09] Assignment 5, Animals: redesign an animal as a character (anthropomorphized, Zootopia-style, not
  realistic); reference real animal anatomy since it differs from human anatomy.
- [0:11:41] Assignment 6, Villains: villain shape language skews angular/pointy; a good villain design should
  radiate power/authority and "something going on beneath the surface," not necessarily overt scariness.
- [0:12:15] If stuck designing visually, write first: list descriptive words or a mini-backstory for the character,
  then translate those feelings into shapes/angles. (a named professor tip, useful and concrete)
- [0:13:20] In-class exercise: given reference concept art from an existing film (Brave), draw an original
  character that could belong in that same world/art style — practicing style-matching, not personal style.
- [0:13:55] Assignment 7, Environments ("organic population"): before designing a whole biome/forest, design one
  small clustered vignette of plants/rocks/nature first — a "slice" that establishes scale and detail density.
- [0:15:04] Iteration principle: first pass is not your best pass — do multiple passes, vary shape/color, get
  feedback. (generic but explicitly drilled "on day one" by his professor)
- [0:16:08] Assignment 8, Props: objects (weapon, backpack, etc.) should visually match and characterize their
  owner; a prop is a chance to add extra characterization for free.
- [0:16:41] Assignment 9, Architecture: draw a building/interior; tip — block out rough 3D shapes in a 3D tool
  (he used Blender) first to get perspective right before drawing on top, even as a 2D artist.
- [0:17:13] Assignment "Costume Progression": take one existing character and design multiple outfits for
  different story contexts (everyday vs. performance, etc.); clothes communicate character just like props do.
- [0:17:52] Assignment 11, Monsters: invent an original creature/alien/mythical beast, bonus points for creepy;
  tip — reference real creature anatomy (e.g., praying mantis) even for an invented monster.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Trait Card before design** (from [0:12:15] and the evil-queen index card at [0:12:48]): kid writes 4-6 bullet
  traits for their mob/character (obsession, physical quirk, a rule about how they act) BEFORE opening Blockbench.
  ~10 min. Directly produces the tooltip/lore text and guides the texture/shape choices. Very low floor — no
  drawing required at all.
- **Hero Silhouette Check** (from [0:04:58]-[0:05:31], the "recognizable from silhouette alone" test): after a kid
  blocks out their Blockbench mob, flip to a dark/unlit render or squint at it — can classmates guess what it is
  from outline alone? ~5 min per mob, works as a paired peer-check activity.
- **One-Line Audience Question** (from [0:07:08], "who is this for?"): a standing discipline question the teacher
  asks whenever a design isn't working — is this mob a friendly ambient creature, a mini-boss, a joke enemy?
  Forces scope clarity fast. (generic, but cheap and reusable weekly)
- **Prop/Item Matches Owner** (from [0:16:08]): when a kid designs a companion item/weapon for their mob or
  player class, it should visually and functionally echo the character's trait card, not be generic. Ties
  directly into item-design work later in the course.
- **Monster/Mob Assignment as-is** (from [0:17:52]): "invent an original creature, reference one real animal for
  its anatomy" maps almost one-to-one onto designing a Minecraft mob. Good as a standalone week-one design prompt.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Silhouette-first thinking survives essentially unchanged and may even matter MORE in Minecraft: distant,
  blocky, low-res rendering makes silhouette almost the only thing that reads at all. This is the single most
  transferable idea in the video.
- Shape language (circle/square/triangle → mood) survives directly into Blockbench cube arrangement: a mob built
  from rounded/thick cube clusters reads soft, one built from thin angular protrusions reads dangerous — this is
  literally achievable with cubes, no drawing skill needed.
- "Write traits before designing" survives perfectly and probably works even better for non-drawers, since it's
  the one assignment format that requires zero art skill.
- The "reference real animal anatomy" idea becomes "reference how a real animal moves/is shaped" for silhouette
  and hitbox proportions in Blockbench — loose but usable.
- Greeble (surface detail for machines) has a rough parallel: adding small cube "doodads" to make an item/block
  look more mechanical without needing an explanation, though 16x16 textures limit how much surface noise reads
  at all — likely needs to be attempted and shown to be judged.

### Doesn't transfer
- Caricature (Assignment 1) needs actual drawing/likeness skill and a real face to exaggerate — no clean
  Minecraft equivalent and not worth class time for non-drawing kids.
- Film vs. TV budget/style distinction (Assignment 3's back half) is a 2D animation production concept
  (budget-driven simplicity) with no Minecraft parallel.
- The Blender-blockout-before-drawing tip (architecture assignment) doesn't apply — kids build structures
  directly in a blocky medium (Minecraft/Blockbench), there's no "draw perspective" step to shortcut.
- Costume progression (multiple outfits per story beat) is likely too much scope for an 8-kid, 20-week course
  where each kid needs one working mod concept; a stretch goal at best, not core.
- Style imitation (draw in someone else's established art style) has no real Minecraft-mod analogue and needs
  drawing skill this class doesn't assume.

## Honest caveats
- This is one guy's memory of one college class, presented as a checklist, not vetted design theory — treat the
  assignment list as inspiration for prompts, not as an authority on what's essential.
- He explicitly says he "lied" about the class being pure character design (it was visual development, broader);
  several assignments (environments, architecture, vehicles) are general visual-dev, not character-specific —
  worth remembering when deciding whether "character design" content alone covers what Ben wants.
- No coverage at all of what makes a MECHANIC or behavior fun — this is 100% appearance-design, confirming Ben's
  suspicion that character-design videos alone won't cover the "game design" half he flagged as separately
  important.
- Several tips are stated as absolutes ("shape language is built into humans") with no evidence beyond personal
  classroom experience — treat as directionally useful convention, not scientific fact.

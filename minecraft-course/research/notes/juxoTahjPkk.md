# A Comprehensive Guide to World Building (everything) (Stoneworks, 24.3 min)

**What it is:** A 5-part structured guide, shown on screen as a numbered outline, covering purpose, science-vs-mythology, premise/theme, map-starting method, and "3 rules of wise worldbuilding" — the most systematically organized, procedure-shaped video in the batch.
**Substance:** substantive — dense, on-topic throughout, explicitly structured as a numbered guide rather than a rambling essay, and it gives a genuine step-by-step "where to start" answer.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:55] Purpose determines method: 3 use cases — sandbox (just for yourself, no rules needed), story/novel, or tabletop RPG — and each should be worldbuilt differently; identify which one you're doing first.
- [0:03:37] For story worldbuilders specifically: "the world is not your story" — build only forward-facing facades (like a film set), not a fully fleshed-out background; names "world builder's disease" explicitly as the trap of building endlessly instead of writing (independently converges with the Sins video's Gluttony).
- [0:05:37] For TTRPG worldbuilders: build "strings" (pre-existing tools/relationships players can tug on) rather than a fixed linear plot, since players are unpredictable; keep stock NPC names/encounters ready for improvisation (not relevant to this class — no tabletop component).
- [0:07:59] Science vs. mythology is a dial, not a binary — his own approach: a little real-world plausibility (loosely-informed geography), then "screw it up" with mythology/magic for cool factor; the only real requirement is that it's understandable to your audience.
- [0:08:59] Core starting technique: pick a premise/theme FIRST — even just a few words ("barbarians on giant war dogs, everyone wears fur") — because a theme immediately answers concrete worldbuilding questions (what's on the road between cities?) and disqualifies infinite other options, which is what makes it useful.
- [0:10:47] Map-starting rule: build ONE region first, leave the rest vague — drawing a full detailed world map before you know what's interesting about your world is a form of world builder's disease; his own example (a mouse civilization using human trash as "magic" material) shows how details discovered mid-build reshape the world, which a locked-in full map prevents.
- [0:13:08]-[0:17:51] ~17 rapid-fire real-world geography rules for making a hand-drawn map look plausible: peninsula/archipelago shapes, mountain range placement, rain-shadow deserts at ~30° latitude, rivers merge downhill and don't split (except at deltas), city placement logic (farmland/water/defensible hills/trade routes), state border types (tribes vs. city-states vs. empires) — mostly Earth-geography trivia for 2D map-drawing, not directly relevant since Minecraft terrain is generated/built, not hand-drawn.
- [0:18:37] "3 rules of wise worldbuilding," shown as a title card: (1) **Rule of Cool** — the cooler something is, the more the audience forgives/doesn't notice inconsistency; good worldbuilding often starts with a cool idea, then reverse-engineers the logic to support it. (2) **Rule of Consistency** — stay inside your own tone/theme once you've set audience expectations (his example: a kid-friendly D&D campaign shouldn't suddenly turn extremely violent) — matches the Sins video's Wrath point about tonal escalation. (3) **Rule of Uniqueness** — derivative ideas are fine as long as the audience can't immediately recognize the source (an unaltered plot beat, or a map that's obviously a traced real country, breaks immersion) — closely matches the Sins video's Envy sin.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Theme-in-5-words" starter card** (10-15 min): each kid writes a 3-6 word theme/vibe for their world (his format: "barbarians on war dogs, everyone wears fur") and lists 2-3 things that theme rules out. The single best "where do you start" activity in the batch — concrete, fast, and it visibly narrows the blank-page problem 11-13-year-olds hit.
- **"One region only" build rule**: kids can only build/detail ONE named place (one biome, one village, one structure) for the first several weeks — anti-"world builder's disease" rule, lifted directly from the map-starting section, reinforced by the Sins video's Gluttony.
- **"Cool thing, then explain it" exercise**: kid picks one cool image/idea first (a purple biome, a mob that explodes into butterflies), then answers "why would this exist in my world?" afterward — matches his own creative process and is friendlier to 11-13-year-olds than "plan the logic first."
- **"Does this feel like MY world or someone else's" gut-check**, applied to a kid's finished item/structure/character before showing the group — direct lift of the Rule of Uniqueness, phrased for peer/teacher review rather than internet-critique culture.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The purpose/theme-first method transfers cleanly and is medium-agnostic. "One region first, not the whole map" is arguably a better fit for Minecraft than for prose, since Minecraft worlds are built block by block — a kid literally cannot build "the whole world" in weekly 65-minute sessions, so the rule matches the medium's real constraint, not just a craft preference. The Rule of Cool -> reverse-engineer-the-logic approach matches how vibecoding will actually work: kids will describe a cool mechanic to Gemini first and only need to explain "why" it exists afterward.

### Doesn't transfer
The TTRPG "strings for unpredictable players" section (no tabletop component in this course); nearly all 17 rapid-fire real-world geography/cartography rules (rain shadows, river deltas, empire border types) are craft for hand-drawn 2D fantasy maps — Minecraft terrain is generated or hand-built in 3D, not drawn as a map, so this whole segment is close to a dead end here, useful at most as background flavor if a kid wants their build's placement to "make ecological sense."

## Honest caveats
The cold open ("add your kink, add a crazy magic system, recreate your home country with double the power") is a joke, not real advice, and the video has crude humor throughout (bleeped profanity) — the teacher would need to pull only the outline/rules content and skip the framing. Otherwise this is the least padded, most structurally reusable video in the batch and the strongest single answer to the batch's special-focus question.

# The Tools to Make Your Worlds Better - Lecture #8 (Brandon Sanderson, 76 min)

**What it is:** BYU "How to Write Science Fiction and Fantasy" lecture, week 2 of worldbuilding. First half:
worldbuilding tools (brainstorm categories, worldbuilder's disease, the iceberg, pyramid of abstraction, standing
in the way of plot). Second half (from ~53:00): a separate topic, viewpoint and tense (first/third/omniscient,
past/present), not worldbuilding.

**Substance:** substantive: dense, example-driven craft talk (Mistborn, Stormlight, Dune, Name of the Wind used as
worked examples), not padding, though ~40% of the runtime (viewpoint/tense) is off-topic for us.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:07:09] Split worldbuilding into three buckets when planning: physical setting, cultural setting, magic/system
  (kept separate because it's "a thing for him"). (generic structure, but useful as a template)
- [0:11:56] Physical setting = everything that would exist without sapient beings (geology, weather, flora/fauna,
  biomes, adaptation, cosmology). Cultural setting = everything sapient beings create (tech level, religion, food
  lore, social mores/taboos, law/government, language, gender/class roles, tradition, education, fashion,
  architecture, festivals).
- [0:13:05]-[0:20:17] Live brainstorm: he puts these two headers on a whiteboard and has the class shout categories
  to fill in under each, building the list live (this is the class activity itself, not just an idea about one).
- [0:20:52] Names "worldbuilder's disease": each worldbuilding category is a rabbit hole, and chasing all of them
  before writing paralyzes you and can eat months/years for a single point (he cites Tolkien spending decades on
  languages).
- [0:24:42] His fix: give yourself a hard time budget for worldbuilding, then move to the actual story; don't wait
  until every category is "finished."
- [0:25:14] Cautionary example from his own first novel: he spent his worldbuilding budget on languages (irrelevant
  to a single-city murder mystery) and none on the justice system (which the plot actually needed) — spend budget
  on what the story needs, not what's fun.
- [0:26:58] The iceberg metaphor, reframed as "the hollow iceberg": you should deeply know a *few* things (the tip,
  what's on the page) and merely gesture at the rest (implied depth) rather than researching everything up front —
  over-researching turns prose into an encyclopedia entry ("info dump").
- [0:29:50] You can also legitimately leave some systems totally unexplained if the audience already has the
  convention (FTL, dragons flying) — don't explain what isn't plot-relevant.
- [0:30:57] Dan Wells' trick: describe one concrete detail in high resolution (bullet holes, flickering light,
  broken ceiling fan) and let the audience's brain fill in the rest of the room. (Named, credited technique.)
- [0:33:02] Rule of thumb for what to fully develop: worldbuild the things that influence your characters' decisions
  and the challenges they face; the rest can stay implied.
- [0:34:46] "Pyramid of abstraction" tool: abstract concepts (love) can be more universally understood than
  supposedly "concrete" nouns (a dog — everyone pictures a different one); you pull a concept down the pyramid
  toward the concrete by adding specific, sensory modifiers until readers converge on the same image.
- [0:41:55]-[0:43:29] Applied to worldbuilding scenes: ground the reader in sensory, concrete detail (the sand
  between your teeth, the shimmer of the spice) before/while delivering abstract lore, so lore lands as
  experienced-in-scene rather than as an info dump.
- [0:45:37] Worldbuilding, like a character, is a "worst thing" if it stands in the way of plot/pacing — treat
  it as spinach the reader has to eat only when it's genuinely necessary, and split it up rather than dumping it.
- [0:47:12] "Every chapter should have a chance to be someone's favorite chapter" (his editorial VP's line) — not
  every reader wants the same kind of content, so different worldbuilding-heavy vs. plot-heavy chapters can each
  serve a different audience segment.
- [0:48:47]-[0:53:55] Three naming techniques: (1) pick a real-world region/language you have some tie to and adapt
  its sound (caution: research appropriation risk, don't blend whole cultures as one monolith, be specific e.g. one
  Chinese dynasty not "China"); (2) study linguistics to design a consistent sound system; (3) pick one simple
  linguistic trick (symmetry, repeated initial consonants) and apply it everywhere. Always Google-check names
  before shipping them (risk of accidental slurs/existing words).
- [0:56:46]-[1:15:02] Viewpoint/tense taxonomy (third limited/omniscient, first true/epistolary/storyteller, past
  vs. present) — craft for prose fiction, not applicable to a Minecraft mod (no first/third-person narrator choice
  in gameplay text).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **The Two-Column Brainstorm worksheet**: recreate [0:13:05]-[0:20:17] directly. Give each kid two columns,
  "Physical" (biomes, mobs, weather, resources, hazards) and "Cultural" (structures, loot, factions, rules,
  tooltips/lore text), 10 minutes, fill in as many as possible for their mod's world. ~15-20 min with a
  class-wide share-out. Produces a raw idea bank to mine for the rest of the course.
- **The Time-Boxed Iceberg**: from worldbuilder's disease + hollow iceberg [0:20:52], [0:26:58]. Kid picks
  exactly 3 things about their world to fully design this session (a mob's behavior, one structure, one loot
  table) and explicitly writes one gesture-line for everything else ("there's also a faction of X, but I'm not
  building that yet"). ~20 min. Directly prevents the predictable failure mode of an 11-13-year-old trying to
  design an entire world before touching Blockbench.
- **One Concrete Detail tooltip drill**: from Dan Wells' trick [0:30:57]. For any item/structure, write ONE
  specific, sensory detail in the tooltip/description (not a full lore paragraph) and see if classmates can guess
  the rest of the vibe from it. ~10 min pair exercise. Maps directly onto Minecraft tooltip writing, which is
  inherently short-form.
- **Name Generator mini-lesson**: the three naming tricks [0:48:47] as a 10-minute worksheet — pick a real
  language/region you have a real connection to, or pick one repeatable sound trick (alliteration, symmetry) —
  then Google-check the result. Produces item/mob/place names for the mod.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- Physical/cultural split maps well onto biomes+mobs (physical) vs. structures+loot+NPC dialogue+tooltips
  (cultural) — kids can literally sort their brainstorm list into "what determines the biome/mobs" and "what
  determines the structures/items/text."
- Worldbuilder's disease and the time-boxed iceberg are probably the single most load-bearing ideas for THIS
  class specifically: 65-minute weekly sessions with kids new to any of this are exactly the setup where infinite
  lore-planning replaces actually building the mod. This needs to become a running class discipline, not a
  one-off worksheet.
- The "worldbuilding must not stand in the way of plot" idea [0:45:37] translates to "lore must not stand in the
  way of gameplay" — a good discipline check when a kid wants to write three paragraphs of backstory instead of
  finishing an item.
- Pyramid of abstraction survives only in miniature: not as prose craft, but as "give one concrete sensory detail
  instead of an abstract adjective" for tooltip writing.

### Doesn't transfer
- Viewpoint/tense (all of [0:56:46]-[1:15:02], over 18 minutes) — this is novel-prose narration technique; a
  Minecraft mod has no narrator or viewpoint character in Sanderson's sense. Skip entirely.
- Deep linguistics-based naming (technique #2, learning conjugation systems) — too much of a rabbit hole for an
  11-13-year-old in a 65-minute slot; keep only the "pick a real language and adapt sounds" and "pick one trick"
  options.
- The cultural-appropriation naming discussion, while a genuinely responsible point for a professional novelist,
  is a heavier ethics conversation than this course likely has time for in full; worth one line of guidance
  ("don't just grab a whole real culture's name-set uncritically") rather than the extended treatment he gives it.
- Brainstorming "food lore," "systems of law," "educational systems" etc. in the deep, encyclopedic sense a
  novelist would (e.g. Tolkien spending decades on language) has no output format in a game mod — these only
  transfer as one-line flavor text triggers, not standalone research projects.

## Honest caveats
- The whole viewpoint/tense half of this lecture (roughly 20 of 76 minutes) is not about worldbuilding at all;
  Ben's brief only needs the first half. Flagging so it isn't mistaken for worldbuilding content in a re-watch.
- Sanderson's core audience is adult aspiring novelists writing 100k-word books over years; his time-management
  advice (deadlines, book-a-year pace) doesn't map 1:1 onto an 8-kid, 20-week hobbyist class, but the underlying
  warning (don't over-plan before building) is, if anything, more urgent here given the short session length.
- No evidence in this video of anything about game mechanics/fun (Ben's separate worry about "game design"
  content) — this is a craft-of-prose lecture; it does not cover what makes a mechanic fun to play.

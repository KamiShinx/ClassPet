# How I 'Design Characters' for my Comics? - The Process (PART 2) (KeshArt, 18.7 min)

**What it is:** A comic artist walks through his named 5-step character design process (Idea -> Character Thumbnail -> Research -> Final Sketch -> Inks & Colours) live, designing a "Warrior" character for his mini-comic from a single word to a finished colored design, explicitly framed around ~10 "character design principles."
**Substance:** substantive: a genuine full worked example with clearly named, repeatable sub-principles (Shapes, Clarity, Flow, Simplicity, Representation, Construction, Variation) each demonstrated on the same character as it evolves; ends with a plug for his free 40-page guidebook (not a paid pitch) which is low-friction promotion rather than padding.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:00] Core thesis: the reason people freeze at a blank page isn't lack of drawing skill, it's lack of design PRINCIPLES (a way of thinking) rather than one-off tricks — learning principles lets you design any character, not just copy references (explicit "teach a man to fish" framing).
- [0:01:38] Step 1, **Idea/character brief**: define basics (age, gender, clothing, job, likes/dislikes...) — but explicitly says for a small project you don't need a full detailed brief; a single word (noun/verb/adjective, e.g. "warrior," "farmer," "blacksmith") is enough to start.
- [0:03:17] Step 2, **Character thumbnail**: once you have a rough mental image, draw many fast, rough thumbnails using flat, simple 2D shapes — this is where the "principles" get applied as filters, one at a time.
- [0:03:49] **Principle of Shapes**: every character should be defined by 3-5 core shapes (for a humanoid: head, body, legs, arms) that alone give the design clarity; you can find these 3 key shapes in almost any existing character design.
- [0:05:31] **Principle of Clarity**: keep the design efficient/legible so it "travels well" (reads at a glance) — tested by turning the thumbnail into a solid silhouette: a closed-up silhouette is hard to recognize, an "opened up" one (limbs/shapes separated, not overlapping/merged) reads clearly.
- [0:06:03] **Principle of Flow**: create rhythm via a clear line of action, varied shapes, and thoughtful arrangement of shapes — contrasts a "rigid and stiff" badly-arranged example against a "flowy and rhythmic" well-arranged one.
- [0:06:37] Step 3, **Research**: after the rough thumbnail exists, gather outside inspiration (style realism/abstraction level, visual motifs like how hair or a body part is drawn, rendering style) — explicitly recommends doing research AFTER the rough thumbnail (not before), specifically so the initial idea stays original/unadulterated rather than becoming a "Frankenstein of other people's characters."
- [0:07:43] Practical method: build a Pinterest moodboard mixing OTHER artists' designs (for style/vibe) with REAL-LIFE reference (the actual "source of all design") — his example: Viking reference + a cartoon style reference (Dexter's Lab) combined for a stylized warrior.
- [0:08:50] Step 4, **Final sketch**, refining the thumbnail using more principles:
- [0:09:22] **Principle of Simplicity**: any real/reference subject has many "data points" (hair type, amount of detail, beard shape, eye shape, etc.) — the skill is picking only the ESSENTIAL data points and dropping the rest, demonstrated concretely on a beard (texture removed, only the shape kept) and eyes.
- [0:11:02] Names a mistake explicitly: he added surface detail before fixing the base body shape, and found the base too bland — states the correct order is base/proportion first, THEN detail.
- [0:11:38] **Principle of Representation**: reinterpret each body part's shape rather than drawing it literally — his worked example: torso becomes an oval, lower body shrinks, legs get wider/shorter, arms get exaggerated large, to hit a specific stylized effect.
- [0:12:43] **Principle of Construction**: once a 2D "base shape" is chosen, build actual 3D volume using basic solid forms (spheres, modified cubes, cylinders) — moving from flat shape design to a constructed, dimensional body.
- [0:13:16] **Principle of Variation** (three named sub-types, shown side by side on the same figure): SIZE variation (large/medium/small elements within one design), SHAPE variation (mixing circular, boxy, and angular shapes across the character), and LINE variation (mixing straight and curved lines rather than being all one or the other) — explicitly says this becomes semi-unconscious with practice but is a deliberate check early on.
- [0:14:54] Applies Principle of Simplicity again to individual final details (hair, mustache, clothing folds) — turns complex real-world reference (detailed cloth folds) into simple flat shapes (triangles, half-circles).
- [0:16:00] Step 5, **Inks & colours**: inking is mechanical (brush pen over the sketch, varying line weight thin/thick to preserve the sketch's implied depth) — treated as fast/rote, not a separate design decision.
- [0:16:33] **Color system** (his personal method, named clearly): pick a PRIMARY color (the character's core/standout color), a COMPLEMENTARY color (usually clothes/skin, chosen to pair well with primary), then NECESSARY colors (colors you can't skip — e.g. black hair, white mustache, specific eye color), and finally an optional ACCENT color (a small pop/highlight). Rendering stays simple: flat color layer, then one shadow layer, one highlight layer.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **One-word brief starter (5 min, no drawing)**: kid picks a single word (noun/verb/adjective) for their mob/character — explicitly validated by the video as sufficient for a small project, removing brief-writing anxiety. Comes from [0:01:38].
- **Silhouette "open it up" check (10 min)**: after a first mob sketch/Blockbench pass, turn it into a solid silhouette and ask "can you tell the limbs/parts apart, or did they merge into a blob?" — directly testable even on a rough model. Comes from [0:05:31].
- **3 Variation types checklist**: a simple after-the-fact review card — "does your design have more than one SIZE of part, more than one SHAPE type (round vs. boxy vs. angular), and (for drawing) more than one LINE type?" Comes from [0:13:16]; the size/shape checks transfer to Blockbench directly.
- **Primary/Complementary/Necessary/Accent color worksheet**: kid fills in a 4-box color chart for their character before texturing — a concrete, fill-in-the-blank version of a genuinely professional color system. Comes from [0:16:33]; maps well onto a 16x16 texture's limited palette.
- **"Base shape before details" rule**: a simple posted reminder/checkpoint before kids are allowed to add surface detail (patterns, small features) to a Blockbench model — directly reusable as a workflow gate, drawn from the presenter's own admitted mistake. Comes from [0:11:02].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The Principle of Shapes (3-5 core shapes define a character) and Principle of Clarity (silhouette must read when "opened up," not merged into a blob) both transfer extremely well to Blockbench, arguably better than to illustration — a cube-based model is inherently a small set of core shapes, and "opening up" a cluttered silhouette (e.g., separating arm cubes from the torso rather than letting them merge visually) is a genuinely useful, literal instruction for building a legible mob. The Variation principle (size/shape/line mixing) maps directly onto varying cube sizes and shapes within one model — a very concrete, teachable Blockbench technique for kids with no art background. The color system (primary/complementary/necessary/accent) is one of the most directly reusable ideas in the whole batch for 16x16 pixel texturing, since it forces exactly the kind of economical color decisions a tiny texture demands. "Research after the rough idea, to protect originality" is a good sequencing tip that works with zero drawing skill (kids can gather 2-3 reference images AFTER sketching their concept in words). The Construction principle (build the 2D shape into 3D volume via cylinders/cubes/spheres) is actually a near-literal description of the Blockbench workflow itself — cube- and cylinder-based volume-building is exactly what Blockbench is.

### Doesn't transfer
The Representation principle (freely reinterpreting/exaggerating/distorting anatomical proportions for stylized effect — huge arms, tiny legs, oval torso) requires an artist's eye for what still "reads" as a body once heavily distorted; a non-artist 11-13-year-old is likely to produce something that reads as broken rather than stylized, and Blockbench's cube grid already constrains proportion play more than freehand drawing does, so this needs heavy scaffolding (e.g., pre-set proportion presets) rather than open-ended distortion. The full inking/rendering pipeline (brush pen line-weight control, shadow/highlight layering) is illustration craft with no Blockbench equivalent. The "many data points -> pick the essential ones" Simplicity principle is conceptually sound but is really a REDUCTION skill (going from complex reference to simple shape) — for kids designing directly in a low-resolution medium, the constraint is already built into the tool, so this principle mostly applies to the reference-gathering/brainstorming phase rather than needing to be taught as a drawing technique.

## Honest caveats
This video's named "10 principles" (only Shapes, Clarity, Flow, Simplicity, Representation, Construction, and Variation are actually covered on screen — the other ~3 are referenced but never named) is presented as the presenter's personal framework, not an industry-standard taxonomy, so the specific labels shouldn't be treated as authoritative terminology to test kids on — the underlying concepts (silhouette clarity, shape variety, economical color, base-before-detail) are sound and echoed by multiple other videos in this batch (Proko, Draw like a Sir, Map Crow all independently emphasize silhouette-first design), which is good convergent evidence. No claim here appears factually wrong; the closest to an overstatement is implying a fixed "10 principles" system exists as settled doctrine, when it's really one working artist's personal codification of well-known design fundamentals. This is one of the two or three strongest videos in the batch for direct classroom material, on par with Proko and stronger than the others on the concrete Blockbench-adjacent "base shape / construction / variation" pipeline.

# Character Design Is Easy, Actually (Drelix, 27.8 min)

**What it is:** A webcomic artist's structured "secrets" video: character design should start from writing
(setting + personality), then move to silhouette design (four tools), then shape psychology, then color
psychology, then four techniques for standing out, ending with "overdesign" as the number one beginner mistake.

**Substance:** substantive and unusually well-organized for the batch — it's explicitly built as an answer to
three stated questions and uses his own published webcomic as a worked example throughout, plus a real
"Good Character Design Chart" graphic he references three times as connective tissue. Some sections (full color
psychology rundown) run long and repetitive with many rapid character examples, but the structure itself is
clean and reusable.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:06] Central claim: character design should start with WRITING, not drawing — designs assembled from
  "cool stuff" without narrative grounding become generic ("edgy protagonist #47"); visuals should be rooted in
  who the character already is on the page.
- [0:03:16] Step 1 = Setting, broken into WHEN (past/modern/future) and WHERE (our world / true story / alternate
  world / fully imaginary world) — a simple 2x4 grid a character's design should visibly answer (dropping Batman
  into Bikini Bottom breaks instantly because setting shapes the whole design).
- [0:04:54] Step 2 = Character personality, deliberately simplified against over-complicated personality-test
  approaches (explicitly dismisses Myers-Briggs as overkill): reduces personality to three questions — WHO are
  they (archetype), WHAT do they want (goal), WHY do they want it (motivation). Explicitly says flaws/strengths
  can be added later but these three are the load-bearing core.
- [0:06:29] Worked example: walks his own webcomic character (a bee-human hybrid) through the whole setting +
  who/want/why pipeline, showing how the answers directly generated his suit design (bee coloring, wings) —
  useful as a template a kid could literally copy the steps of.
- [0:09:13] Step 3 = Silhouette, argued as the most important design layer via a personal Ben 10 anecdote (could
  identify aliens from silhouette-only transformation flash); includes a live "guess the silhouette" viewer game.
- [0:09:46] Four named tools for building an iconic silhouette: (1) Physical features — built-in body traits
  that read even nude (Marge's hair, Venom's tongue, Shrek's ears); (2) Proportions — overall body balance
  (height/width/limb length), explicitly distinguished from features using Squidward's two body states as
  example; (3) Accessories — removable props/clothes/weapons (TMNT differentiated purely by weapon silhouette
  despite identical bodies); (4) Body language — stance/movement/posture alone, demonstrated by showing a
  featureless generic silhouette that still reads as Spider-Man purely from his signature crouch pose.
- [0:12:28] Shape psychology, same three-shape framework as the other two batch videos (circle=safe/approachable,
  square=stable/dependable, triangle=sharp/dangerous/unstable) — corroborated a third time in this batch, so this
  is a broadly-agreed convention, not one video's idiosyncratic claim.
- [0:14:41] Explicit "break the rule on purpose" move: assign a shape that CONTRADICTS a character's nature for
  memorable irony (Boss Baby = circular/soft shapes but acts like a ruthless CEO; Powerpuff Girls = round/cute
  but violent). Framed as an intentional, secondary technique, not a beginner default.
- [0:15:46] Step 4 = Color psychology, with a long named-color rundown (red=energy/danger, blue=trust/calm,
  green=nature/toxic/alien, yellow=happiness/notice-me, purple=royalty/power or corrupted-power, white=neutral/
  futuristic/wealth, black=mysterious/powerful/aura) each backed by several rapid character examples. Frames
  color choices as deliberate psychological manipulation the same way brand/logo design uses color (cites
  fast-food red/yellow, health-brand green/brown, Facebook blue-for-trust).
- [0:22:49] Four "stand out" techniques once basics are covered: (1) Exaggeration — push one trait to an absurd
  degree (Generator Rex's giant fists, Squirrel Girl's tail); (2) Mixing ideas — combine unrelated concepts
  (his own bee+Spider-Man+Blue-Beetle mashup); (3) Genre crossing — reuse a trope from a different genre so it
  reads as fresh rather than a ripoff (70s Hulk becoming Muscle Man in a comedy context); (4) Parody with a twist —
  lean into being a clear riff on an existing character but alter it enough to be its own thing (Omni-Man =
  Superman with a mustache).
- [0:25:30] Names "overdesign" as the #1 beginner mistake: too many competing unnecessary details, no clear focal
  read, mixed signals, and (practically) a nightmare to redraw/animate consistently — illustrated via his own
  negative reaction to Hazbin Hotel's cluttered designs and Thor: Love and Thunder's busy redesign vs. Ragnarok's
  cleaner one.
- [0:26:35] Concrete self-test for overdesign: "if you can't sketch it 50 times without rage-quitting, it's too
  much" — ties directly back to repeatability/consistency as a practical production constraint, not just taste.

## What the frames add
This video visualizes its own framework more than any other in the batch and is worth mining for reusable
worksheet templates directly. Key visual assets: (1) [0:01:28], [0:09:20], [0:16:00] — a recurring "Good
Character Design Chart" table (columns: Character Writing / Silhouette Segment / Shape Language / Colour
Psychology) that structures the whole video; the column headers are legible even though the cell text is too
small/blurred to transcribe — still useful as a template shape for a class worksheet. (2) [0:05:44] a clean
three-circle Venn diagram ("Who are they / What do they want / Why do they want it") — directly reusable as a
kid worksheet graphic. (3) [0:04:16]/[0:06:48] the "Setting 101" Venn (When: past/modern/future crossed with
Where: our world/alternate/imaginary) — another directly reusable worksheet template. (4) [0:09:52]-[0:12:24] a
four-quadrant color wheel diagram labeled Physical Features / Proportions / Body Language / Accessories, shown
repeatedly — visualizes the four-tools silhouette framework as a literal wheel a kid could fill in per mob.
(5) [0:17:36]-[0:17:44] a labeled color wheel titled "Colour Psychology" — a clean reference chart. These five
diagrams together are essentially a ready-made worksheet packet; a teacher could screenshot them directly rather
than re-deriving the structure.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Who/Want/Why card**: the three-circle Venn from [0:05:44], filled in for a kid's mob or character in one
  sentence each. ~10 min. This is probably the single best entry-point worksheet in the whole batch — it's
  concrete, fast, requires no drawing, and its outputs (archetype/goal/motivation) are exactly what feeds both
  the mob's tooltip text AND its behavior spec for Gemini.
- **Setting Grid**: the When x Where Venn from [0:04:16], filled in once per kid's whole world (not per mob) —
  establishes the world's time period and reality-relationship in one 10-minute pass, useful as an early-course
  worldbuilding anchor that then constrains every later character design.
- **Four-Tool Silhouette Wheel worksheet**: recreate the quadrant wheel from [0:09:52] — for each mob, kid notes
  one physical feature, one proportion choice, one accessory, and one body-language/pose cue. Directly maps onto
  Blockbench model decisions (which limb to exaggerate, what item to hold, what stance the idle animation uses).
  ~15 min per mob.
- **Guess-the-Silhouette game**: recreate [0:09:13]'s viewer game as a classroom activity — show mob silhouettes
  only, have classmates guess which is which; corroborates the silhouette-first emphasis from both other batch
  videos with a ready-made game format.
- **Overdesign Self-Check**: adapt [0:26:35]'s "50 times without rage-quitting" test into "would this readable at
  16x16 pixels and 30 blocks away?" — a standing question before a kid finalizes a texture, functionally serving
  the same discipline role but rephrased for the actual medium constraint.
- **Stand-Out Technique menu**: when a kid's mob feels generic, offer the four techniques (exaggeration, mixing
  ideas, genre crossing, parody-with-a-twist) as a quick menu of fixes rather than "just make it better" — usable
  as a teacher prompt during 1:1 check-ins.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
- The Who/Want/Why writing-first approach survives essentially unchanged and is arguably the best-fitting single
  idea across the whole batch for this course: it needs no drawing, produces both narrative flavor text AND
  mechanical direction in one pass, and scales down cleanly to a 10-minute activity — a strong candidate for
  literally the first design worksheet of the course.
- The four-tools-for-silhouette framework (features/proportions/accessories/body language) maps unusually well
  onto Blockbench specifically, since Blockbench models are BUILT from exactly these kinds of discrete, addable
  parts (a cube for a feature, a resized limb for proportion, a held item for accessory, an idle-pose choice for
  body language) — more directly buildable than 2D drawing.
- Shape psychology (triangle=danger etc.) is now corroborated by THREE independent videos in this batch — treat
  it as a safe, well-established convention to teach kids, not one video's pet theory.
- "Break the rule on purpose" (cute shape + evil behavior) is a nice twist for a kid whose mob otherwise reads
  too on-the-nose, and requires zero extra tooling — just an intentional mismatch note on the trait card.
- Genre crossing / parody-with-a-twist survive as writing techniques for a kid stuck for an original idea (take a
  known creature type and reskin it into their world's setting) — very achievable without design skill.

### Doesn't transfer
- The full color-psychology rundown (7+ colors, dozens of rapid character citations) is too long and reference-
  heavy to teach as a lecture to 11-13-year-olds; better distilled to a one-page cheat sheet (3-4 colors, one
  keyword each) than taught in the video's full form.
- Overdesign-as-redrawability ("sketch it 50 times") is a hand-drawn-animation production constraint; the literal
  test doesn't apply to a 3D voxel model, though the underlying "keep it simple, complexity has a cost" lesson
  does transfer once reworded (see the 16x16-pixel Overdesign Self-Check reframe above).
- Comparing designs against a large personal back-catalogue of existing IP (Omni-Man vs. Superman, Muscle Man vs.
  70s Hulk) assumes broad pop-culture/comics literacy the kids may not fully share, and depends on media some of
  it copyright-sensitive to lean on directly in class materials — use the TECHNIQUE (parody-with-a-twist) without
  necessarily requiring the same reference pool.
- The full "personality test" digression dismissing Myers-Briggs is really just a rhetorical setup for simplifying
  to Who/Want/Why — nothing here needs to be taught to kids beyond the three-question distillation itself.

## Honest caveats
- Like the Kirbs and BaM videos, this is entirely appearance/identity design; it does not address mechanics,
  balance, or "what makes something fun to play" — the Concord/Blaze video in this batch is still the only one
  that touches actual game-design function.
- The video's own examples lean heavily on existing IP redesign/critique (Thor, Hazbin Hotel) rather than
  original-from-scratch design walkthroughs beyond his own single webcomic character — the "worked example" is
  thinner than the amount of theory around it suggests.
- Cultural framing note from the creator himself [0:07:02]: he preemptively defends his choice to depict Black
  Kenyan characters against apparently-recurring viewer pushback — not itself design theory, but the video author
  flags it as a real audience-reaction issue worth being aware of if any of his footage/clips get shown directly
  to students.
- No mention anywhere of playtesting, iteration with peers, or revision cycles (contrast with the Kirbs video's
  explicit "iteration" tip) — this video presents its framework as close to a one-pass formula, which risks
  reading as more prescriptive/complete than character design (per BaM's own video) actually is.

# What Makes A Good Game Companion? (Design Doc, 19.3 min)

**What it is:** A taxonomy of the ROLES a sidekick/companion character can play in a game (guide, living UI, ability source, comic foil, protagonist-in-disguise, etc.), with examples of each done well and badly.
**Substance:** substantive: longest and most systematic video in the batch, though the second half drifts into pure narrative/character-writing territory that's less mechanical.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [2:18] Sidekicks default into being nags/boat-anchors unless their responsibility is deliberately scoped; the more roles a sidekick is asked to fill, the easier it is to dislike.
- [2:48] Guide/hint role done badly = interrupting constantly with info the player already has (Navi in Ocarina of Time); done better = a quiet, opt-in cue (Tatl's bell chime, later HD Skyward Sword making hints optional).
- [4:53] Guide/hint role fails the other direction if it's inconsistent or unhelpful when actually needed (Paper Mario: Sticker Star's Kersti) — a hint system that doesn't reliably help is worse than none (generic but well-illustrated).
- [6:30] "Living UI" sidekick: a character that IS a piece of interface — Sparx in Spyro shows health and points to collectibles, 808 in Hi Fi Rush is a rhythm metronome for the whole world. The character and the mechanic are the same object.
- [7:03] "Ability source" sidekick: a core mechanic is delivered entirely through the partner (FLUDD's water spray in Mario Sunshine, Cappy's throw/possess in Odyssey). Removing the companion mid-game and having the player feel the loss reinforces how load-bearing it is.
- [8:40] "Swiss army knife" sidekick: nearly every player verb routes through the companion (Kazooie does almost everything; Banjo just rolls), which can read as an imbalance if never addressed in writing.
- [9:48] Contrast-personality sidekick: works only if the other half of the pair has enough personality to bounce off (Jak/Daxter improved once Jak got an actual character in Jak 2) — generic "give both characters something to play off of" advice, but concretely illustrated.
- [12:56] Some games substitute a companion with the player character mumbling hints to themselves (Horizon Forbidden West) when there's no one around — flagged as an awkward workaround, not a good pattern.
- [15:03] Companion-as-real-protagonist: Midna in Twilight Princess carries more of the plot and character arc than Link does — a way to let a new character take narrative risks without changing the (silent) hero.
- [17:09] Villain-turned-companion: Portal 2's GLaDOS/Wheatley role-swap humanizes the antagonist once she becomes your ally.

## What the frames add
Nothing beyond the games shown; standard Design Doc gameplay-clip + dialogue-box captures, no diagrams.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"What does your companion actually do" spec (20-25 min)**: pick ONE role from the taxonomy (guide, living UI, ability source) for a tamed companion mob, and write what breaks if you remove it. Comes from [7:03]/[8:40]. This is the same underlying exercise as the "one job" worksheet from the AI Allies video (tIze-mre0ss) — teach them together, don't duplicate.
- **"Ability source" pet tooltip**: a tameable mob that grants the player a genuinely new ability while nearby (e.g., a status-effect aura, or right-click the mob to trigger a special move) — this is a real NeoForge mob-behavior + potion-effect task, and it's the one role from this video that's clearly buildable as a mechanic, not just writing.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The "living UI" idea already exists in vanilla Minecraft (a wolf's tail position/whimper reflects its health) — kids can be pointed at this as the working precedent before designing their own. "Ability source" companion is buildable. Guide/hint sidekick becomes, at most, a simple mob-chat-bubble or right-click tooltip system (not adaptive, not branching) — genuinely useful for teaching new mechanics in-game, closer to a UI feature than "character."

### Doesn't transfer
Everything from [9:48] onward (personality contrast, protagonist-companion role-swap, villain-to-ally arcs, Midna carrying the plot) is dialogue/narrative-writing craft that needs a branching story system this course doesn't have. It's worldbuilding/character-design content, already the domain of the earlier (non-GM) batches, not a Minecraft mechanic.

## Honest caveats
About half this video (roughly [9:48]-[18:13]) is really about writing companion characters, not designing their in-game behavior — genuinely good material for the character-design side of the course, but it does not answer this batch's "is it a Minecraft mechanic" filter. Flagging rather than force-fitting it into a mechanic card.

# Quick Pro Character Design Techniques (The Drawing Codex / Tim McBurnie, 117.6 min)

**What it is:** An unscripted, real-time screen-recorded Photoshop session where a 20-year professional illustrator ideates a 4-character fantasy lineup for his own comic project from blank canvas to finished flat-color concept sketches, narrating as he goes. He says explicitly at [0:01:19] this is not edited or prepared — "hit record and see what happens."
**Substance:** thin relative to its length. It is a genuine demo, not a structured lesson: maybe 15-20 minutes of distinct, restateable teaching points are spread across 117.6 minutes, with heavy repetition of the same handful of tips, constant hedging ("I don't know," "let's see," "not sure," "maybe"), and long stretches of real-time redrawing/erasing with no new information. It is a good process artifact but a poor teaching video on its own.

## How much of the 2 hours is teachable content vs. execution
Roughly: ~20 min (0:00-0:20) rough silhouette-thumbnail ideation of 3 creature designs with intermittent tips; ~35 min (0:20-0:57) is almost pure silent-ish linework clean-up of those 3 designs plus adding a 4th (a dragon-riding witch) — the transcript is continuously narrated (Whisper never shows real silence) but the content is overwhelmingly "let's see, maybe this, I don't know" with very little new instruction, and consecutive frames 8 seconds apart are frequently near-identical pencil strokes; ~35 min (0:57-1:33) is flat-color blocking using a repeatable Photoshop technique (genuinely useful to see once, repetitive to watch four times); the final ~20 min (1:33-1:56) is a "plussing" pass (shading, contrast, graphic pop) demonstrated once in detail then largely repeated per character. There is no chat/stream chatter (not a live stream, a solo recorded demo), but there is a lot of thinking-out-loud with low information density. A teacher should treat this as "watch 15 minutes of extracted technique," not "watch 2 hours."

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:04:49] Before drawing a character lineup, mark a ground line, a horizon line, and one reference "average character" height — keeps every character's relative scale consistent as more are added, rather than eyeballing size character by character.
- [0:06:18] Treat the very first pass as silhouette-level shape exploration only — draw deliberately loose/faint so you're not tempted to judge "good or bad" too early; the fuzziness is the point.
- [0:10:06] Don't chase "purpose" or backstory logic in this ideation phase — the task is generating as many visually interesting silhouettes as possible first; filtering for what actually makes sense comes later, as a separate step.
- [0:10:39] Don't erase mistakes in a lineup — resize/rework characters in place (warp, squash-and-stretch) to keep session momentum rather than restarting a drawing.
- [0:16:05] Expect first-pass ideas to read as generic ("meh") — that's normal and expected, not a sign to stop; the real creative work is the second pass asking "could this be more interesting."
- [0:16:47] A benefit of working in a lineup (several characters on one canvas) rather than one drawing at a time: it's easier to notice when designs are becoming "samey" and to actively push differentiation across a cast.
- [0:18:57] When a design still feels flat/generic, add one genuinely differentiating element (his example: making a background creature specifically blue) rather than generally "adding more."
- [0:22:37] Periodically flip/mirror the canvas horizontally while drawing — a fresh orientation catches proportion and construction errors your eye has gone blind to.
- [0:27:47] View the work on a second window/monitor at a smaller "thumbnail" size while working, to judge whether the silhouette reads at actual viewing scale rather than while zoomed into the working canvas.
- [0:57:13]-[1:01:04] "Line and color" workflow: after rough lineart, use Quick Mask to build a clean flat silhouette shape for each character (redrawing/simplifying the edge as you go) — this doubles as both a cleanup pass and a readability/silhouette check before color goes on.
- [1:02:49] A "contrast square" — a drawn or marquee-selected block of contrasting value placed near/behind a character — stops a figure from visually disappearing into a busy background and creates room for a strong silhouette or backlit read; a reusable compositional trick, not specific to this piece.
- [1:08:56]/[1:09:20] Once flats are built as isolated shapes, Alt-click a flat-color region to select it, then Hue/Saturation-shift that whole area instantly (e.g. recolor a character's skin from default to green) — lets you test several color directions on the same linework without repainting.
- [1:10:23] For a cast meant to feel visually related (a faction, a "visual data set"), eyedrop and reuse the same few colors across characters rather than inventing a new palette per character — cheap, fast way to make a group read as belonging together.
- [1:20:24]-[1:22:02] States plainly what this whole loose, messy process is actually producing: at this fidelity the drawing functions as a silhouette/graphic breakup exercise, not a finished illustration — the looseness is what allows bigger shape decisions, and if you're stuck on a design problem, working purely in silhouettes (no faces, no detail) can unstick it.
- [1:26:14]/[1:28:08] His stated argument for why he doesn't want to skip this grinding ideation phase with AI: even fully discarded rough passes are valuable because the process of struggling through 20-30 of them "downloads" understanding of the project into the designer's head, which pays off in later, faster, better decisions on the same project.
- [1:36:16] A fast non-destructive shading trick: a new "hard light" blend layer at low opacity lets you push form/light-and-shadow on top of flat colors in a couple of minutes without repainting; going lighter than 50% gray on the same layer brightens, darker shades, so one layer can do both highlight and shadow.
- [1:44:32]-[1:46:00] "Spotting blacks" — a technique borrowed from comic-book inking: after color, add a values-only pass that deliberately pushes some areas to near-black purely for graphic/pictorial punch (anchoring the art, adding "variety"), explicitly not because that's logically where a shadow falls.
- [1:51:04]-[1:52:00] A final atmosphere/lighting pass (airbrushed dark-to-light gradient, hard-light warm color wash) is a fast way to lift flat concept sketches out of feeling "too simple line-and-color" if a more illustrative/dramatic finish is wanted — explicitly optional, not always needed.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Height-ruler worksheet**: before Blockbench, kids state their mob's height in block units relative to the player (2 blocks tall) and one existing Minecraft mob (zombie, iron golem) as a size anchor — directly reusing [0:04:49]'s ground-plane/scale-reference idea, and it maps exactly onto Minecraft's block grid, no drawing required. ~5-10 min.
- **Small-screenshot readability check**: once a mob is built and textured in Blockbench/spawned in-game, take a screenshot small enough to view at thumbnail size (or literally back away from it in-game) and ask "does it still read at a glance?" — direct reuse of [0:27:47]/[1:20:24]'s "judge it at final viewing scale" habit, and it's arguably a better test in Minecraft than in his illustration, since mobs really are seen small and at a distance.
- **Palette-swap comparison**: before finalizing a texture, try the same mob shape with 2-3 different color schemes side by side and pick one — a simplified, drawing-free version of [1:08:56]'s hue-shift trick (in a pixel texture tool this is a literal recolor-and-compare, not a Photoshop-specific skill).
- **Faction palette rule**: for kids grouping their mobs/items into one faction or world region, require they reuse 2-3 signature colors across every piece in that group — direct reuse of [1:10:23], cheap and effective at 16x16 resolution, good for the worldbuilding unit.
- **"Three rough shapes before you build" rule**: before opening Blockbench, sketch (or just describe in a sentence each) three different silhouette ideas for the mob and pick the least generic one — reuse of [0:16:05]/[0:18:57]'s "first ideas are usually generic, push past them" habit, compressed to fit a short class.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The scale/ground-plane thinking and the "check it small/at a distance" habit survive close to perfectly and are arguably more directly true for a blocky, distance-viewed game world than for his illustration. Shared palette across a faction's mobs is cheap and effective at 16x16 texture resolution. The higher-level mindset points (silhouette-first thinking, don't over-invest emotionally in a first idea, iterate in a lineup so you can compare designs side by side) are useful design habits independent of medium. Almost none of the actual software technique survives: his whole toolkit (Quick Mask silhouette building, Alt-click flat-color selection with Hue/Saturation, hard-light shading layers, "spotting blacks" comic inking, contrast squares, airbrushed atmosphere) is Photoshop-specific 2D painting craft that has no equivalent in Blockbench + a 16x16 pixel texture workflow.

### Doesn't transfer
The extensive real-time figure construction (Loomis-method ribcage/spine/centerline building, redrawing anatomy and proportions repeatedly) needs drawing skill our kids don't have, and Blockbench doesn't need it — cube models are assembled from primitive boxes, not built up from a figure skeleton. The Photoshop-specific color/shading techniques listed above don't carry over to a pixel-texture workflow. The extended personal reflection on professional production pace, working on your own project over weeks/months, and his pushback on AI speed-running ideation (roughly [1:25:00]-[1:30:00] plus the outro, maybe 8-10 minutes total) is about a working illustrator's career and creative process, not something to teach or show to 11-13-year-olds. And structurally, there is no equivalent classroom activity to "watch someone slowly draw for 90 minutes" — none of the real-time execution itself is reusable, only the handful of extracted tips above.

## Honest caveats
This is a live, unscripted, unedited demo (his own description, [0:01:19]) — treat it as a process artifact, not a lesson plan; do not assign kids to watch it. The signal-to-runtime ratio is low: strip out the repetition and hedging and the useful content compresses to roughly 15-20 minutes, all captured in the bullets above. Unlike Cuora's video (Batch C's other source), this one has almost no design-*theory* content (no shape language, no color psychology, no silhouette-readability framework as such) — it is workflow/technique for an experienced illustrator, so it does not overlap much with the first video and does not fill Ben's "game design" gap either. His "quantity over perfection, don't skip the grind" philosophy is a reasonable studio-practice opinion but is explicitly his own take (partly framed as a rebuttal to AI-assisted ideation) and should be labeled as opinion, not settled craft, if quoted to Ben.

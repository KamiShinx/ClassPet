# What Makes a Good RPG Town? (Design Doc, 15.5 min)

**What it is:** Essay on the roles an RPG town plays: sanctuary/checkpoint, non-combat gameplay space, story/reputation hub, diegetic hint-giver, environmental-storytelling canvas, and living/changing place.
**Substance:** substantive — dense with named, specific examples (Pokemon, Dragon Quest, Xenoblade, Final Fantasy V/XIV, Chrono Cross, Paper Mario) and one ~90s sponsor read (Porkbun ".design" domains) that is skippable padding, not core content.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:48] Town's most basic role: sanctuary/checkpoint — resupply, heal, and a breakpoint from the resource-depletion loop (Pokemon's Pokecenter towns).
- [0:02:48] A town can also be a break from combat-focused gameplay entirely by offering different verbs — secrets, dialogue trees, no-fail minigames (Dragon Quest towns: search closets, read books, no game-over risk).
- [0:03:50]-[0:04:22] When a game doesn't need towns for resource restocking (Xenoblade's auto-heal/cooldowns), towns instead carry their own progression system — a unique local economy and a per-city reputation/affinity chart tied to NPC relationships and quests.
- [0:05:25] Town NPC dialogue can work as a diegetic hint system (rumors about nearby dungeon secrets) rather than a direct tutorial or quest marker (Final Fantasy V).
- [0:07:35] Best towns imply the humanity/history of residents through subtle details rather than an info-dump.
- [0:08:08] Towns can carry story allegorically through subtle change — Chrono Cross's "Another Arni" is the same place, quietly wrong (people don't recognize you, even the music is retuned) — a heavy fiction-craft device, not a quick trick.
- [0:10:31] Environmental storytelling: architecture/setpieces alone can imply class structure and history without dialogue (FFXIV's Eulmore — literal vertical divide between rich "Canopy" and poor "Derelicts").
- [0:12:39] Named failure mode: NPCs repeating identical lines from hour 2 to hour 30 makes a town feel like "a theme park facade" (cites Paper Mario: The Origami King's static Toad Town). Fix: have the town visibly change (new NPCs/areas/events) as the story progresses.
- [0:13:41] Alternate fix for the same problem: give NPCs their own background storylines that progress whether or not the player is watching (Trails in the Sky) — reinforces "the world exists without you."

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Town job card" worksheet** (15-20 min): kid picks 1-2 roles for their hub structure — sanctuary/heal point, non-combat minigame space, NPC rumor board, or a town that visibly changes over weeks — forces scope before building.
- **"NPC isn't wallpaper" check**: kid writes one line each for 2-3 villagers that must be different by week 15 than week 5 — direct lift from the "same lines all game" flaw at [0:12:39].
- **"Diegetic hint" worksheet**: write one villager line that hints at a nearby structure/mob without a quest marker, from [0:05:25].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Town-as-sanctuary maps directly onto a player-built hub structure populated with villagers (trading covers the "unique economy" idea); environmental storytelling ([0:10:31]) maps cleanly onto Blockbench structure design — a kid can imply a faction's wealth or history through build choices alone, no new code needed, just placement and material choices. This is the most buildable, lowest-Gemini-effort idea in the note.

### Doesn't transfer
Chrono Cross's "same place, subtly changed by unseen time-travel" ([0:08:08]) needs branching dialogue and parallel-world state tracking — well past a 65-min/week mod's scope. Note as "admire, don't attempt."

## Honest caveats
The video is well-cited throughout with no filler beyond the one sponsor read, but it never hands you a single reusable checklist — the bullets above had to be compressed into worksheets ourselves. Its clearest visual artifact is the 4-category title card, not a diagram original to the analysis.

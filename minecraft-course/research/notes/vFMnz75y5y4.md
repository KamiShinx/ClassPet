# The Seven Levels of Worldbuilding (Large Lads Studios, 29.5 min)

**What it is:** a talking-head taxonomy of worldbuilding maturity, 7 tiers from "draw a map" to "a self-sustaining world that generates its own stories," illustrated with movie/game/book examples.
**Substance:** mixed: the ladder itself is a clean, memorable framework, but it is general fantasy worldbuilding (books, D&D, TV, with a few games mixed in) and never addresses how a *player* experiences a world differently from a *reader*. No original research, just curated examples.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:59] Everyone's first map has illogical rivers (crossing mountains, splitting at random) — learning real river/geography logic retroactively exposes shallow worldbuilding. (generic anecdote, sets up the whole video)
- [0:03:53] Level 1, Map: land must obey cause-and-effect (rivers flow downhill and converge, mountains cause rain shadows, cities sit near water/trade/defensible terrain); if you break real physics you must know *why*. Westeros holds up under this test; a "Kings of the Wild" fantasy map doesn't; Discworld breaks all rules but defines its own consistently.
- [0:07:47] Level 2, "Rule of Cool": stacking exciting ideas (dragons, cursed forests, assassins) without systemic thought. Fine for many stories, but bigger isn't deeper — basic questions ("what does this magic do to daily life?") expose the gap.
- [0:10:10] Level 3, Surface Identity: naming conventions, architecture, clothing, symbols that make a culture recognizable at a glance without labels (Tolkien's constructed Elvish vs Rohirric; Warhammer 40k's all-in gothic Imperium aesthetic; Avatar's four nations each built from one real-world culture).
- [0:12:33] "Planet of the Hats" warning: don't reduce a whole culture to one trait; keep individual variation inside a strong regional identity. (generic but well-placed caution)
- [0:14:58] Level 4, Functional Power: magic/tech/divine power must reshape society, not just decorate it (Mistborn's magic creates a ruling class; Dune's ban on thinking machines reshapes religion, combat and politics; Attack on Titan's walls reshape architecture, military and government).
- [0:19:24] Level 5, Deep Culture & History: beliefs, taboos and traditions that evolved and compound over time, not static lore-dumps (Witcher's aged regional prejudice; Silmarillion's consequences spanning ages; Elder Scrolls' deliberately conflicting, unreliable histories).
- [0:22:50] Level 6, Infrastructure & Institutions: banking, trade, logistics that outlast any one ruler (Iron Bank of Braavos constrains kings by controlling debt; Fallout's Vaults as engineered social experiments; Snowpiercer's train literally *is* the class system).
- [0:26:49] Level 7, Generative World: once geography, culture, power and institutions all cohere, the world can produce new stories on its own — you can "zoom into any city, any era" and find a story already implied.
- [0:28:13] Closing framing: these are tiers of awareness, not a checklist; most stories/games don't need level 7; getting better is about asking "why is this here, who benefits, what happens if it changes" — not talent. (generic)

## What the frames add
Nothing structural. Pure movie/show/game clip reel (LOTR, Game of Thrones, Avatar, Mistborn book cover, Dune, Attack on Titan, Elder Scrolls, Fallout, Snowpiercer, Warhammer 40k) plus plain text title-cards per level ("Level 3 — Surface Identity" etc). No diagrams, no UI, no process footage, nothing game-mechanic specific.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Why is it here?" card**: before placing any structure/biome feature, kid answers one line each for 3 features (a hill, a ruin, a village) — why does it exist where it is. ~15 min, Level 1 exercise, directly primes the spec they'll later give Gemini.
- **Faction identity kit**: kid picks one naming pattern + one block/color palette + one recurring motif for their mob/faction, consistent across every item and structure they build. ~20-30 min, Level 3, maps straight onto Blockbench + 16x16 textures.
- **"If it's real, what changes?" one-liner**: for a mob ability or item power, kid writes one cause-effect sentence (Level 4) that becomes the literal spec line for a Gemini prompt about mob/item behaviour.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
Levels 1, 3 and 4 survive well — they map onto terrain placement, texture/model consistency, and item/mob behaviour design respectively, all things Gemini can actually build. Levels 5-6 (deep culture, institutions, banking) don't have a mechanical hook in a small Minecraft mod; at best they become a paragraph of unused flavor text.

### Doesn't transfer
Levels 6-7 (infrastructure, institutions, self-sustaining generative world) require a scale of economic/political simulation and writing volume no 8-kid, 65-minute-a-week, 20-week course can produce or a small mod can express mechanically. Cut, or compress to a single "one institution that limits your faction" one-liner at most.

## Honest caveats
This video is essentially general fantasy worldbuilding wearing a "worldbuilding" title; it never engages the reader-vs-player question this batch is supposed to investigate, and only touches games in passing (Elden Ring, Skyrim mentioned once each, no gameplay footage). Batch-mate Millard (bups0ZUQdvc) directly contradicts the implied "more depth/levels = better" arc: he argues more lore volume often *hurts* games specifically. For this class, side with Millard over the Level 6-7 ambition.

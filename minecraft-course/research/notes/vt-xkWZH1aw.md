# What's The Point of a First Level? (Design Doc, 18.7 min)

**What it is:** A survey of what a game's opening level has to accomplish (teaching + hooking the player), with
case studies spanning platformers, racers, RPGs, and replay-heavy games.
**Substance:** substantive: high, consistent example density across genres; sponsor read (NordVPN, ~30s) is
clearly separated from the content and doesn't dilute it.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:02:53] A first level has two jobs at once: crash course (teach controls/structure/nouns & verbs) and sales
  pitch (make you want to keep playing) — the ratio between the two depends on the game.
- [0:03:23] Mario 64 has two "firsts": Peach's Castle is a risk-free toy/sandbox to learn 3D movement itself (no
  fail state possible), and Bob-Omb Battlefield is the actual first "level," introducing the Star-objective
  structure at low danger while sampling most of what the rest of the game will ask of the player.
- [0:04:57] A first level must establish the DEFAULT baseline (basic mechanics, structure, difficulty) before
  layering in the game's crazier twists later; SUPERHOT teaches its whole core loop in "3 sentences" plus a small
  tinkering space.
- [0:06:04] First levels/tracks in a genre are traditionally kept deliberately plain (Mario Kart's early circuits:
  flat, simple turns, no gimmicks) — but staying too basic too long is itself a mistake (Mario Kart DS criticized
  for 3 bland tracks in one cup).
- [0:07:05] Games with an unusual core mechanic need MORE dedicated upfront teaching: Sonic Heroes' 3-character
  formation system gets literal "formation gates" that auto-swap the right character for the obstacle at first,
  then those gates quietly disappear later, leaving only a small hint/sign — "taking the training wheels off" as
  competence builds. (Independently corroborates the isolation principle from ZIs5189lmRA, different channel.)
- [0:11:59] A first level can be a "vertical slice"/sales pitch: show the most exciting, easy-to-digest version of
  the game's peak experience immediately (Bayonetta's bombastic, easy-combat opener; Bastion's narrator-driven
  intro) to hook the player before asking for real investment.
- [0:13:41] The "power curve" problem: if a first level shows the player at their most powerful, where do you go
  from there? Devil May Cry 5 throws you into an unwinnable super-boss right after a confidence-building opener,
  explicitly to reset expectations; Yakuza Kiwami does the inverse (max power for ~1 hour, then a forced humbling
  reset). Both explicitly flagged in the video as risky techniques (loss aversion).
- [0:15:55] "In medias res" openers (starting mid-action, explaining later) can hook players fast without needing
  to explain who everyone is yet (Chrono Cross's opening dungeon).
- [0:08:42] Games designed to be replayed (branching paths, New Game+) need a first level that's lean on repeat
  visits — Shadow the Hedgehog is a bad example (branching objectives force a long grind on every replay); Star
  Fox 64's Corneria is a good one (skippable cutscenes, tutorials that don't even trigger if the player is already
  doing fine).

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"First 60 seconds" worksheet**: for a kid's chosen mechanic, design the very first encounter a stranger
  classmate has with it — must be safe (no punishment for trying it wrong) and must show off the coolest part
  fast. Ties directly to the W10/W20 cold-play showcase. 20 min.
- **Isolation-principle cross-check**: show the Sonic Heroes formation-gate clip alongside ZIs5189lmRA's Mario
  3D World clip as proof this is a convergent idea across two channels, not a one-off.
- **Vertical-slice pitch**: sketch the ONE thing a classmate should see or do in the first 10 seconds that makes
  them think "that's cool" — a presentation-focused companion to the idea-generation deck from 0m60QbT85Tc.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
The crash-course + sales-pitch framing fits a kid's short showcase demo well, since they effectively only ever
get a "first level," never a full game — reframing the mod showcase as "design your mod's first level" is a
genuinely useful lens the teacher hasn't been given yet.

### Doesn't transfer
The replay-focused material (Shadow the Hedgehog's branching paths, New Game+ design) assumes a single game
replayed many times by the same player — a kid's mod is played once, cold, by a classmate, so "don't be annoying
on the 9th replay" isn't this class's problem. The power-curve-inversion technique (start overpowered, then
humble the player) needs a full game's pacing to land and is too advanced/risky for a single-mechanic school
demo — inspiration only, not a worksheet.

## Honest caveats
No exaggeration needed — content density is consistently high and the sponsor segment is clearly walled off from
the analysis. The power-curve-inversion and replay-design sections are genuinely strong ideas but a poor fit for
this specific class's format; that's a scope mismatch, not a flaw in the video.

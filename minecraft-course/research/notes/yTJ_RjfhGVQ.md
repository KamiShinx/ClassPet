# Side Quests - How To Make A Good Detour (Design Doc, 18.3 min)

**What it is:** A three-part framework for side quest design — how it starts, what you do in it, how it ends/rewards — with a taxonomy of side quest "types" in the middle section.
**Substance:** substantive: the clearest structural framework in the whole batch (start/middle/end + 4 content types + 3 reward types), directly on-theme for "side quests" in Q2, backed by well-chosen contrasting examples (including a real bad example, Paper Mario TTYD's Trouble Center). ~1 min sponsor read ([0:00:44]-[0:01:48]).

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:48] Core framework: every side quest breaks into three parts — how it starts (branches off the main line), how it goes (the content), how it ends (reward/reconnection to main story). Use this shape as the spec template.
- [0:02:18] "How it starts" needs to be convenient/low-friction or it won't be worth the player's time investment. CrossCode: NPCs hand out fetch/kill quests casually while wandering, no detour required to pick them up — not innovative, but frictionless, and that's enough (explicit "good design doesn't have to be innovative design" line).
- [0:03:56] Alternative start style: make the side quest NOT visibly flagged as a side quest. Hollow Knight's quest NPCs are just scattered in the world with no checklist/signpost — finding one feels like discovery, not menu management, which reinforces the game's exploration theme.
- [0:04:26] Explicit bad example: Paper Mario TTYD's Trouble Center forces you to walk to a central hub board, read a note, then walk BACK to the person who's often standing right where you started — pure travel friction with no payoff, cited as actively killing motivation to do side quests at all. A clean contrast to CrossCode/Hollow Knight.
- [0:05:29] Middle-section taxonomy — 4 side quest content types, each suited to a different kind of game: **story-driven** (fleshes out side characters/world, can feed back into the main plot — Mass Effect 2 loyalty missions, framed as "an optional Star Trek episode" per character), **variety-driven** (a genuinely different minigame/activity as a palate cleanser — Yakuza's minigames/management sims), **elaboration-driven** (uses the game's EXISTING mechanics in a deeper/unusual combination not explored by the main game — Majora's Mask's time-loop-based quests use the core 3-day clock mechanic more thoroughly than the 4 main dungeons do), **challenge-driven** (optional super-bosses/secret dungeons that spike difficulty without blocking the main story — Kingdom Hearts 2 Final Mix's Organization XIII rematches).
- [0:07:05] Design rationale for making great story content OPTIONAL: lets players skip characters/arcs they don't personally care about, tailoring the experience — not every player needs to be forced through content aimed at someone else's taste (Mass Effect 2).
- [0:11:16] Rationale for challenge-driven side quests specifically: they let you put real difficulty spikes and system-mastery tests in the game WITHOUT gatekeeping the ending — "a fantastic reward for really, fully understanding what the game lets you do," rather than a punishing mandatory wall.
- [0:12:51] "How it ends" — reward taxonomy: **loot/progression** (gear, currency, crew — FTL, where quest choices materially affect run survival), **story payoff** (side quest events change how the MAIN story plays out — Mass Effect 2 loyalty missions affecting the ending), **world-state change / "cool stuff happens"** (Breath of the Wild's Tarrey Town literally grows a town with new shops/music over the course of the game as a direct result of quest completion — praised as satisfying because it's visible and permanent, not just a stat).
- [0:13:55] Warning: don't hoard your best rewards for the hardest/latest challenge quests — if there's nothing left to use great new loot ON, the reward loses its point. Rewards should still have runway to matter.
- [0:15:35] "No side quest is an island" — the best side-quest systems (Bug Fables cited) nail convenience of starting, avoid backtracking friction via shortcuts/fast travel, drip-feed quests gradually instead of dumping a wall of generic ones, and combine several of the taxonomy's content/reward types across its 50+ quests — a synthesis example of everything above working together.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **"Spec a side quest" worksheet (30 min) — the single best-fit activity in this whole batch:** kid fills in the three-part template directly from [0:01:48]: (1) How does the player find this quest — a villager, a sign, something they stumble on exploring? (2) What do they actually do — pick ONE of the 4 types: fetch/kill (story), a genuinely different activity (variety, e.g. a minigame block), using an existing mod mechanic in a new combo (elaboration), or a harder optional fight (challenge)? (3) What do they get — an item, a world change (a building appears, a villager unlocks new trades), or nothing but lore? This is concrete enough that a teacher could run it in one session and it produces a real spec for something to vibe-code next.
- **"Fix the annoying quest" exercise (15 min):** show the Paper Mario Trouble Center failure [0:04:26] as a worked bad example, then have kids identify what's wrong with a similarly bad hypothetical Minecraft NPC quest (villager sends you across the map and back for no reason) and redesign the start so it's convenient — teaches the "how it starts" lesson through contrast.
- **"World-state reward" prompt (15 min, ties to worldbuilding pillar):** from Tarrey Town [0:15:02] — kid designs one quest chain where completing it visibly changes the world (a building gets built, a villager's shop restocks with new items, a new structure spawns) instead of just handing over an item.

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
This is the batch's most directly useful video because Minecraft already has almost all the primitives: Minecraft has no quest system, BUT advancements (trigger on player actions), structures with loot tables (rewards), and villager trades (a quest-giver-like NPC with items/dialogue-lite) can fake all 4 quest types listed. Fetch/kill quests (story/variety type) are directly buildable as an advancement chain (kill X mob, bring Y item to a block/villager, get a custom loot reward) — squarely in NeoForge's comfort zone. "Elaboration-driven" quests (use an existing mechanic in an unusual way) map onto designing an advancement that rewards combining two of the mod's own systems in a clever way — buildable, and a good creative constraint for kids. "Challenge-driven" quests (optional super-boss) map directly onto a custom boss mob with a loot table gated behind finding/beating it — very buildable and already how vanilla Minecraft treats the Wither/Ender Dragon. The Tarrey-Town-style "world visibly changes" reward is the one to flag as harder: dynamically spawning/rebuilding structures based on quest progress needs some datapack/event-trigger work beyond a beginner's first mod, but "unlock new villager trades after an advancement" is an easy, real substitute that gets 80% of the same feeling.

### Doesn't transfer
The full branching dialogue / quest-board UI (Paper Mario's Trouble Center, or any menu-driven quest log with descriptions and tracked objectives) is explicitly the kind of custom UI ANALYST_BRIEF_GM.md flags as hard for Gemini to build — a real quest-tracking system with a UI is a stretch goal, not a week-by-week deliverable; better to fake "quest state" with advancements (which already have their own toast notification and tracking page for free) than to hand-build a UI. The Mass Effect 2 "optional content changes the ending" idea needs branching narrative state well beyond a one-page kid spec.

# Character Design Secrets Everyone Should Know (Proko / Evgeniy Evstratiy, 16.2 min)

**What it is:** A professional game concept artist (Riot/Valve/Marvel credits) presents a structured, named framework for character design: idea generation methods, a reference-gathering taxonomy, a ranked "priority" hierarchy of design elements (silhouette/proportion/scale -> upper body/face -> design elements/outfit -> color), and a deep-dive on outfit/equipment (body exposure + iconography).
**Substance:** substantive: this is the most systematically organized video in the batch — every claim is paired with a named concept, a labeled slide, and 2-4 visual examples; there's a short outro plug for Proko's paid reference-timer tool (~30 sec) but essentially no padding elsewhere.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:00] Defines "good design" functionally: one that fulfills its purpose/solves the brief (client) or the artist's personal goal — not a subjective "looks cool" standard.
- [0:00:30] For self-directed projects with no brief, three idea sources: copy/riff on another artist's work, make an OC for an existing game/movie world, or build your own original setting (demoed via his own project, "Gold Rush").
- [0:01:04] Six named idea-generation techniques, each with 2 examples: **Visual mix** (combine unrelated objects — motorcycle suit + German helmet + skull = Darth Vader; spider + tank = spider-tank); **What if?** (unconventional premise — "what if the Wild West was in space" = Cowboy Bebop/Firefly); **Reverse approach** (remove something instead of adding — "what if reproduction was taken away"); **Scale experiments** (change relative size — giants who eat humans, ant-sized people); **Influence of environment** (how setting shapes character/design, stated briefly); **Genre fusion** (combine two genres/settings — steampunk+fantasy, post-apocalyptic+dark fantasy); **Anachronisms** (insert period-inappropriate elements, or imagine alternate tech history).
- [0:02:37] Three reference categories, clearly named: **Mood board** (inspiration/style/feeling, not literal), **General** (concrete starting-point material — historical photos, existing designs), **Supportive** (technical/construction reference — how a mechanism or garment actually works).
- [0:03:39] States a ranked design-priority order by what viewers notice/interpret first: (1) silhouette/proportion/scale, (2) upper body (head/face), (3) design elements (shape language, gear, weapons, clothing), (4) color/texture/pattern — explicitly flags this as usually-true-but-not-universal (Stormtroopers reorder this, since face/upper-body barely matters for them).
- [0:04:39] Defines silhouette (contour shaped by proportion+outfit+gear+hair+posture), proportions (relative size/shape relationships between parts), and scale (character size relative to a normal human, sometimes conveyed via head size or familiar objects).
- [0:05:09] "Recognizable from silhouette alone" test, with real-world non-character examples first (AK-47, Vespa scooter silhouettes are identifiable filled solid black), then classic cartoon silhouettes and TF2/Overwatch character silhouettes.
- [0:05:40] Real human bodies vary hugely in proportion too (cites a Howard Schatz athlete photo series) — silhouette/proportion thinking isn't just a stylization trick, it reflects real variation.
- [0:06:12] Notes silhouette is also a primary design concern in real fashion and hairstyling, not just character art.
- [0:06:43] Demonstrates via example: changing PROPORTIONS alone (same silhouette) creates different designs; changing SILHOUETTE alone (same proportions) also creates different designs; combining both multiplies the possible variety.
- [0:07:18] Ranks second-tier "personality carriers," in order: facial features/emotion (most direct emotional signal — "even a robot gets personality from a face"), pose/body language (implies thinking about how the character will move/animate), outfit/equipment (signals status/profession/culture; ABSENCE of an item can also tell a story), hair/facial hair (signals personality/lifestyle), feature traits (scars/tattoos/prosthetics — signals backstory/history), color palette (last-ranked; signals emotion/group belonging or "taste").
- [0:09:26] Key nuance: these rankings are not fixed — a design can deliberately shift which trait dominates (shown via examples where a character reads almost entirely through pose, or almost entirely through outfit, or almost entirely through prosthetics/tattoos+color) — the right priority mix depends on the character's defined backstory/personality, using faceless-soldier Stormtrooper vs. charismatic Han Solo again as the anchor contrast.
- [0:10:31] Outfit/equipment gets special deep treatment as "a silhouette within a silhouette," broken into two sub-concepts: **body exposure** (how much/which body parts are shown — same exposure reads differently in different contexts, e.g. naked in a locker room vs. naked on the street) and **iconography** (visual shorthand that triggers cultural recognition, e.g. minimal-shape "Family Guy"-style icon figures still read as recognizable characters/archetypes with zero detail).
- [0:12:08] Concrete iconography demo: a plain figure becomes "cowboy," "pirate," or "special agent" just by adding a couple of key attributes; a base "guy in street clothes + gun" is walked through many archetype swaps (gangster -> mercenary -> PMC contractor -> security guard/soldier -> war correspondent/police officer -> bodyguard/robber) by changing only 1-2 elements each time.
- [0:13:44] Applies the same logic to his own professional work: a character's sci-fi/punk/rock/heist identity is built from three deliberately chosen clothing-category "ingredients" (black suit=sci-fi, leather+belts+collars=punk/rock, bags+weapons=robbery).
- [0:14:16] Footwear alone carries archetype/subculture signaling and can shift a character's felt weight (heavy/squat vs. light/dynamic) — extends the "type of clothing = cultural code" idea to a specific category, and notes the same principle applies to typefaces (a tangential but real design-transfer point).
- [0:15:04] Closing distinction: **grounded** design (film-industry-favored: realism, function, practicality due to production constraints) vs. **entertainment** design (games-favored: stylization, exaggeration, "engaging" over "practical") — frames this as the main style-axis, not a value judgment.

## What the frames add
This video is built almost entirely around labeled title-card slides, and they ARE the structure — nearly every idea above has its own on-screen heading (e.g., "SILHOUETTE PROPORTIONS SCALE" at 00:03:44, the ranked bullet list at 00:04:56, "PHYSICAL" at 00:10:56) which makes it unusually easy to build a class handout directly from the frame captures. Sheet 4's silhouette demonstrations (00:05:20-05:52: AK-47, Vespa scooter, a wall of ~30 classic cartoon silhouettes, then TF2 and Overwatch character silhouettes) are the strongest concentrated proof-by-example of "silhouette=identity" across the whole batch. Sheet 4 also shows the Howard Schatz real-athlete body-diversity photos (00:06:00-06:08), directly supporting the "proportion variety is real, not just stylization" claim. Sheet 6's iconography sequence (00:11:28-14:16) is a genuinely striking, reusable visual: a plain gray silhouette figure re-skinned step by step into cowboy, pirate, secret agent, gangster/mercenary/soldier variants, and finally a full sequence of increasingly-detailed archetype outfits (00:13:04-13:44) — this could be lifted almost directly into a class worksheet on "how 2-3 items change what a character reads as." Sheet 8 (00:15:20-15:52) closes with a side-by-side of Overwatch/Zelda/Mario-style entertainment-design characters vs. grounded film characters (Joker, Terminator, Jack Sparrow, C-3PO) and military-realistic game character art, visually anchoring the grounded-vs-entertainment axis.

## For our class (11-13, Minecraft mod, Gemini writes the code)
### Becomes something kids do
- **Idea-generator picker (10 min, no drawing)**: print the six technique names (visual mix, what-if, reverse, scale, environment, genre fusion, anachronism) as cards; kid draws one at random and must generate a mob/item concept using it — a strong, ready-made icebreaker for a "stuck for ideas" week. Comes from [0:01:04].
- **Silhouette recognition game (10-15 min, no drawing)**: show kids 6-8 solid-black shape silhouettes (mix of real Minecraft mobs + a few of their own past designs) and have them guess which is which — a direct, playable version of the AK-47/Vespa/TF2 test. Comes from [0:05:09].
- **"2-item archetype swap" worksheet**: give a plain base figure (or plain Blockbench humanoid) and a grid of small icon stickers (hat, weapon, badge, mask, tool) — kid picks 2 items and explains what archetype/role the combo now reads as, mirroring the gangster-to-soldier chain. Directly reusable, needs zero drawing skill since it's icon selection. Comes from [0:12:08]-[0:13:44].
- **Priority-ranking exercise**: give kids the ranked list (silhouette > face > outfit > hair > scars/tattoos > color) and ask them to identify which ONE or TWO traits will carry their own mob's personality, then focus effort there instead of spreading thin. Comes from [0:07:18]-[0:09:26].
- **Grounded vs. entertainment style choice**: a simple up-front worksheet question — "is your mob supposed to feel real-world-believable, or big and exaggerated/game-y?" — frames the whole rest of their design decisions. Comes from [0:15:04].

### Survives the move to Minecraft?
This video's core ranking — silhouette/proportion/scale FIRST, face/detail LAST — maps almost perfectly onto what actually reads in Minecraft: at typical play distance and 16x16 texture resolution, silhouette and proportion are close to the ONLY things that reliably read, while fine facial expression and skin texture detail are the first things lost. This makes the priority ranking not just transferable but arguably MORE true in Minecraft than in illustration — worth stating directly to kids as "in our game, tip #1 (silhouette/proportion/scale) matters even more than in drawing, because that's almost all a player will actually see." Iconography (2-3 key items signal a whole archetype) is extremely well suited to Blockbench + 16x16 textures, where a kid literally cannot afford lots of detail and must rely on a small number of recognizable shapes/colors — this is the most actionable single idea in the batch for a resource-constrained medium. The idea-generation techniques (visual mix, what-if, genre fusion, anachronism) require no drawing and work as pure brainstorming, transferring directly to inventing a mob or item concept before Gemini ever gets involved. "Design driven by a defined backstory/personality" also feeds directly into the course's worldbuilding half — a mob's silhouette and priority traits should follow from lore decided in that strand.

### Doesn't transfer
Body exposure as a design signal ("what does exposed skin communicate in context") is not really applicable to blocky, clothed Minecraft mobs and isn't age-appropriate framing to teach directly to 11-13-year-olds regardless. The fine granularity of the outfit-archetype-swap system (distinguishing PMC contractor from mercenary from security guard via small realistic gear differences) requires real-world knowledge of military/professional dress codes that won't land with this age group or this game's blocky aesthetic — the CONCEPT (few items = archetype) transfers, the specific real-world examples don't. Footwear-as-archetype and typeface-as-mood are too subtle to render at 16x16/cube resolution — a boot silhouette in Minecraft is at best 1-2 pixels of visual information. The nuanced "shift which trait dominates" idea (e.g., "let color alone carry personality this time") is sophisticated art-direction thinking that's probably too abstract for most 11-13-year-olds to apply deliberately, though simplified to "pick ONE main trait to focus on" it becomes usable (see worksheet above).

## Honest caveats
This is the strongest, best-organized video in the batch and nothing here reads as obviously wrong or oversold — claims are consistently qualified ("this is usually the case, but there are exceptions") rather than stated as universal law, which is more careful than most of the other videos in this set. Where it overlaps with earlier-covered ground (silhouette identifiability, cartoon silhouette tests) it agrees with and reinforces both Pixel Pete's and Draw-like-a-Sir's videos rather than contradicting them, which strengthens confidence in that specific point (silhouette-first design) as something worth building the class's core rubric around. The one place to flag as an extrapolation, not the video's own claim: the presenter never discusses low-resolution/blocky/distant viewing conditions at all (his examples are all high-fidelity game/film art) — the argument that his priority ranking "matters even more" in Minecraft's low-fidelity medium is this analyst's inference, not something stated in the source.

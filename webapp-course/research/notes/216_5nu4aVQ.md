# Secrets of Game Feel and Juice (Game Maker's Toolkit, 5.3 min)
**What it is / substance:** a fast, dense list of small polish tricks ("juice") that make games feel responsive and alive, illustrated with clips from ~15 different games. Good enough to assign, but only WITH a guide: the pace is rapid (a new trick every 5-10 seconds) and it names zero how-to specifics — a kid needs the "Juice techniques as Gemini prompts" list below alongside it, not the video alone.

**Watch-list:** [0:02:42]-[0:04:23] is the actual payload (every juice trick, back to back) — if short on time, watch only this. [0:00:00]-[0:01:06] is worth adding for the "game feel" concept and the blank-room test.

## The ideas (in order, with [h:mm:ss])
- [0:00:00] Two mobile platformers of the same genre are compared: Random Heroes looks "limp and lifeless," Super Time Force feels "alive and responsive... it pops and crackles." Same kind of game, very different feel.
- [0:00:33] Names the core idea, **game feel**: jargon for the invisible, moment-to-moment sensation of controlling a game — separate from graphics, story or music.
- [0:01:06] Gives a test for it: strip out points, story, graphics, music and clever level design — is just moving/interacting still fun on its own? (the "blank room" test.)
- [0:01:06]-[0:01:38] Example: in Super Mario 64, the character's jump, wall-kick, long jump and dive are so fun that the game was reportedly built by perfecting Mario's movement in an empty test room before any levels existed. The core feel here is **friction, momentum and weight** — this is foundational "game feel," not "juice" (see below), and it's genre-specific, so the video admits it can't give a universal recipe for it.
- [0:02:09] Pivots from "feel" (hard to generalize) to **juice**: small, addable effects usually polished in late development. Credits a linked talk, captioned as "torqus... by vamir Cactus and grapefruit" — auto-caption garble, almost certainly the GDC talk on this exact topic by Vlambeer-adjacent devs (Cactus / Grapefrukt handles are real indie devs); the video does not literally spell this out cleanly, flagged as our inference below in Caveats.
- [0:02:42] **Screen shake**: shaking the camera briefly on gunfire or hits, credited to Vlambeer's games.
- [0:02:42] **Hit-pause / freeze-frame**: pausing the game for a split second on a hit or kill, also seen in Street Fighter, God of War and most Zelda games.
- [0:03:15] A bundle of enemy-hit feedback: flashing the enemy white, knocking it back, spraying blood, switching its animation, playing a distinct sound — the video's point is that any of these signal "you're damaging something," and stacking several works best.
- [0:03:15] **Particles and recoil** make the player character feel present in the world: dust puffs on landing, gun recoil on firing.
- [0:03:15]-[0:03:51] Sound design tips: make weapon sounds bassy and loud (with a bad-vs-good sound comparison played but not describable from a transcript); randomize sound effect variants so repeated actions don't sound identical; use a rising pitch on repeated hits to sell a growing combo (cites Mario and Peggle).
- [0:03:51] **Camera as a juice tool**: Luftrausers' camera reframes intelligently to reveal nearby threats instead of rigidly following the plane; Hotline Miami's camera pushes ahead of the direction you're facing so you can see what's coming.
- [0:03:51] **Exaggerate scale**: make bullets, explosions and blood bigger than realistic (Nuclear Throne's bullets, Super Time Force's explosions, Hotline Miami's blood).
- [0:04:23] **Permanence**: Hotline Miami's bodies and blood stay on the ground after the fight, so walking back past them reinforces how much chaos you caused.
- [0:04:23] The organizing rule: juice should double down on whatever the game's core action is — a shooting game gets camera shutter + fast fire rate + kickback, a jumping game gets stillness and character friction instead. Juice should amplify the core verb, not just be piled on randomly.
- [0:04:56] Explicit disclaimer: full game feel takes developers months or years; a 5-minute video isn't a complete formula, just some things to try.

## Vocabulary for prompting Gemini
- **Game feel** → the overall sensation of controlling something, from movement/response alone → mostly not directly promptable in a turn-based Sheets game, but frame requests as "make clicking/tapping feel responsive and instant."
- **Juice** → small, stackable polish effects layered onto an action → "juice up the attack button: add a small shake, a flash, and a sound when it's pressed."
- **Screen shake** → briefly jiggling the whole visible area → "when the player clicks Attack, shake the whole screen 4px for 100ms."
- **Hit-pause / freeze-frame** → briefly freezing all motion right when an impact lands, before showing the result → "freeze the screen for 80ms right when the damage number appears, then show it."
- **Knockback** → an enemy or object visibly jolts backward when hit → "when an enemy takes damage, slide its image 10px away from the attacker and back over 150ms."
- **Squash and stretch** → (named in this video's genre of tricks generically as "change animation," not explicitly spelled out as a term here — treat as our own addition, not the video's wording) → "when a button is pressed, squash it to 95% height for 100ms then spring back."

## Juice techniques as Gemini prompts
*(Numbers/durations below are not stated by the video, which never gives exact figures — they are our own reasonable defaults for a kid to paste and then tune by eye.)*
- **Screen shake** [0:02:42] → "when the player clicks Attack, shake the whole game screen by 4px left-right for 150ms using CSS."
- **Hit-pause / freeze-frame** [0:02:42] → "when an attack lands, pause all animation on screen for 80ms before showing the damage result."
- **Flash white on hit** [0:03:15] → "when an enemy takes damage, flash its image white for 100ms using a CSS filter, then fade back to normal."
- **Knockback** [0:03:15] → "when an enemy is hit, animate it sliding 10px away from the attacker and back over 200ms."
- **Blood/impact spray (particles)** [0:03:15] → "when an attack lands, spawn 6 small red dots at the hit point that fly outward and fade over 300ms."
- **Change animation on hit** [0:03:15] → "when an enemy's HP hits 0, swap its image to a 'defeated' sprite instead of just removing it."
- **Satisfying hit sound** [0:03:15] → "play a short punchy sound effect the instant an attack connects, before the damage number appears."
- **Landing/movement particles** [0:03:15] → "when a character finishes an action, show a small puff of 4-5 dust particles at its feet for 200ms."
- **Recoil on action** [0:03:15] → "when the player clicks Attack, nudge the attacker's own image back 5px and return it over 100ms."
- **Bassy/loud sound design** [0:03:15]-[0:03:51] → "make the attack sound effect deep and punchy, not thin or quiet — use a low-pitched short sound file."
- **Randomized sound variation** [0:03:51] → "pick randomly between 3 different attack sound files each time, so it doesn't sound identical every click."
- **Rising pitch on combo** [0:03:51] → "each time the player scores a hit in a row, raise the sound effect's pitch by 10%, resetting after a miss."
- **Dynamic/reactive camera framing** [0:03:51] → *(N/A for a static Sheets-style layout — see Caveats/game section below; skip or reinterpret as "pan the view toward the newest event.")*
- **Exaggerated scale on key moments** [0:03:51] → "when the player wins a battle, show the victory number 2x normal text size for 1 second before shrinking to normal."
- **Persistence of impact (don't clean up immediately)** [0:04:23] → "leave the 'defeated' enemy sprite greyed out on screen for 2 seconds before removing it, instead of deleting it instantly."
- **Match juice to the core action** [0:04:23] → "since this game is about clicking to collect resources, put all the juice on the click (pulse + particle + sound) and keep everything else still."

## Page material
- Test your game with everything else stripped away (no points, no story, no music) — if the bare interaction isn't fun, juice won't fix it, but it also can't hurt it.
- Give the player instant proof their input landed: a sound, a flash, a shake, a particle — the moment right after a click is where juice matters most.
- Stack 2-3 small feedback effects on the same hit rather than one big effect (shake + flash + sound beats shake alone).
- Match the juice to what the game is actually about — don't add camera shake to a calm puzzle game just because it's on this list.
- Randomize repeated sounds/animations slightly so the same action doesn't feel robotic on the 50th click.
- Don't delete evidence of what just happened immediately — a brief pause or a lingering visual (a greyed-out defeated enemy, a damage number that lingers) helps the player register it.

**Exercises:**
1. Screenshot your app's main action button (Attack, Roll, Collect, whatever it is). What happens on screen in the 200ms after you click it? If the answer is "nothing, then the new state just appears," pick one juice trick from the list above and add it.
2. Pick one number that changes in your game (HP, coins, score). Make it pop briefly (bigger text, a color flash, a short animation) instead of just updating instantly, and time how long the flash lasts.

**Quiz:**
1. Q: What's the difference between "game feel" and "juice" as this video uses the terms? A: Game feel is the core, hard-to-fake sensation of the base interaction itself (like Mario's jump); juice is small add-on polish effects (shake, flash, particles) layered on top of an already-working interaction.
2. Q: According to the video's closing rule, should a jumping game and a shooting game use the same juice? A: No — juice should double down on the game's core action (shooting: camera shake + fast fire rate; jumping: stillness + character weight), not be applied identically everywhere.

**For a browser game built with Gemini on Apps Script (turn-based/idle, no real-time):** almost everything in the juice list is pure frontend (CSS transitions/animations, a `<canvas>`, `requestAnimationFrame`, or even just adding/removing CSS classes with `setTimeout`), so it works fine even though the game logic itself is turn-based — the trick is to fire the juice the moment the player's own click happens, and again the moment `google.script.run`'s callback returns the result, not in response to another player's live action. Screen shake, hit-pause, flashes, particles, sound, knockback, exaggerated numbers and lingering "defeated" states from the list above all apply directly to a click -> server call -> result flow. What doesn't fit: Luftrausers/Hotline Miami's *reactive real-time camera* (there's no continuously-moving viewport to reframe in a static page layout) and Mario 64's underlying *momentum/friction movement feel* (there's no continuous player-controlled movement to tune in a turn-based game) — both are safe to skip or treat as inspiration only, not literal prompts.

## Caveats
- 2016 video; several example games (Random Heroes, Super Time Force) will be unfamiliar to a 14-year-old today — the underlying tricks (shake, flash, particles, sound) are unaffected by age and transfer fine.
- The talk credited as the source of "most of these" tricks is mangled by auto-captions ("torqus... by vamir Cactus and grapefruit," [0:02:09]) — we could not verify the exact title/speakers from the transcript alone; treat the credit as real but the specific names as unreliable without checking the video's on-screen link.
- The video never gives concrete numbers (pixels, milliseconds, percentages) for any technique — every number in the "Juice techniques as Gemini prompts" section above is our own reasonable default for a kid to paste and tune, not something Brown states.
- No code, engine, or tool is shown or named (not Unity-specific, not Figma/Aseprite-specific) — this is a pure design-concepts video, which is actually a plus for reuse since nothing needs translating away from a specific tool.
- The "game feel" half (friction/momentum/weight, [0:01:06]-[0:01:38]) is presented as basically un-teachable ("not sure that's particularly useful advice," [0:02:09]) — don't oversell that part to kids as actionable; the "juice" half from [0:02:42] on is the actually actionable content.

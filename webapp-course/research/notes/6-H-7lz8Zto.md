# BattleMechs and the Power of Project Based Learning | Kory Lloyd | TEDxTwinFalls (TEDx Talks, 13.9 min)

**What it is:** single-speaker TEDx talk, mostly a personal narrative (his childhood love of Mech video games, his own "multipotentialite" career path, then his current work running a college student club, CSI MechTech, that is literally building a giant rideable robot). PBL is introduced only in the last third as the name for what he's already doing.
**Substance:** thin as a PBL explainer (PBL itself gets under 2 minutes of direct discussion, [0:07:19]-[0:09:18]), but the concrete example — a large, real, multidisciplinary, multi-year, real-audience student project — is a genuinely useful existence proof of "how big and how cross-disciplinary a project-based effort can get," even though the age group is college, not 14-15.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:10]-[0:07:19] Long personal narrative: childhood computer/Mech-game nostalgia, his own varied career (real estate, business owner, welder, cowboy, now English teacher), discovering the word "multipotentialite" (someone drawn to many different interests) — almost entirely biographical, not a classroom practice (generic/skippable for class purposes, though the "multipotentialite" framing [0:04:35] could be a nice validating idea for kids who feel scattered across interests).
- [0:07:19] PBL defined, briefly and generically: "a multidisciplinary approach between maybe a math class and an English class or an English class and a physics class," where "instructors work together to sit down with students working on a hands-based project to help them accomplish course objectives" — thinner and less specific than the Edutopia videos' definitions in this same batch; adds nothing new to the concept itself.
- [0:07:51] Names "synthesis" explicitly as a taught skill: "the creation of a new idea from the convergence of two existing ideas" — he frames his own BattleMech-plus-PBL idea as an act of synthesis, a nameable habit of mind worth pointing out to kids designing their own final projects (combine two things you already know/like into one project idea).
- [0:08:24]-[0:09:32] The actual project: a student club (CSI MechTech) built around fabricating a real, giant, rideable Mech, deliberately framed as STEAM (Science, Technology, Engineering, ARTS, and Math) rather than STEM — the addition of Arts (conceptual/art design, marketing/ad-copy writing) to the usual STEM list is worth calling out, since it validates non-coding roles (writing, design) as first-class parts of a technical project.
- [0:10:03]-[0:11:44] Team structure across departments that had "never worked together before": an engineering/design team (with an electrical-engineering advisor and a mechanical-engineering advisor), a fabrication/machining team, a business-management team, and a power-systems team, each with its own student team lead coordinating with faculty advisors as an "executive team." A genuinely detailed real example of how to split a large project into workstreams with named owners.
- [0:11:44]-[0:12:17] Explicit list of skill areas the project pulls in: welding, IT/automation/control, robotics, 3D CAD design (SolidWorks), art/conceptual design, management/marketing/ad-copy writing, and sponsorship/fundraising — a good reminder that a single ambitious project can have several genuinely different "lanes" a kid could specialize in, not just "the coder" and "everyone else."
- [0:12:52] States the project is deliberately "scalable" and "sustainable," with an explicit backlog of unbuilt future ideas the students themselves generated (jetpacks, shoulder rockets, an all-terrain track system) — a good model for "keep a running idea backlog beyond what's actually being built this cycle," distinct from scope-creeping the current build.

## What the frames add
Almost entirely narrative b-roll and nostalgia, not classroom-usable visuals. [0:00:00]-[0:04:30] is the speaker alone on stage with game-cover and product images (MechWarrior boxes, a Kickstarter page, concept art) — pure personal-story illustration, no pedagogical content. [0:08:20]-[0:09:50] shows real students in a lab: pouring/measuring liquids, working with test tubes, examining a specimen under a microscope with a laptop feed, working on an iPad, and separately a board-game-style tabletop simulation being played by students — confirms "hands-on, real lab work" is part of the broader PBL claim but isn't specific to the Mech project itself (looks like stock/generic classroom b-roll, possibly reused footage, not necessarily the same students). [0:09:00]-[0:10:00] shows actual CAD renders (a yellow-and-grey robot chassis design, a walking-mech render) and a "CSI EAGLEMECH" title card — genuine evidence the project has real engineering deliverables, not just an idea. [0:10:00]-[0:10:10] a real photo of the assembled multidisciplinary student team in front of a building — confirms scale (looks like 20-30 students).

## For our class (14-15, Gemini writes the code, Apps Script + Sheets)
### Backend concepts it shows or explains
None — no software content at all; the Mech is a physical/mechanical/electrical engineering project.

### Becomes something kids do (activity, mini-project, milestone), concrete enough to run next week
Borrow "synthesis" as a named exercise for choosing the class's big final project: have each kid or group explicitly state their idea as "X + Y" (two things they're each already into, combined), mirroring how he frames his own Mech-plus-PBL idea — a light, concrete way to generate more personal project ideas than "build an app" alone would produce. Also borrow the STEAM framing (explicitly naming an "arts" role — visual design, copy/text, presentation — as equal to the "coding" role) for grouping kids who aren't drawn to the technical side, since not every 14-year-old in a 10-15 kid class will want to be "the one talking to Gemini."

### Traps a kid will hit
Not applicable (no build content).

### Doesn't transfer, and why
The project itself (a 15-20ft, multi-ton rideable robot, college-level, multi-year, involving welding/hydraulics/electrical engineering) is wildly out of scale for a 20-meeting, 90-minute, 14-15-year-old class — useful only as an extreme upper-bound example of "how far a project-based structure can scale," not as a template to imitate directly. The first 7 minutes of biography add nothing usable for lesson design and should be skipped entirely if this video is shown; at most, show [0:07:19]-[0:12:52].

## Honest caveats
This is the weakest PBL video in the batch as a *concept explainer* — its PBL definition is thinner than any of the four Edutopia videos in this batch, and the speaker is not a PBL researcher or K-12 practitioner (he teaches college English and runs a hobbyist-adjacent engineering club). Its value is narrow: a single, vivid, real existence-proof of a large-scale, genuinely cross-disciplinary, multi-year student project with a real public audience (Kickstarter backers, Maker Faire) — worth a short clip [0:09:00]-[0:10:20], not the full 13.9 minutes. No AI-building content; nothing outdated, but nothing about school-classroom PBL specifically either — this is club/extracurricular structure, not a classroom unit design.

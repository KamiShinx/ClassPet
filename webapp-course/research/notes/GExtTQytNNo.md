# Facilitating Learning in a Student-Driven Environment: Keys to PBL Series Part 4 (Edutopia, 3.2 min)

**What it is:** deep-dive on one of the five keys from the umbrella video (hnzCGNnU_WM) in this same batch — "student driven." Same interview subjects (teachers Liza/Sheela, researcher Peggy Ertmer), same documentary style, with on-screen "TIP" callouts overlaid on the footage.
**Substance:** substantive and focused: shorter than the umbrella video but more specific about *how* a teacher stays in control of a student-driven classroom without directing it — the "scaffolding, not control" argument is the throughline.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:06] Direct framing of the anxiety a teacher will actually feel: student-driven lessons feel "scary because you don't feel like you're in control, but you really are" — the resolution given is planning: "you are scaffolding every single aspect of that lesson," which is heavy upfront work that pays off later. Directly useful reassurance for Ben as a first-time PBL teacher running an unfamiliar format (AI-assisted coding) with young teens.
- [0:00:37] A modelled teacher move: instead of giving answers, the teacher repeatedly redirects a whole-class discussion back to the students' own knowledge ("What else can we use the lake for... think about the lakes in our community") — a real, transcribable example of "ask, don't tell."
- [0:00:41] Sheela's key distinction: it's "not about just giving kids a worksheet that the teacher has created to complete," it's about "introducing the topic in a way that will build curiosity" and giving space to "enquire and ask questions" — explicit rejection of worksheet-driven PBL, worth naming since a vibe-coding class could easily collapse into "follow these steps" without noticing.
- [0:01:09]-[0:01:41] Reflection built into the structure deliberately, not left to chance: "setting aside the last three minutes," or stopping at benchmarks when a part of the project finishes, to ask "what's working well for your group?" One teacher uses a whiteboard "reflect on this learning so far" ritual mid-lesson — a concrete, repeatable practice (end-of-session reflection minutes) that fits a 90-minute meeting well.
- [0:01:41] After reflection: "engage in discussion with their peers, to gauge what was the thinking from their partner... and how does that now shape my thinking" — peer discussion as the next step after individual reflection, not just journaling alone.
- [0:02:13]-[0:02:46] Daily/weekly "learning targets," sometimes written by the teacher, sometimes by students, with students tracking their own progress against the target ("either throughout the lesson or throughout the week") — gives students ownership of knowing whether they're learning, rather than depending on the teacher to tell them. A student quote paraphrase: this makes them feel like they know they're learning because *they're* the ones tracking it, not waiting for the teacher's judgment.

## For our class (14-15, Gemini writes the code, Apps Script + Sheets)
### Backend concepts it shows or explains
None — pure pedagogy.

### Becomes something kids do (activity, mini-project, milestone), concrete enough to run next week
Adopt the "last three minutes" reflection ritual as a fixed structural element of every 90-minute meeting from week 1: stop with ~5 minutes left, gather at a shared whiteboard, and have each kid or group say one thing that worked and one thing that's still broken in their project. Also adopt written learning targets per meeting (e.g., "today: my app saves a row to the Sheet when the button is clicked") that kids track themselves — gives a lightweight, teacher-light way to know whether the *concept* landed, separate from whether Gemini successfully produced working code.

### Traps a kid will hit
Not applicable directly, but flags a real facilitation trap for Ben: with "a weak, forgetful AI" (per CONTEXT.md) doing the actual coding, the temptation to hand kids a worksheet of exact prompts to paste into Gemini is exactly the anti-pattern this video warns against ("not about just giving kids a worksheet... to complete") — the scaffolding needs to be in the *questions asked* (what should happen when someone clicks this button? what does the app need to remember?), not in pre-written prompts to copy.

### Doesn't transfer, and why
The specific example (a lake/community-uses discussion, presumably an environmental science unit) doesn't map to software; use only the facilitation moves, not the content area.

# Embedding Assessment Throughout the Project: Keys to PBL Series Part 5 (Edutopia, 2.7 min)

**What it is:** deep-dive on the fifth key from the umbrella video (hnzCGNnU_WM) in this batch — "multifaceted assessment." Same documentary style, same and additional interview subjects (Peggy, Sheela, Liza/"Lisa," plus a new voice "Marianne"), on-screen "TIP" overlays.
**Substance:** substantive: gives specific, nameable formative-assessment techniques (not just "assess throughout"), plus a clear description of a culminating public event — the most immediately actionable of the three PBL-series parts in this batch for a 20-meeting course.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:00:07] Because PBL projects are "multi-week," the teacher must "build in opportunities to assess where they are and what they're learning as you go along" rather than waiting — directly relevant given the class's 20-meeting arc and a single big final project.
- [0:00:07] Assessment starts "from the minute a teacher introduces that learning target," via "strategic observation" — assessment is framed as continuous and often informal, not just formal checkpoints.
- [0:00:39] Two concrete formative-assessment techniques named and explained: "fist to five" (a hand signal from 0/fist = "I have nothing" to 5 = "I feel really good about this learning target") and, at [0:01:10], "thumbs up/sideways/down" as a quick check for understanding. Both are free, instant, and require zero prep or tooling — genuinely usable in a 90-minute Apps Script class as a mid-session pulse check ("show me fist-to-five on whether your app can save a row yet").
- [0:00:49] Purpose stated plainly: "you really have to know what kids know and don't know" so the teacher can "support or differentiate the learning" — assessment exists to steer teaching decisions, not just to grade.
- [0:01:40] Assessment responsibility is explicitly shared: students assess themselves, and students assess their teammates — "some of the most important and useful feedback is when kids can offer it to each other." Peer feedback is named as high-value, not just a formality.
- [0:01:40]-[0:02:11] The culminating event: presenting the final product to an audience beyond the classroom — "community members... other teachers... parents," sometimes in the evening — described as "usually a big deal," and the teacher should "take advantage of that being a big deal" because students are proud to present what they've learned. Directly maps onto the idea of a public demo night for the class's big final project.

## For our class (14-15, Gemini writes the code, Apps Script + Sheets)
### Backend concepts it shows or explains
None — pure pedagogy, though note the culminating "product or performance beyond the class" idea pairs naturally with the class's own live, real backend hub concept: a public demo night could literally run through the class's own Apps Script "hub" web app rather than slides.

### Becomes something kids do (activity, mini-project, milestone), concrete enough to run next week
Adopt fist-to-five as the default mid-session check-in from week 1 ("show me fist-to-five: can your button write a row to the Sheet yet?") — costs nothing, gives Ben an instant read on the room without interrupting flow. Build a peer-feedback step into every project milestone: before a group moves on, one other kid or group tries their app and gives one specific piece of feedback (what worked, what broke) — mirrors the video's "kids offering feedback to each other" point and is cheap to run in a small 10-15 kid class. Plan one real culminating event for the year's big final project — an evening demo night with parents/other classes trying the deployed apps — matching this video's description almost exactly.

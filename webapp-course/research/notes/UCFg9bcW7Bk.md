# Teaching Methods for Inspiring the Students of the Future | Joe Ruhl | TEDxLafayette (TEDx Talks, 17.7 min)

**What it is:** single-speaker TEDx talk by a 37-year veteran 9th-grade biology teacher, mixing a concrete classroom-design idea (choice-menu units) with an inspirational/values-driven close about teacher-student relationships ("love"). No slides with data; mostly rhetorical.
**Substance:** mixed: the "menu of activities" structure [0:06:36]-[0:10:21] is a real, concrete, reusable classroom mechanism; roughly the second half of the talk [0:11:24]-[0:17:35] is motivational content about caring for students, valuable as a values statement but not a classroom practice to extract.

## Ideas, in the video's order
- [0:01:07] Names the traditional "teacher-centered classroom" (rows, no talking, teacher downloads info, students "regurgitate it back up on a test") as the default most people remember — useful as the explicit contrast case before introducing PBL-style structure.
- [0:01:42] Origin story for his method: noticing kids "love having choices" in the cafeteria line, and testing whether choice would work in the classroom too (generic personal anecdote, but grounds the whole talk in a real, testable hunch rather than theory).
- [0:02:14] Adds "choice" to the National Education Association's "four Cs" (Collaboration, Communication, Critical Thinking, Creativity) as "a characteristic of the classroom," not a fifth skill to teach — a useful reframe: choice is an environment property, not a competency.
- [0:04:25] Core structural shift stated plainly: "a shift from a teacher-centered classroom to a student-centered classroom," teacher becomes "a guide on the side rather than a sage on the stage," freeing the teacher "to coach, to mentor, to nurture and inspire."
- [0:06:36]-[0:07:41] The concrete mechanism: each 2-3 week unit is divided into a "menu of all the smorgasbord activities," and no matter which activities a student picks, or in what order, "they'll still achieve the required objectives for the unit" — the hard, unglamorous teacher work is writing enough varied activities to cover every learning style while guaranteeing the same objectives are hit through any path. Two required activities exist on every menu regardless of path: the end-of-unit test, and a self-paced "computer tutorial" (his own recorded lecture replacement) — this is the clearest evidence in the batch that "differentiated path, same required checkpoints" is workable and has been run for years, not just theorized.
- [0:08:12]-[0:09:18] Lists the actual menu options a visitor would see running simultaneously: computer tutorials, website activities, video-plus-worksheet stations, lab activities, ongoing science-fair-style long projects, an educational game corner, hands-on simulations, and "reflection sheets" (self-evaluation, connecting past knowledge to new knowledge) — a genuinely varied, concrete menu of activity *types* that could be ported wholesale into a coding class's own per-unit menu.
- [0:09:18]-[0:10:21] "Arts and Entertainment" as a required end-of-unit option: kids take what they learned and present it in a nontraditional format of their choosing (song, skit, model, poem) — his given example is two students building a chlorophyll molecule model out of gumdrops. A genuinely different, low-stakes creative-synthesis milestone distinct from a working-software demo.
- [0:10:52]-[0:11:24] Names the "teacher paradox": by stepping back from front-and-center, the teacher becomes less visible but *more* important, because it frees them to actually respond to individual students' questions and thinking in small groups — a useful reframe for Ben as he shifts from "person who writes the code" to "person who circulates while Gemini writes the code."
- [0:11:24]-[0:17:04] The talk's second half is about two teacher "loves" (passion for subject, genuine care for students) as the real driver of inspiration, illustrated with personal anecdotes (a childhood teacher reading Tom Sawyer aloud) and quotes (Plutarch, Einstein, C.S. Lewis's "agape love") — values/motivation content, not a classroom mechanism; useful as a mission-statement reminder, not something to "implement."

## For our class (14-15, Gemini writes the code, Apps Script + Sheets)
### Backend concepts it shows or explains
None — pure pedagogy.

### Becomes something kids do (activity, mini-project, milestone), concrete enough to run next week
Structure each 2-3 meeting mini-unit (matching the video's 2-3 week unit cadence, scaled to the class's 90-minute meetings) as a menu with one fixed required deliverable (their working app hits a specific backend milestone, e.g. "saves and reads back a row") reachable via different paths/scaffolds depending on the kid's comfort level, plus one open "Arts and Entertainment"-style option at the end of the bigger project phases: kids explain what their backend is doing via a nontraditional format of their choice (a diagram, a skit, a one-minute video) rather than only a technical demo — useful for kids who freeze up doing a live technical walkthrough.

### Traps a kid will hit
Not applicable (no build content) — but a real structural risk if this format is copied literally: writing "enough varied activities" so every path still hits the same required objective is exactly the heavy prep-work trap the video admits to ("it's been challenging... it's been fun; it's been a challenge") — for a first-time PBL teacher running an unfamiliar AI-coding format, that authoring overhead should be budgeted for explicitly rather than assumed to be free.

### Doesn't transfer, and why
The biology-specific examples (chlorophyll models, lab activities, science-fair projects) don't map to software; extract only the *menu mechanism* and the *creative-synthesis milestone* idea, not the subject content. The second half of the talk (teacher "love," personal reading-teacher anecdote, Einstein wax-museum joke) is inspirational framing for teachers, not a classroom practice — useful once as a mission reminder, not something to extract bullet points from for lesson design.

## Honest caveats
No data or research citations are shown for any claimed outcome (engagement, retention, etc.) — this is one veteran teacher's personal account of what worked for him over 37 years, not a study. No AI-building content (talk gives no indication of a publication year in-frame, but content and production suggest pre-2020s; nothing about the pedagogy itself is dated).
